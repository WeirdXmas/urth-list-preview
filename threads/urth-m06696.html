The thread: Readers debated whether Severian's governance views in Silk's Caldo, with voting limited to specialists, reflect the Autarch's elitism and whether benevolent dictatorship can work. Marc Aramini defended an elitist position and conceded weak examples, while Tim Walters, James Wynn and Chris countered with Popper, accountability, and shared responsibility under dictators. Brunians and John Watkins argued over Plato's Republic and the Autarch as a pimp-like judge of character, and Watkins asked a yes-or-no question that went unresolved. Son of Witz and Chris clashed over whether comparing the Torturers and the Plato-like ruling class to India's caste system was fair. Much of the thread drifted into music chat and political commentary away from the text, and it ended without agreement.
  • Marc Aramini: Severian's elitist view of voting is his own, and ordinary people need not have a say in government
  • Tim Walters: a bad dictator cannot be removed, so unaccountable rule inevitably errs; citizens should help make laws they obey
  • Chris: even under dictators people share responsibility through obedience, disobedience or overthrow; voting works as collective consensus
  • brunians: the Autarch is a shrewd judge of human nature like a pimp, and Plato's Republic should be read closely as a deliberate trap
  • John Watkins: asks brunians whether Plato's Republic is an actual political proposal, to test their reading
  • Son of Witz: the Plato comparison meant a wise elite, and birth does not determine wisdom, not endorsement of caste

Themes: Unreliable narration, Power, rulers and politics, Justice, torture and mercy

2008-11-06 Tim Walters replying to Chris #

Argues that a citizen can change a bad performer by choosing another, but cannot escape a dictator, who will err without accountability. Responds to Aramini's remark that ordinary people should not have a say in government, citing Karl Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies and the moral point that those who must obey laws should help make them.
disputes

If I don't like the trapeze artist's performance, I can go see a different trapeze artist. If I don't like my dictator, I'm out of luck.

No matter how benevolent the dictator's intentions, he will go wrong with no accountability. See Karl Popper's THE OPEN SOCIETY AND ITS ENEMIES for a full treatment of this thesis.

This is leaving aside the obvious moral point that if one has to obey the law, one should get a say in it.

2008-11-06 Chris P replying to Son of Witz #

Argues that Severian's politics in his narrative are hard to read, since he writes as the Autarch and may be sermonizing, and that Severian himself is a self-ruled body politic. Responds to Son of Witz by citing the Torturers' education in obedience and disputing the comparison of India's caste system with Plato's republic.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's narration is an Autarch-sponsored sermon; Severian is a self-ruled body politic; Caste system differs from Plato's republic

Generally speaking it's hard to interpret Severian's writing on politics because it could be one or more of several different things.

For one thing, it is difficult to forget that Severian is writing this account *as* the Autarch. He's not disinterested, and may well be trying to sermonize.

Secondly, the lesson being repeated is one that is part of the education of a Torturer. Recall the mystery of their Guild. Part of the educational process has to be to prepare the student to accept the ideal of obedience - in this case, to the Autarch - that makes it possible to do that job and live with yourself. (Gurloes is a good example of what happens when things start to come apart at the seams in that regard).

And another, more abstract point, that occurs to me is that Severian is himself a body politic. This aspect was minimized in an earlier post but I don't think it's separable from the story as a whole. Severian is a self-ruled and self-ruling man. I cannot help but think a large part of the purpose of that conversation had everything to do with this point.

Also: the comparison of India's caste system to Plato is an unfortunate one, and I think very unkind to Plato (or perhaps very optimistic about the caste system). Both have a hierarchy but the purpose is strikingly different. Not to mention the type of stratification. For Plato this occurs via differences in the individual's capabilities, and circumstances for each are adjusted according to those abilities. In the traditional caste system your place in life is determined by the circumstances of your birth, and no amount of natural ability can change them.

Not to say there's nothing objectionable about Plato's republic. But the motivations are quite different.

2008-11-06 Chris P replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that voting works as a collective matrix of societal values rather than a decisive individual act, answering Marc Aramini's claim that a minority voter's ballot is pointless. Uses a hypothetical 98-2 election versus a 51-48 split to show how overwhelming consensus pushes policy, and responds to Aramini's remarks on bankers and pimps having no say in government.
disputes

"I don&#39;t really want to start a riot, but my position on voting is that there are specialized members in every society who are qualified for some tasks but should not be involved in every one. I do not feel that your average trapeze artist or even banker has any right to have input into government - but they can certainly show you how the trapeze works or how to apply for a loan."

Horribly oversimplified, and this is the kind of oversimplification that you cannot make when you want principles for a stable government.

I would like to introduce to you Proposition 8b, in which marriage is defined as being between a non-banker man and a non-banker woman. Surely you can't imagine that bankers should have any right to have input into this governmental process, right?

"Similarly I do not want the input of an unconvicted pimp to have equal sway with my words"

Who knows, perhaps the pimp is better educated? But on a serious note: just who, exactly, *should* have equal sway with your words? Which people count for less than you, which count for more, and who gets to decide? If you let ME decide, then don't worry, I'll take care of everything for you.

The phrase "unconvicted pimp" is an unfortunate rhetorical device, because it invokes a situation in which the presumed audience knows that person X is a pimp but the government does not (given that this is what "convicting" someone is *for*). What it suggests is that it's OK, part in parcel, to make decisions about whose vote is worthless in the absence of actual knowledge by the parties making the decision. Something along the lines of "Well, I don't have any proof, but I just *know* he's a bad guy".

"and since my attitudes are in the minority any vote I make will be peeing into the ocean. Even if &quot;Every little bit helps&quot; (was that a Wolfe joke for Jonas?).

When your ideas are doomed to the minority and you don&#39;t believe the people should have the right to dictate their governance there is little point."

I'm not going to call it ignorant but it betrays a deep and mistaken presupposition. Roughly speaking, it is the notion that responsibility/power attaches only if your decision is a sufficient condition, on its own, for an event to happen. But the action of societies is collective, and the type of powers/responsibilities in play are of a different fundamental type. And, at any rate, what votes DO in our republic is not quite what most people think of them as doing.

The distribution of votes functions as a matrix that represents the overall values held by society as a whole. And this sways, at least in theory, the policy decisions of the person who is given authority, regardless of which side won. As a mental exercise consider the difference in government we would get if a Democrat, let's say, got elected by a 98% to 2% margin as compared to a 51/48 split or something like that. In the 98/2 case, the populace isn't setting specific policy decisions, but what emerges is that there is an overwhelming consensus about what that society VALUES, and this means that certain agendas based on those values would be pushed much much harder than they otherwise would.

In a sense even under the dictator you have a say, insofar as you obey (or don't obey) the law, you overthrow (or don't overthrow) the dictator who made it, etc. It's hard to be a member of a society without bearing a portion of responsibility for the way that society is. Same applies in democracies; those who don't vote are equally responsible for the state of affairs by tacitly approving whatever is done in their name.

It is nice, of course, when your part in this process includes some alternative other than violent revolution and risk of your life and limb.
