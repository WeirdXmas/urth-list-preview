The genres of PEACE

The thread: Adam Stephanides argued that each of Peace's five parts is driven by a different genre (realistic fiction, screwball comedy, horror, hardboiled detective, documentary exposé), citing episodes like the Chinese egg hunt and the forgery plot's Maltese Falcon and Big Sleep allusions, and retracted his earlier psychological-disguise reading. Michael Andre-Driussi agreed with the multi-genre point but disputed Adam's bookshop description, recalling a Bogart bookstore scene and a real bookwoman. Adam replied that the bookshop is an allusion to The Big Sleep rather than a parallel, and that the genres owe more to film noir and screwball comedy, pointing to cinematic structure and the memory office's film can. The thread ended with the two largely agreeing on the multi-genre reading while differing on details.

2000-10-31 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that each of the five parts of Peace is driven by a different genre: realistic fiction, screwball comedy, horror, hardboiled detective story, and documentary exposé. Grounds this in specific episodes such as the Chinese egg hunt, the forgery plot with allusions to The Maltese Falcon and The Big Sleep, and Weer's own comparison of himself to Bogart. It also retracts his earlier view that the novel is a psychological book in disguise.
proposes · genre-and-craft · close-textual-reading · theories: Each part of Peace follows a different genre

I don't know if this observation has been made before, but each of PEACE's five parts is "in the mode," so to speak, of a different genre. (I'm referring to the predominant action of each part, not to the short tales embedded within them.)

Part one is, I think, in the "genre" of serious realistic fiction, the "Dickens and Jane Austen and Proust and Stendahl" that Charles Turner craves. We see a scene of childhood innocence about to be shattered, as in the beginnings of many "literary" novels. (Actually, I'm less sure about this first part than about the others.)

Part two is a screwball comedy, with its eccentric, spirited heroine baiting her stuffy suitors, the "scavenger hunt" for the Chinese egg, and its farcical discovery in the barn.

Part three is a horror story, of course; even the storytelling frame is a frequent device of horror stories.

Part four is a hardbooiled detective story. We have a crime being investigated (forgery) and an alluring but treacherous woman whom the "detective" gets entangled with. The bogus treasure is out of THE MALTESE FALCON, and Gold's dusty bookshop is out of THE BIG SLEEP. Weer even compares himself to "Humphrey Bogart or Charlie Chan." (213)

And part five is a documentary, or a journalistic expose: a day in the life of a corporation.

I doubt that Weer intended this structure. But Wolfe has experimented with telling a single story through several different genres elsewhere, in the four-part story beginning with "The Dark of the June." Here he applies the technique to a man's life.

Incidentally, I take back what I said earlier about beginning to understand PEACE. I still think there was a lot of truth in what I said about Weer's psychology; but I no longer think that the book can be seen as fundamentally a straightforward psychological novel in disguise, as I was trying to read it. There's too much more going on.

--Adam

2000-11-01 Adam Stephanides #

Replies to Michael Andre-Driussi by clarifying that the bookshop in Peace's part four is an allusion to The Big Sleep rather than a real parallel, and argues the novel's genres, especially part four's detective story, owe more to film noir and screwball comedy. Grounds this in the Bogart mention, the cinematic structure of parts three and five, and the projector and film can in the memory office.
supports · genre-and-craft · source-and-allusion

Thanks. I actually got the part four-detective story connection first, and that encouraged me to go after the others.

Well, I was seeing the bookshop as just an allusion, not evidence for any real parallelism with THE BIG SLEEP (although the decrepit Blaine, with his "proud, cold eyes (184) does put me in mind of General (?) Sternwood). It's actually been a long time since I either read or saw THE BIG SLEEP.

One thing I didn't mention in my original post was that my list of genres was surprisingly cinematic. "Screwball comedy" is really a film genre, and part four owes as much to film noir, it seems to me, as to written detective stories. And parts three and five could also easily be films. Surprisingly, because there are almost no explicit references to films, although there is that unexplained projector and can of film in his memory office. (Any other references aside from the Bogart mention I quoted above?)

--Adam
