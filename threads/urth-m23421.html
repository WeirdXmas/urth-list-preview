2012-11-15 Mo Holkar / UKG replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Recommends The Devil in a Forest as an enjoyable, relatively easy Wolfe novel for a trip, noting its puzzling elements are explained in the text. Disagrees with a common right-wing parable reading, arguing it is instead about mistaken perceptions and the disappointment of leaving childhood.
supports · genre-and-craft · theories: The Devil in a Forest is about mistaken perceptions and leaving childhood

I have read it, some years ago. I found it pretty enjoyable. It is slightly 'easier reading' than most Wolfe, because a lot of the puzzling elements are explained in the text rather than being left for decades of further online debate.

A lot of people seem to read it as a right-wing parable, warning against the dangers of redistribution. Not sure about that myself; it seems to me to be more about mistaken perceptions and the disappointing realizations of leaving childhood.

best,

Mo

2012-11-15 Sergei SOLOVIEV replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Argues that The Devil in a Forest has the same flavour as Wolfe's best works, with subtle and beautiful ambiguities. Answers Antonin Scriabin's question about the book by proposing that the bandit Wat and Sir Ganelon, the king's man supervising the forest, seem to be one and the same.
answers · puzzle-solving · theories: Wat and Sir Ganelon are the same person

Dear Antonin,

I did like it, I think it has the same flavour as his best works. Very subtle, with a lot of beautiful ambiguities,
e.g. the bandit Wat and Sir Ganelon (the king's man to supervise the forest) seem to be one and the same.

Sergei
