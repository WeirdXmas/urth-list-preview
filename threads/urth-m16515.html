The thread: Readers began with a lull in list activity and a LinkedIn request, then turned to Soldier in the Mist and whether Latro is a werewolf, a question Daniel Petersen raised and Larry Miller pursued. The longer debate concerned reading order for Wolfe's books: Daniel Petersen argued order shapes first impressions, while António Pedro Marques and Gerry Quinn held that Wolfe rewards re-reading and that order matters less. Daniel Petersen and António Pedro Marques also disputed whether The Fifth Head of Cerberus suits newcomers and whether the Book of the New Sun feels like sword-and-sorcery. The thread ended without agreement, drifting into spoiler etiquette and private theories.
  • Daniel Petersen: reading order shapes first impressions and re-readings, and the Sun books feel like sword-and-sorcery despite their SF setting
  • António Pedro Marques: order matters little, The Fifth Head of Cerberus is too difficult for newcomers, and the Sun books' fantasy feel never settles
  • Gerry Quinn: Wolfe is best on re-reading, so spoilers hurt first reads most, and magic in the Sun books is unexplained technology
  • Larry Miller: proposes reading Long Sun first to trace connections, and suspects Latro is a werewolf

2011-12-13 Daniel Petersen replying to Larry Miller #

Argues that Latro's constant memory loss makes Soldier in the Mist lull the reader, and proposes that Latro is secretly a werewolf. Responds to Larry Miller's thread by describing reading in separated snatches, noting the visionary moments and the Severian companion characterisation, and admitting he is less interested in puzzle-solving.
proposes · puzzle-solving · genre-and-craft · theories: Latro is a werewolf

I'm half way through Soldier in the Mist, Larry. Glad to finally get round to this series that seems to be nearly as popular and central as the solar cycle. I shouldn't have started it yet, though, as I can only read it in fairly separated snatches. Because of its mellifluous nature (a brilliantly achieved effect due to the narrator's constant memory loss), it can lull you a bit. I suspect it should be read as quickly as possible, all in a weekend or a week or something. There have been some wonderfully magic moments, both action-y (just a few) and visionary (lots). And just when it seemed it was going to merely be an extended meander, it seems to promise you might find out more about Latro's background and identity.

I'm not good (or always especially interested) in the puzzle-solving Wolfe's books require (I prefer to explore the themes in a literary criticism sort of way - it takes all sorts though), but it looks fairly strong to me that Latro is a werewolf. (What are that race called - starts with an 'N'? Or have I already gotten hopelessly mixed up in the puzzle pieces.)

A cracking read all in, I think. (*Excellent *characterisation companion-piece to Severian. Ingenious on that level.)

-DOJP

2011-12-14 Daniel Petersen replying to Gerry Quinn #

Argues that reading Long Sun after The Book of the Short Sun spoiled the wonder of meeting the robot Maytera Marble, so reading order can make a personal reading history better or worse. Responds to Gerry Quinn's claim that Wolfe is best on re-reading, offering a first-person account of his own experience rather than a logical rebuttal.
disputes · theories: Reading order shapes the experience of later Wolfe books

If it's not already clear, I'm an experiential sort of reader. For me, the 'problem' with reading Long Sun *after* I had already read OBW was that, to use one example, I had already had the wonder-sensation of running into a uniquely drawn intelligent robot character like Maytera Marble and so that initial aesthetic pleasure and strangeness were *behind* me when I encountered her being first introduced by Wolfe in his own canonical unfolding of the story. That's just really disappointing as a reader. It gave a certain 'worn-ness' to Long Sun that *shouldn't* be there. Your intuition about multiple readings is probably right in that I will no doubt lose sight of that initial disappointment with enough subsequent readings. But it did make an important difference in the reading experience and thus it seems to me one's overall personal reading history with Wolfe *could* be better or worse, based on what order one reads his books in. There are probably better and worse pathways to the 'best' (second and beyond) readings. (And I still wonder if these very pathways will always colour the later readings.)

-DOJP
