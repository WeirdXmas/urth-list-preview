Some problems if the Pancreator/Outsider is theHoly Trinity

Urth list · 6 messages · 2002-06-10 to 2002-06-12

2002urthThe Book of the New SunRoy C. LackeyAndy RobertsonAndrew ReevesJames Wynn
The thread: The thread asked whether James Jordan's reading, that Urth is an earlier universe-cycle with no promise of a prior Noah, has any basis in an authoritative Wolfe interview or only in the text, and whether it fits the Holy Trinity reading. Andrew Reeves questioned its sourcing and cited the Apollo photo, Severian's box surviving a singularity, and the twentieth-century window as problems. Roy Lackey argued that a prior Christ figure with an earlier creation clashes with theology and with 5HC's Earth references, grounded in Severian's water-to-wine, baptism and resurrection. James Wynn offered cyclic cosmology as a possible explanation for the Christ-like details, then in his second post downplayed Wolfe's interview remark about the distant past. The thread ends with the cosmology question unresolved and the interview's weight still disputed.
  • Andrew Reeves: doubts Jordan's cycle reading has a source outside the text and cites textual problems
  • Roy C. Lackey: a prior Christ figure with an earlier creation conflicts with Trinity theology and 5HC's setting
  • James Wynn: cyclic cosmology could explain the Christ details, and Wolfe may have invented it to avoid theology
  • James Wynn: Wolfe's interview remark about the past should carry little weight given his 'toying' comments

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Deep time and ruins, Christ figures and messiahs

2002-06-10 Andrew Reeves #

Questions whether James Jordan's claim that Urth is an earlier universe-cycle, with no promise to a prior Noah, comes from an authoritative Wolfe interview or elsewhere outside the text. Points to the Apollo moon landing picture, Severian's box with the book surviving a singularity, and the 20th-century window in the botanical gardens as problems for that reading.
questions · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic

The reason I ask is because it seems odd that in a whole different cycle of creation you'd still get the Apollo moon landing whose picture is recognizable as such. And as someone else mentioned, how would Severian's box with BOTNS make it through the singularity?

That aside, what's going on with that window into the 20th c. in the botanical gardens?

Andrew

2002-06-11 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that treating Severian as a prior Christ figure, with Urth as an earlier creation, creates theological and factual problems for the Holy Trinity reading and for placing The Fifth Head of Cerberus in the Sun universe. Grounds this in Severian's water-to-wine, baptism and resurrection, the Brown Book's Earth myths, and mentions of Ireland, New York and David Copperfield in 5HC.
disputes · theology-and-religion · source-and-allusion · theories: Severian is a prior Christ figure; Urth is in Earth's far future; Fifth Head of Cerberus is not in the Sun universe

From: James Jordan

In addition to the objections raised by others, there are all those stories in the Brown Book, clearly recognizable to students of Classical mythology as founded on Earth's mythology. It also contains a direct quote from Genesis, and another from Marlowe's FAUSTUS. No. No. No. Urth is in our Earth's far future.

Facts aside, as well as theological difficulties, the notion of Severian, turning water into wine, being baptized, dying and being resurrected, a world savior, etc., in a previous iteration of creation, makes him the prototype for Jesus. Kind of cheapens the whole Holy Spectacle, doesn't it? Putting the cart before the horse.

Not to mention it plays hell with Nick Gevers' idea of placing 5HC in the SUN universe. A separate creation with an Earth, complete with Ireland, Scotland, Wales, and New York City, DAVID COPPERFIELD, and Catholic saints (all mentioned in the text), stretches credulity past all endurance.

-Roy

2002-06-11 James Wynn #

Argues that the cyclic expansion-and-collapse cosmology could explain repeated Christ-like details in The Book of the New Sun, while suspecting Wolfe invented it to avoid theological justification. Cites Wolfe's denial that Severian is Christ and his description of him as a mere literary 'Christ figure'.
proposes · theology-and-religion · author-and-biography · theories: Cyclic big bang cosmology reconciles Wolfe's theology

I seem to recall an expansion theory in which the universe eternally expands, collapses, and expands again in an unending cycle of big bangs and big gnabs. According to this theory, each universe will would be precisely (or *almost* precisely) like the ones preceding it because it started the same way. I understand this theory is now out-of-favor with recent evidence that the universe cannot ultimately collapse. But it wouldn't have been at the time of writing.

This could reconcile Wolfe's claim with the familiar references in the story.

But in my heart of hearts I believe Wolfe concocted this explanation to rescue himself from having to constantly justify theologically everything in the BOTNS. I DO recall that he denied Severian is Christ. He said he was a "Christ figure", but that's merely a literary term.

-Crush

2002-06-12 James Wynn #

Argues that Wolfe's interview remark placing the cycle in a distant past should carry little weight, since the novel is futuristic and Christian theology cannot be fit to it in every way. Grounds this in another Wolfe quote calling his ideas 'toying' and comparing Genesis to picturesque language, and in the poster's dislike of placing the book in the past.
disputes · theology-and-religion · author-and-biography · theories: The Book of the New Sun is set in the future, not a distant past; Christian theology cannot fully fit the Book of the New Sun

I really would NOT place a lot of weight on this particular statement of Wolfe's in the quoted interview. It's just too difficult to reconcile the other clues. I think there is no doubt when Wolfe was writing this FUTURISTIC novel he imagined it in the future. I think the following statement is more explanatory:
"I was toying with those ideas, I think, rather than trying to make sense of them. Is our resurrection going to be in another universal cycle? Well, yes, maybe it is. I don't know. We don't know what is really meant by the world coming to an end, and God rolling up the sky like a carpet and all that. It is all picturesque language. Figurative language to try to give a general idea to an audience that would not be capable of understanding the actuality. And I am not sure we are more capable of understanding that actuality than they were. It is like the Genesis story. I don't believe in a literal apple and I don't believe that literally biting into the fruit had this effect but if you have to explain to a bunch of primitives how men differ from animals and where men went wrong in differing from animals, this is a pretty good way to do it."
"Toying" is an important word here. Based on the Wolfe answer I'm quoting here, I'd say trying to make tBotNS fit with Christian theology in every way (or most ways) is like trying to create a perpetual motion machine or trying to rectify general relativity with quantum mechanics: It seems so logical and straightforward, but then....

-- Crush

But no, placing tBotNS in a distant past distresses me. It's too complex.
