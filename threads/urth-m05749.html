What's So Great About Ushas?

Urth list · 18 messages · 2008-07-10 to 2008-07-11

2008urthThe Book of the New SunLane HaygoodJeff Wilsonb sharpPaul B
The thread: Participants debated whether the Hierogrammates' plan to destroy Urth through Severian and Ushas can be judged morally, and whether Wolfe's world permits moral relativism. Lane Haygood and b sharp defended treating the Hierogrammates as beings beyond human moral standards, citing theodicy and Kantian time, while Paul B argued for absolute morality and that the sacrifice is not justified by utilitarian reasoning. Jeff Wilson defended Ushas's return as beneficial to life and proposed a 'moral segregation' framework separating human and nonhuman morality, which Paul B challenged as relativism in disguise. The exchange ended without agreement, with each side restating its position and some personal rancor over dismissiveness; a late retraction by Paul B about Severian's sexual partners narrowed one earlier overstatement.
  • Paul B: Hierogrammates are not God and Urth's destruction violates an absolute moral principle against murder
  • Jeff Wilson: morality is split between human and nonhuman domains, so Ushas's return can be defended as serving life
  • Lane Haygood: human moral judgments may not apply to Hierogrammates, whose relation to time differs, and Severian is a moral agent
  • b sharp: the all-powerful-God-permits-evil problem already exists in legend, so the setting needs no special science fiction moral framework

Themes: Gods, angels and the Increate, Free will and choice, Suffering and sacrifice

2008-07-10 Lane Haygood replying to b sharp #

Argues that the Hierogrammates' plan to have Severian bring the New Sun is judged too one-sidedly by utilitarian standards, and that the act should be assessed on its own moral terms. Supports this with an analogy to Noah's Flood and the 'God has his reasons' theodicy, suggesting the Hierogrammates are imperfect beings who may accept evil for a greater good. Responds to Paul B's objection about a God who permits atrocities, which the post treats as a philosophical point to be developed.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Hierogrammates accept evil to achieve greater good; Severian's destruction of Urth is morally justified by eschatology

Now we've strayed into my territory (philosophy!).

I think that the analysis given of the Hierogrammates is too one- sided, ethically. We're assuming that morality works on a broadly utilitarian basis: e.g., it is somehow better to save the population of Urth than let it pass away.

Rather, if we look at the moral actions internally, we ask whether it is morally right for Severian to bring the New Sun, not on the basis of what effects it might have, but whether that act was right in and of itself. Analogizing to Biblical morality, look at the use of Noah's Flood to wash away what passed for a degenerate Earth/Urth. I'm no Catholic, so I don't know if there is a standard dogmatic theodicy that is accepted in response to the problem of evil, but I think that the majority-Christian view on that argument is the "God has his reasons" theodicy. In other words, it is unquestionable and axiomatic that God does only good things. So sending the Flood (make of that what you will) is a good act. Even if we cannot understand how it is good, if God has his reasons for it, they may be beyond what we can understand, being finite mortals and all

Then again, the Hieros don't suffer from God's disability in this case, as it were. They're not perfect beings, so they don't have to always act perfectly good. They may see it necessary to do evil in order to do some greater good, so they may accept that Severian must destroy Urth to get to Ushas. After all, this is eschatology and genesis we're talking about here, death and rebirth. And perhaps we're guilty of imposing a mortal, linear-time-bound morality upon beings that are not so bound. Maybe we view death and destruction as "bad" in and of themselves, when really they're just a necessary step along the way to birth/rebirth?

Lane

2008-07-10 b sharp #

Replies to Paul B by arguing that a legend of an all-powerful God who permits evil already exists, so the moral objection does not require a science fiction setting to make sense. Suggests Wolfe's Hierogrammates and their relation to the Increate are his attempt to reconcile divine power with suffering, while adding a scientific explanation of religion.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Hierogrammates reconcile an omnipotent God with evil

Paul B posts:

and

I think there is a large gap in the broad exposition of your argument, that being that we don't need a science fiction story to find a legend of a God who has the power to stop all war, genocide, disease and child rape but chooses not to. Extending your argument would seem to demand either that Gene Wolfe feels the Judeo-Christian God is morally bankrupt or that Gene Wolfe is an atheist (or both). I don't think either is true.

I think any intelligent, thinking Christian eventually is troubled by the classic problem of an omnicient, omnipotent God who allows evil to flourish. I suspect the Hierogrammates and their relationship with the Increate are an attempt by Gene Wolfe to reconcile the contradiction (while injecting a healthy dose of science to explain religion).

-bsharp

2008-07-10 Paul B replying to b sharp #

Argues that the Hierogrammates in Wolfe's far-future setting are not God, since they were created by the posthuman Hieros, so the Problem of Evil does not apply to them. Responds to b sharp's objection that condemning the Hierogrammates would imply Wolfe rejects the Judeo-Christian God, and asks who the Increate is.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Hierogrammates are not God but created by Hieros

I don't understand the difficulty in holding a race of aliens, even an advanced race, to a moral standard.

That, or perhaps more reasonably my argument would demand that the Hierogrammates aren't God. They aren't, being created by the Hieros (posthumans). The Problem of Evil, to which you're alluding to here, doesn't apply to them in Wolfe's world so there's no problem.

I don't think the Hierogrammates have any different a relationship with God than the Hiero humans do, as both are created - one by the other, no less, according to Wolfe. Who is this "Increate" person, anyway?

Wolfe can logically both worship a God and condemn a race of aliens playing god.

Incidentally, the Problem of Evil has never had an answer besides "stop asking stupid questions", which is less than satisfactory in my mind. However, I'm not Wolfe.

Paul

2008-07-10 Paul B replying to Lane Haygood #

Disputes Lane Haygood's claim that the analysis of the Hierogrammates is too utilitarian, arguing that condemning planet-wide murder assumes only a general valuing of life. Holds that Severian understands little, so theorizing him as a conscious moral agent is unhelpful, and that the Hierogrammates' morality is only meaningful if morality is universal.
disputes · theology-and-religion

I think we're straying too far afield here.

Wha?! Saying that murder on a planetwide scale is wrong is not an inherently utilitarian statement but a very general one. It doesn't assume much about a moral framework, save one that places inherent value on life.

I think that one of the primary findings from the tetralogy is that Severian doesn't understand much of anything. It's not useful to theorize about him as a conscious, moral agent.

Like I said in the opening of this, it's only a useful exercise if you assume that morality is in some way universal. If the Hierogrammates operate on another moral level (and I see little reason to assume this - they are just advanced aliens), we can just give up and go home. However, that would sound a little like the proposed solutions to the Problem of Evil.

Paul

The difficulty is in agreeing on the units of moral judgment. A Hierogrammate may be a race unto themselves and destroy an intelligent individual whose actions they disklike the way you or I destroy itchy nerve fibers by scratching ourselves or remove warts from each other's bodies.

2008-07-10 Lane Haygood replying to Paul B #

Argues that human moral judgments about death may not apply to the Hierodules and Hierogrammates, whose relation to linear time differs from ours, so the Urthling killing could look moral to them. Grounds this in a Kantian account of time as a condition of experience and in a contemporary writer's tsunami-based theodicy, while defending Severian as a moral agent despite his lack of introspection.
disputes · theology-and-religion · genre-and-craft · theories: Hierogrammates' non-linear time makes Urthling killing moral; Severian's lack of introspection reflects objective history

I suppose I could make myself clearer. Ever a problem.

The reason why human concepts of morality might not apply to the Hieros goes back to my residual Kantianism. For humans, our experience of the world is always subject the conditions for sensibility, space and time. That is, we _need_ time to be linear and run one way, or else our way of representing the world goes to bunk. But how does that apply to Hierodules/-grammates? We know that they are not similarly bound by the linear flow of time as humans are. Thus, the human moral fear of death, and our value judgment of death as bad are because death represents the end, or cessation of life. But for beings moving the opposite direction (Barbatus, etc.) or those capable of moving in any direction through the Atrium or Madregot might have a different feeling about death. So while we regard the putative murder of the Urthlings as an immoral act, to them it might be the height of morality because it paves the way for rebirth, or that worldly death is not something they need fear.

I forget who has it, but there is a contemporary writer who has a similar response to the problem of evil. He takes up the recent tsunami as evidence that perhaps, in God's view, the death of people is not something to be lamented, if we assume a beneficial afterlife for good people. I'm not saying I buy it, mind you (in fact, I don't believe in gods of any sort) but the argument is, I think, defensible.

And as for Sev being a moral agent, well, we _all_ are moral agents, whether we're conscious of our status as such. And I don't necessarily read Severian's lack of introspection or analysis of his own actions as being an inherent blindness in his person, but from the tone he takes he seems to be attempting to give as objective a history as he can, not a memoir-ish analysis of his own life.

Lane

Okay, I think I understand what you're getting at. My own beliefs don't matter that much, but I'm certainly willing to entertain the notion that in Wolfe's books this sort of thing is possible, it certainly does fall into the purview of the Problem of Evil. Christians usually don't take this to mean that moral relativism exists, rather saying that God is a moral exception.

However, as far as the destruction of Urth goes, this doesn't have much bite simply because it's not the Increate that destroys Urth, but fleshy beings like us. You would have a hard time making the case for a theologically-based moral exception for aliens the same way that one is made for God.

If anything, it would seem relativism would be much more compelling with nonhumans around. Is "moral segregation" a term in use by alien psychologists?

2008-07-11 Jeff Wilson replying to Paul B #

Argues that Wolfe's answer to pain and evil is that God alone judges when suffering and death should come, so selfish infliction is evil yet still serves the Increate's plan. Explains the poster's coined term "moral segregation" by analogy to racial segregation, distinguishing it from moral relativism and answering Paul B.'s charge of relativism.
proposes · theology-and-religion · theories: Moral segregation: humans and aliens have separate equal morals; God alone decides when mortals suffer or die

There's Wolfe's answer to the problems of pain and evil right there: God has the infinite and unfailing moral judgment to decide when people should suffer or die, and it's good that he does so. (There's no question of them not suffering or dying, because mortals have to suffer and have to die by definition, it's just a question of when and where.) When someone inflicts the suffering or death for selfish reasons, it's not just pain but evil, but it can still further the plan of the Increate.

The details of how much the varied parties are each responsible for the destruction that happens and the destruction that is avoided are murky.

I coined the phrase in analogy to racial segregation, the idea that different races can't / shouldn't share completely in a common social system; "separate but equal" being the ideal. Ergo, moral segregation is to say that human and aliens can have separate but equally moral values.

It's not moral relativism in that the actors are differentiated by biology more than sociology. There is no surmountable difference in viewpoint, because there are viewpoints that humans and aliens *can't share*. I believe humans can have moral absolutes that should apply to every person, but I don't believe those absolutes will apply to every alien. The whole reason for the word is to express the concept of "profoundly different".
