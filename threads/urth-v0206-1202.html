Loyal to the Group of Seventeen

Urth list · 8 messages · 2002-06-11 to 2002-06-13

2002urthThe Book of the New SunRobert KingJerry FriedmanAndy RobertsonThomas BittermanDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: Readers debated whether the Ascians' restricted speech in The Book of the New Sun echoes Orwell's Newspeak or instead alludes to Mao quotations and Correct Thought. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes pressed the Mao reading and the Sapir-Whorf question, which bobking countered by arguing Wolfe rejects Orwell's pessimism because the Group of Seventeen can still tell its own story. Tom offered a Planet Engineering passage as support for a language-shapes-thought view, and Jerry Friedman questioned whether the Group's survival is really optimistic, suggesting a Winston Smith-style fate. The thread ended without resolution, with the Newspeak and Mao readings still standing side by side.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Ascian speech alludes to Mao quotation and Correct Thought more than Newspeak; Sapir-Whorf is too strong, not untrue
  • bobking: Newspeak echo, but Wolfe rejects Orwell's pessimism since the Group can still tell its story
  • tom: Planet Engineering passage shows Wolfe favors a language-shapes-thought view
  • Jerry Friedman: Group's survival may be a Winston Smith-style fate, so Wolfe's optimism is uncertain

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Books, libraries and writing, Myth and folklore sources

2002-06-12 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is too strong rather than untrue, since language guides thought without absolutely constraining it, and that Pinker overstates whether deep grammar is settled. Responds to Hartshorn by tracing Korzybski's General Semantics and its influence on van Vogt, Heinlein and Delany, a tangent from Wolfe.
disputes · source-and-allusion

Hartshorn writes...

I think "too strong" is probably more accurate than "untrue." Language, along with other mental structures, does pretty clearly _guide_ the way we think about things. It doesn't have the property of absolute constraint/restraing that Sapir-Whorf suggests.

One of the reasons Sapir-Whorf (whether or not named as such) has so strong a pedigree in SF is doubtless its strongest instantiation, the study of General Semantics, founded by Count Alfred Korzybski, which essentially claims that most if not all "mental" problems stem from the use of "language" that does not correspond to "reality." (I place those words in quotes because in g-s they have special technical meanings.) General semantics has (to a greater or lesser extent) influenced a large number of SF writers directly, including van Vogt, Heinlein, and Delany; it seems likely enough that it also influences others indirectly through these.

H'mmm. I think to say "we know" is, again, a bit too strong: "we have strong reason to believe," yes. Some popularizers (Pinker comes readily to mind) have a tendency to state these things as firm and established fact, when we're dealing in an area where experimentation is nearly impossible and field data is always already tainted by interaction with other cultures.

I don't know if they are "standard," but they are certainly influential and taught in most linguistics curricula. I think the whole "deep grammar" idea is still somewhat controversial, despite the claims of, once again, Pinker that the whole thing is settled.

--Blattid
