Wolfe's Lamarckism

Urth list · 3 messages · 1998-06-14 to 1998-06-15

1998urthThe Book of the New Sunadam louis stephanidesMichael Andre-Driussi
The thread: Craig Christensen's question about Lamarckism in Wolfe's work led Adam Louis Stephanides to cite an explicit statement in Castle of Days and an Urth of the New Sun passage on migrants resembling a continent's original inhabitants, which he reads as Lamarckian inheritance. M.Driussi asked whether climate-driven racial traits count as Lamarckism or as Darwinian selection, and offered the peppered moths as a selection example. Stephanides replied that the rapid skin-colour change within fifty generations fits Lamarckism better than selection alone, and contrasted it with the faster peppered moth changeover. The thread ends with the two sides still divided over the mechanism, with no resolution recorded.
  • Adam Louis Stephanides: Wolfe's texts show Lamarckian inheritance, especially the rapid skin change in Urth of the New Sun
  • m.driussi: Climate-driven traits and migration spreading random mutations look Darwinian, not Lamarckian
  • Craig Christensen: asked for textual evidence of Wolfe's Lamarckism

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Beasts and the animal in us, Colonization and the native

1998-06-14 adam louis stephanides #

Points Craig Christensen to two examples of Wolfe's Lamarckism: an explicit statement in Castle of Days, p. 225, and an Urth of the New Sun passage on migrants resembling a continent's original inhabitants. Reads the Urth passage as most easily explained by Lamarckian inheritance, quoting it and citing page 73 of the hardcover.
answers · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Urth passage explained by Lamarckian inheritance; Wolfe explicitly defends Lamarckism

A couple of examples I've recently come across:

In CASTLE OF DAYS, p. 225, he explicitly states his belief in Lamarckism.

In URTH OF THE NEW SUN there's a brief passage that seems most easily explained Lamarckially (Lamarckianally?): "On Urth, the mystes know that each continent has its own pattern for mankind, so that if people from one shift their abode to another, they will in a short time--fifty generations or so--come to resemble the original inhabitants." (73 of hardcover)

--Adam

1998-06-14 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Asks whether explaining human racial traits by climate and terrain, such as pale skin from caves or yellow skin from desert sand, counts as Lamarckism, and whether random mutation spreading by migration would be Darwinian instead. Also raises the peppered moths of industrial England as a textbook case of selection that the poster thinks fits the Darwinian side.
questions

So attempts to link "racial" characteristics to geographical details ("Caucasians are pale because they lived in caves and/or dark forests"; "Asians have a yellowish skin because of the yellow sands of the Gobi Desert"; "Tropical people are dark skinned in response to greater influx of sunlight year round") amounts to Lamarckism? Hmmm.

As opposed to something like, "The pale mutant by chance wandered off that way, into Europe by way of Central Asia, the reddish mutant went that way, into the Americas by way of Alaska, the yellowish mutant went . . . etc."? Is this form (a sort of Neo-Noah scenario) then "Darwinism"? (No, I'm not trying to open the debate on "single human/hominid genesis" versus "multiple location human/hominid genesis"!)

But I thought that the English moths of Industrial England were always cited as textbook examples of evolutionary process? That is, when the environment changed (through sooty pollution, in this case), some moths had camouflage and some moths didn't--those that did, survived, until all remaining population (or majority, at least) had the same advantage. Then when pollution was reduced, the process reversed itself.

=mantis=

1998-06-15 adam louis stephanides #

Argues that the rapid skin-colour change Wolfe describes within fifty generations is better explained by Lamarckism than by natural selection alone. Contrasts this with the peppered moth case, where stronger selection pressure from predation produced a faster changeover, answering mantis's challenge about evolutionary textbook examples.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Wolfe's fifty-generation skin change reflects Lamarckian inheritance

No, saying that the process would be complete within fifty generations seems to me most easily explained by Lamarckism. I don't believe that natural selection alone would operate that quickly in this case.

But in the case of the moths, the selection pressures were a lot stronger--those who didn't have camouflage got eaten--so the changeover was much more rapid.

--Adam
