Baptism vs. Resurrection

Urth list · 4 messages · 1999-11-05 to 1999-11-06

1999urthThe Book of the New SunOuroboros The WormJim HenleyCarlos MartinhoDaniel Fusch
The thread: The thread asked whether Severian's submersion in the Gyoll in The Book of the New Sun is better read as Christian baptism or as resurrection. Ouroboros argued resurrection, citing the nenuphars as symbols of the grave and the Balder-like crossing, and Daniel Fusch sided with him while preferring baptism as the closer parallel, pointing to Palaemon's sprinkling at guild initiation and the parody of communion. Carlos Martinho conceded his earlier baptism reading was a guess and argued both symbolisms are entangled, since Severian's public career begins after the submersion. Jim Henley agreed the two readings can coexist because immersive baptism already carries death and rebirth. The thread ended in a loose synthesis rather than a clear winner.
  • Ouroboros: Gyoll submersion is resurrection more than baptism, with nenuphars as grave symbols
  • Daniel Fusch: Baptism by John is the closer parallel, though the drowning reads more like resurrection
  • Carlos Martinho: Baptism and resurrection are both entangled in the Gyoll submersion
  • Jim Henley: Immersive baptism already contains death and rebirth, so both readings hold

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Death and resurrection

1999-11-05 Ouroboros The Worm #

Argues that the Gyoll and its nenuphars in The Book of the New Sun symbolize death and the grave, so Severian's crossing fits the resurrection of Christ better than John's baptism of Jesus. Supports the case by citing the nenuphar roots reaching into dark water, the comparison of the necropolis roses with the nenuphars, and the Balder-like crossing of the river.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Gyoll symbolizes the grave and death; Severian's crossing parallels Christ's resurrection rather than baptism

<<<<Nessus? I can be mistaken, but I think you mean
the
river Gyoll (is this the correct spelling?). This
could be a Christian paralel, anyway: not with the
resurrection of Christ -- wich, anyway, marks the end
of Jesus's "career" on Earth --, but the Baptism by
John.>>>

Oops! Thanks for the correction, Carlos.

However, the Baptism of John is a not a good match
IMO. The bottom of the Nessus represents death. The
nenuphar represents death because "under flower and
leaves are black roots...reaching down in to the dark
waters" at the bottom of the Gyoll (where the
life-giving sun's rays do not reach). Also, Severian
makes a straight-forward comparison between the
necropolis with it's roses and the Gyoll with it's
nenuphars. The Gyoll=grave.

Now, I know the Jordan river has also been a symbol of
death, but the symbol is in *crossing* the river, not
sinking to the bottom of it. Later Severian will cross
the Gyoll but that's the death of Balder not the
baptism of Jesus. And Christian baptism does represent
Jesus' death, burial, and resurrection, but that's not
the kind of baptism John did and a symbol of a symbol
of burial and resurection is top heavy.

Finally, in orthodox theology it is at his
resurrection when Jesus' *real* career began. When
Christian's are baptized at their conversion or as
infants it is representative of Jesus' resurrection.
Of course, for Catholic children there is a 13 year
period between baptism and confirmation, but now I'm
chasing rabbits because that's not how Wolfe became a
Christian.

I suppose it's possible both things symbols are being
used simultaneously, but the resurrection of Christ
seems to fit better than his baptism by John.

--Ouroboros

1999-11-05 Daniel Fusch #

Argues that Severian's drowning in the river Gyoll reads more like a resurrection than a baptism, backing Ouroboros's point that the Baptism by John is the closer Christian parallel. Supports the claim by citing Palaemon sprinkling water on Severian's brow at guild initiation and the depraved parody of communion in The Claw of the Conciliator.
supports · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Gyoll drowning is resurrection rather than baptism

Also consider that Severian's "baptism" occurs during the ceremony in which he enters the guild. Master Palaemon sprinkles water upon his brow, if I remember aright. Later, in "The Claw of the Conciliator," Severian also partakes of a depraved parody of communion--so the sacraments appear in ritual.

I agree with Ouroboros. The drowning scene seems more like a resurrection than a baptism.

--Daniel

1999-11-06 Carlos Martinho #

Concedes that the earlier baptism reading was a guess and now argues that baptism and resurrection symbolism are both entangled in Severian's submersion in the Gyoll, since his public career begins after it as Jesus's does. Offers a quoted passage from Clark Ashton Smith's "Nostalgia of the Unknown" as an added echo, without further argument.
supports · allegory-and-typology · theories: Gyoll submersion combines baptism and resurrection symbolism

Thanks for the impressive amount of info & insight! I
read the books almost an year ago, and I didn't
remmembered all the death-sybbols at the Gyoll
submersion -- I was mostly guessing when I wrote about
the baptism. Anyway, now I really believe that both
symbols (resurrection and baptism) are entangled in
the Gyoll episode. After all, Severian's "public
career" begins after the submersion, just as
Jesus's...

of translucent azure marble, mocking the
windless waters of lakes unfathomably calm"

1999-11-06 Jim Henley #

Argues that immersive baptism already carries the idea of death and rebirth, so the Gyoll submersion can be read as both baptism and resurrection. Responds to Carlos Martinho, who linked Severian's submersion in the Gyoll to Jesus's public career beginning after baptism, and supports his point with a loose scriptural echo.
supports · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Gyoll submersion is baptism and resurrection

Doesn't "immersive" baptism always represent death and rebirth? The old self drowns and the new, sanctified self rises. Except ye be born again ye shall not enter . . . and all that.

Best,

Jim
