There Are Doors

Urth list · 12 messages · 2009-05-20 to 2009-05-22

2009urthThe Book of the New SunJordon FlatoDavid StockhoffJames WynnJeff WilsonJames Jordan
The thread: Readers debated why There Are Doors has parallel worlds that resemble one another, with James Wynn arguing that Green's guitar-string resonance between adjacent universes is the book's systemic justification. Jeff Wilson disputed that the resonance theory is adequate, pointing to the travel between worlds and to other multiverse novels. James Wynn replied that There Are Doors predates them and that Green's own explanation seems most likely, while James B. Jordan read the church and priests allegorically and proposed a Petrarch and Laura reading without tying it to the characters. The thread ended without resolution, with Wynn conceding Wilson's point only in part and the discussion drifting to a moopsball question.
  • James Wynn: parallel worlds influence one another through non-rational resonance, as with the guitar-string and black-wedding examples
  • Jeff Wilson: resonance theory does not hold, since the novel shows frequent travel and long-term crossings between worlds
  • James B. Jordan: Green is not intelligent, so his explanation may be wrong; church as Mama, priests as Boys
  • David Stockhoff: calls There Are Doors one of Wolfe's more haunting novels

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Stories within stories, Catholic faith and sacraments

2009-05-21 James Wynn replying to Jeff Wilson #

Disputes Jeff Wilson's claim that spreading artifacts and genes is rational, saying the book's explanation is instead a guitar-string resonance between adjacent universes. Illustrates with the example of wedding black, which has no reason in one world but a rational one in the other.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Universes resonate like tuned guitar strings

That's not the explanation given. Green compares the universes to two adjacent guitar strings tuned to the same note. When you pluck one, the other resonates. By the same token, men in our world wear black at their weddings. There's no "reason" for it. They wear black here, because they wear black there. They wear black there for a rational reason: The man is about to die.

J.

2009-05-21 Jeff Wilson replying to James Wynn #

Argues that the resonance explanation the quoted post gives for parallel worlds in There Are Doors need not hold, since the novel shows frequent physical travel between worlds and the resonance idea may be just one of Green's notions. Notes three other works with similar justifications, including Watt-Evans' Out of This World, Stasheff's Greymarye series and Ford's Fugue State.
disputes · genre-and-craft

But there is *also* a vector of frequent physical travel that the author tales pains to display, so that resonance explanation need not be valid. It may just be another of Green's notions.

As for it being the only story with such a justification, I can think of at least three others: Lawrence Watt-Evans' _Out of This World_ and Christopher Stasheff's Greymarye series, where the present days in worlds of different histories are similar because the mental powers that bridge between the worlds only works between worlds where someone is thinking with a similar vocabulary along similar lines at either end. John M. Ford's _Fugue State_ and his metafictional work in GURPS TIME TRAVEL establishes that of all possible worlds, only the most similar are reachable with finite resources, and "similar" comes in various degrees.

2009-05-21 Jeff Wilson replying to James Wynn #

Argues that There Are Doors shows more long-term crossing between worlds than Wolfe's novel is usually credited with, citing shops stocked with goods from Green's world, an asylum housing displaced people, and a transplanted playwright. Disputes the adequacy of Green's resonance theory and says the book should show many worlds influencing one another, while also replying to James Wynn's point that such travel is rare and not lasting.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Green's resonance theory is incomplete; Many worlds influence one another in There Are Doors

In fact, I first read _Fugue State_ and _The Death of Doctor Island_ the same day because they were published as an Ace Double. :)

I doubt JMF took more from TAD than from Moorcock's Eternal Champion or a dozen other sources of heroic striving across the boundaries of continuity, intentional or otherwise.

I think it may be exactly that! The Chinese man's shop in the Goddess' world has shelves of chattels from Green's world, and there's the intricate arrangement of utility wires strung through both neighborhoods, making dozens of possible doorways for people to weave through. Also, the asylum seems to house people over the long term, and enough of them are from Green's world that the handy man is so very tired of explaining why they can't have a color TV. And wozzname, the Nixonian guy who does the subversive plays, he seems to have been transplanted for good.

I think the resonance theory as Green conceives it is incomplete. There should be evidence of many worlds' influence on his, as well as ours on theirs, as there should have been at the very least several pagan powers, even if they have been subsumed by Laura or the Elohim by now.

2009-05-21 James Jordan replying to James Wynn #

Argues that Green in There Are Doors is not intelligent, so his explanation of the parallel universes may be wrong, and reads the church as Mama and the priests as Boys seeking to redeem that world. Responds to James Wynn's quoted claim that the guitar-string and black-wedding analogies give the real reason for the parallel worlds, offering a caveat and tying the Italian restaurant to the sacramental church as the main door.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: The Italian restaurant is the sacramental church and main door; Mama and the Boys represent the church redeeming the parallel world; Green is too unintelligent to be trusted about the universes

Well, but remember that Green is not too bright. He's one of Wolfe's successful, I think, usages of a rather low IQ character who is still good and good-hearted. Remember how SilkHorn has some very odd medical views, which we as readers know to be wrong.

I'm not really disputing you. I'm just tossing in a caveat. What Green thinks might not actually be so.

What is clear is that Mama (the church) and her Boys (priests) want to save (redeem) this parallel world. It's an Italian Restaurant (the sacramental church) that is the main Door.

Some may not know it, but this is Wolfe's favorite novel.

Proposes that There Are Doors draws on Petrarch, particularly the figure of Laura, and reproduces a long Wikipedia-derived account of Laura de Noves, Petrarch's Canzoniere and the vague, idealized beloved. Offers no explicit argument tying this material to Wolfe's characters, leaving the connection implied rather than demonstrated.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: There Are Doors draws on Petrarch's Laura

Another question is how much Petrarch there is here. Laura, you know.

From Wikiland:

<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/File:Francesco_Petrarca01.jpg> []

<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/File:Francesco_Petrarca01.jpg> []

Laura de Noves

On
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/April_6>April
6, <http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/1327>1327,
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Good_Friday>Good
Friday, after giving up his vocation as a priest,
the sight of a woman called "Laura" in the church
of Sainte-Claire
d'<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Avignon>Avignon
awoke in him a lasting passion, celebrated in the
Rime sparse ("Scattered rhymes"). Later,
Renaissance poets who copied Petrarch's style
named this collection of 366 poems
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Il_Canzoniere>Il
Canzoniere ("Song Book"). Laura may have been
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Laura_de_Noves>Laura de Noves, the wife of Count
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//w/index.php?title=Hugues_de_Sade&action=edit&redlink=1>Hugues de Sade (ancestor of the
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Marquis_de_Sade>Marquis de Sade). There is little definite information in
Petrarch's work concerning Laura, except that she
is lovely to look at, fair-haired, with a modest,
dignified bearing. Laura and Petrarch had little
or no personal contact. According to his
"Secretum", she refused him for the very proper
reason that she was already married to another
man. He channeled his feelings into love poems
that were exclamatory rather than persuasive, and
wrote prose that showed his contempt for men who
pursue women. Upon her death in 1348, the poet
finds that his
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Grief>grief
is as difficult to live with as was his former
despair. Later in his "Letter to Posterity",
Petrarch wrote: "In my younger days I struggled
constantly with an overwhelming but pure love
affair - my only one, and I would have struggled
with it longer had not premature death, bitter
but salutary for me, extinguished the cooling
flames. I certainly wish I could say that I have
always been entirely free from desires of the
flesh, but I would be lying if I did".

While it is possible she was an idealized or
pseudonymous character - particularly since the
name "Laura" has a
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Linguistics>linguistic connection to the poetic "laurels" Petrarch
coveted - Petrarch himself always denied it. His
frequent use of l'aura, as in "Erano i capei
d'oro a l'aura sparsi" is also remarkable: for
example, the line may both mean "her hair was all
over Laura's body", and "the wind ("l'aura") blew
through her hair". There is psychological realism
in the description of Laura and Petrarch's love
is nothing conventional - unlike some clich?
women of
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Troubadour>troubadours and
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Courtly_love>courtly love. Her presence causes him unspeakable joy,
but his unrequited love creates unendurable
desires, inner conflicts between the ardent lover
and the
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Christian_mysticism>mystic Christian, making it impossible to reconcile the
two, his quest for love a hopeless, endless agony.

Laura is unreachable - the few physical
descriptions are vague, almost unpalpable as the
love pines for, and such is perhaps the power of
his verse, which lives off the melodies it evokes
against the fading, diaphane image that is no
more consistent than a ghost.
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Francesco_De_Sanctis>Francesco De Sanctis remarks much the same thing in his
Storia della lera italiana, and contemporary
critics agree on the powerful music of his verse:
Gianfranco Contini, in a famous essay on
Petrarch's language ("Preliminari sulla lingua
del Petrarca". Petrarca, Canzoniere. Turin,
Einaudi, 1964) has spoken of linguistic
indetermination - Petrarch never rises above the
"bel pi?" (her lovely foot): Laura is too holy to
be painted, it is an awe-inspiring goddess.
Sensuality and passion are rather suggested by
the rhythm and music that shape the vague contours of the lady.

but salutary for me, extinguished the cooling 
flames. I certainly wish I could say that I have 
always been entirely free from desires of the 
flesh, but I would be lying if I did".

While it is possible she was an idealized or 
pseudonymous character - particularly since the 
name "Laura" has a 
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Linguistics>linguistic 
connection to the poetic "laurels" Petrarch 
coveted - Petrarch himself always denied it. His 
frequent use of l'aura, as in "Erano i capei 
d'oro a l'aura sparsi" is also remarkable: for 
example, the line may both mean "her hair was all 
over Laura's body", and "the wind ("l'aura") blew 
through her hair". There is psychological realism 
in the description of Laura and Petrarch's love 
is nothing conventional - unlike some clich? 
women of 
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Troubadour>troubadours 
and 
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Courtly_love>courtly 
love. Her presence causes him unspeakable joy, 
but his unrequited love creates unendurable 
desires, inner conflicts between the ardent lover 
and the 
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Christian_mysticism>mystic 
Christian, making it impossible to reconcile the 
two, his quest for love a hopeless, endless agony.

Laura is unreachable - the few physical 
descriptions are vague, almost unpalpable as the 
love pines for, and such is perhaps the power of 
his verse, which lives off the melodies it evokes 
against the fading, diaphane image that is no 
more consistent than a ghost. 
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki//wiki/Francesco_De_Sanctis>Francesco 
De Sanctis remarks much the same thing in his 
Storia della lera italiana, and contemporary 
critics agree on the powerful music of his verse: 
Gianfranco Contini, in a famous essay on 
Petrarch's language ("Preliminari sulla lingua 
del Petrarca". Petrarca, Canzoniere. Turin, 
Einaudi, 1964) has spoken of linguistic 
indetermination - Petrarch never rises above the 
"bel pi?" (her lovely foot): Laura is too holy to 
be painted, it is an awe-inspiring goddess. 
Sensuality and passion are rather suggested by 
the rhythm and music that shape the vague contours of the lady.

2009-05-22 James Wynn replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that Jeff Wilson's complaint about the resonance theory in There Are Doors is fair only in part, since Green visits just one world and so can only see influences from Laura's world. Defends Wolfe by noting that no other worlds need be alluded to, and compares Green's theorizing to the non-expert explanation given by V.R.T. in The Fifth Head of Cerberus.

Maybe there are. Green only goes to one, so he is only able to recognize influences of Laura's world on his.

The obvious response to this is, "Then shouldn't Wolfe have alluded to those other worlds?" Maybe, but not necessarily.
