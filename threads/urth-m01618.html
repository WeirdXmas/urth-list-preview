Hera and Sphinx / achilles

Urth list · 13 messages · 2006-04-01 to 2006-04-09

2006urthThe Book of the Long SunMarc AraminiStanisław BocianChris PJeff WilsonDavid Duffy
The thread: Opening from a question about Hera, the sphinx and Achilles' ghost in Soldier of Arete, the thread narrowed to whether Latro is divine. aramini1 argued yes, citing his being called Pleistorus and Basias's lack of divine marking, and read Latro's deliberate loss in the Olympic wrestling as a lesson in restraint for Ares. Chris and Jeff Wilson countered that wrestling is sport, that Hera's claim proves little, and that the lesson suits Athena better, while Jeff Wilson suggested Latro's brain injury explains the gods' interference. Stanislaus offered the myth of Titans crippling Ares as background, and the thread ended with Jeff Wilson asking which exalted being struck Ares in the temple, with no resolution.
  • aramini1: Latro is probably divine, based on the Pleistorus naming and Basias's unmarked status
  • Chris: wrestling belongs to sport, so Ares is only secondarily involved; the deception lesson is Athena's style
  • Jeff Wilson: Latro's brain injury, not divine frailty, makes him open to manipulation; wrestling teaches warfare
  • Jeff Wilson: a god learning to yield would be world-changing, since gods embody natural forces and psychology
  • Stanislaus: Titans once crippled Ares, and Greeks disliked him unlike the Romans' Mars

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Gods, angels and the Increate

2006-04-01 Marc Aramini #

Proposes that in Soldier of Arete the goddess Hera and the sphinx are linked, and that Achilles' ghost opposes the Spartans who have replaced the Achaeans, with Latro's companions Aglaus and Lykaon marking the old order. Grounds this in Latro's identification of the sphinx as Gaea, the boar hunt, Aglaus's satyr-like traits, and his effect on Latro's memory, and closes by asking which side Achilles' shade would back.
proposes · myth-and-structure · close-textual-reading · theories: Achilles' ghost opposes the Spartans; Sphinx is Gaea and Hera; Aglaus stands for the Achaean past

This is another post I made in May of 2003 that I was most impressed with reading it again. This will be the last repost - just thought this was some important stuff.

"Latro offends Hera or Gaea by fighting in her temple (in the books, Gaea and Hera are blended together and oppose the triple goddess, who is Echidna(enodia)+Moon (Selene) + Huntress) The sphinx is identified by Latro as Gaea (!) in chapter 29 of Soldier of Arete. this would make her Hera, who would be Ares mother (I am your mother, and your mother's mother).

However, I believe that there is an undercurrent here in the soldier series: the Achaens have been replaced by the Dorians (Spartans), who have taken over the old ways. If I'm not mistaken, this would make the ghost of Achilles mad, and he wants the Spartans to be vanquished. The remains of the achaens are symbolically put to rest with Lykaon in the boar hunting segment late in Soldier of Arete, where Latro picks up the shepherd Aglaus, who he then takes into Sparta with him. He keeps Aglaus around because Aglaus reminds him of things, and helps him remember. I don't know if anyone else has said anything, but Aglaus is the goat man who identifies Polos as a centaur (or bull-killer) in chapter 33. He later makes a pipe for io and begins dancing, where Latro says that he is reminded of Elata by Aglaus. Aglaus is a satyr, who chases nymphs, and the same carnal force flows through him. So, Latro is surrounded by a nymph, a satyr, a centaur, and a man named Seven Lions, and he is probably chasing the shape shifting Zalmoxis as well.

Why would a satyr, or a being who resembles Pan, remind Latro so strongly of his past? Notice that after Aglaus shows up, Latro starts to remember. The memory house of Simonides helps, but Latro claims that he bought Aglaus' services after Themistocles no longer required a guide to Sparta because of Aglaus' effect on his memory.

In the boar hunting scene at the middle of part three of Soldier of Arete, we see several things: the Achaens are old and wiped out, replaced by the Spartans, and dying out obscurely in the mountains. Aglaus and Lykaon seems to represent the older forces, while Latro represents the burgeoning triumph of Rome that looms over all Greece (he is the seed of Rome, as has been said). I need to look up which side was the Achaen side, but I'm pretty sure that Achilles is mad at the Spartans and is working from beyond the grave to insure that they are going to have some big problems in the near future, and that Latro is somehow involved in that effort to thwart them.

Then again, maybe I have it backwards. Aeneas is the survivor of Troy, whose seed goes on to found Rome. Perhaps Achilles wouldn't want that force to rise again, since he vanquished Hector and fought for it. What do you think? Which side would the shade of Achilles take? The Great King of the Medes or the Twin Kings of Sparta? Or would he want both to fail so that Rome could eventually come and take over everything?"

2006-04-02 Marc Aramini replying to aramini1 #

Argues that Latro's repeated naming as Pleistorus, a child of the gods, and the lack of divine marking for Basias point to Latro being divine, rejecting the view that Hercules's coaching settles the question. Responds to Jeff Wilson's objection that it is inappropriate for Hercules to tutor Ares, saying that objection does not weigh against Latro's possible divinity.
disputes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Latro is divine, a child of the gods

Yet this doesn't explain why Latro is explicitly called Pleistorus on two separate occasions, whereas Basias, who bested him, is never identified as a god, despite the fact that he wins the match.

Why would Hercules help a mortal, anyway? Why would Odysseus help him take out three people? Once he hears the advice of Hercules, he is able to implement it. If Hercules gave someone who wasn't born to be a warrior advice, they would still lose the match, no matter how good the advice was. You can tell someone how Mike Tyson fights, but it isn't easy to hear someone else and to just go out there and do it.

Your first argument, about the conventions of naming someone as a child of the gods, is cogent. I don't feel that this one, about it being inappropriate for Hercules to tutor a brain damaged Ares, effectively weighs one way or the other about the possibility of Latro being, in some way, divine.

2006-04-02 Chris P replying to aramini1 #

Argues that wrestling belongs to sport rather than war, so its skills fall only secondarily under Ares, which may explain why Hercules would teach Latro that art. Responds to a quoted objection about Latro being called Pleistorus and Basias never being named a god, noting that Hera's claim about lineage proves little since she is everyone's mother.
disputes · myth-and-structure · close-textual-reading · theories: Wrestling is secondary to Ares' domain

Another possibility is the fact that wrestling is sport, not war, and thus perhaps only secondarily in the realm of Ares - the techniques necessary to win a wrestling match are not entirely the same as the ones you would need to kill or incapacitate an opponent in battle.

As far as the lineage comments go, I don't see how Hera's statements establish anything. She is, in a sense, everyone's mother - and thus, as she says, everyone's mother's mother as well.

2006-04-03 Marc Aramini replying to aramini1 #

Argues that Latro's deliberate loss in the Olympic contest matters for his development from bloodthirsty mercenary to a fighter who knows when to hold back, and possibly as a lesson for the war god. Supports this with the Spartans' inability to grasp restraint, Latro's suicidal disgust, and the Pindaric-style line he quotes on entering the games.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Latro's deliberate loss marks his moral development; Ares must learn restraint through Latro

I never understood the importance of Latro throwing the match. Maybe it does have some thematic importance in terms of Latro's development: going from a bloodthirsty mercenary to someone who knows when it is acceptable to fight well without destroying your enemy (something the spartans don't understand, and something that disgusts Latro so much that he becomes suicidal).

Or, as I said before, maybe it is an important lesson for the war god to learn: sometimes you must lose and NOT use your full power to avoid greater destruction. (which is what I believe the significance of the poem Latro quotes when entering the Olympics: "Ares quits the spear proud throng". Remember that Latro wins the boxing and Pankration events, even though they occur off screen, because they expect him to win the chariot races, too.)

Marc

2006-04-03 Jeff Wilson replying to aramini1 #

Argues that the gods in Latro's books embody natural forces and human psychology, so a god learning a lesson like losing gracefully would be a world-changing event rather than a fitting character development. Responds to aramini1's suggestion that Latro's deliberately thrown match in the Greek contest teaches the war god Ares a lesson about knowing when to yield.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Gods cannot learn lessons without changing human nature

I'm really not sure it's in keeping with the character of the gods for them to learn such life lessons. Since they embody natural forces and human psychology, a god leearning a new behavior would literally be a world-changing event, or signal a change in human nature.
