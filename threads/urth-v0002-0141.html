The thread: Readers debated whether Kyle murdered Skip in "Hellish Hues" and what film Kyle quotes. Matthew Freestone argued that Kyle's "I think so" to Marilyn's "Can you cure him" implies Skip is still alive, and also corrected the bird as a macaw and endorsed the yolk-as-shadow-matter reading. Jim Jordan proposed that Kyle lied and arranged Skip's death aboard the mother ship, citing Farewell to the Master and The Day the Earth Stood Still as sources. RRHMAH then supplied bibliographic facts correcting the attribution of Farewell to the Master to Harry Bates, without linking them to any reading of the text. The thread ended without resolution on the murder question.
  • Matthew Freestone: Skip is still alive, since Kyle's "I think so" does not confirm a murder
  • Jim Jordan: Kyle lied and arranged Skip's death aboard the mother ship
  • RRHMAH: supplied bibliographic correction only, no reading of the story

Themes: Unreliable narration, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Stories within stories

1997-07-06 Matthew Freestone #

Disputes Nutria's claim that Kyle murdered Skip in "Hellish Hues," arguing that Kyle's reply "I think so" to Marilyn's "Can you cure him" implies Skip is still alive. Also corrects Alga that the bird is a macaw per the opening line, endorses Ron's view that the yolk is shadow matter, and asks which film Kyle quotes.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Kyle did not murder Skip; The yolk is shadow matter; Polyaris' cry evokes apocalypse

Hi,
A few more points on 'Hues...'

Nutria:
Where did you infer this from? Near the end of the story, Marilyn asks Kyle, 'Can you cure him (Skip)?'. Kyle answers, 'I think so...' Surely this implies he's still alive. Or do you think Kyle is lying? If so, how did he get away with the murder in what seems to be a pretty small ship?

I agree with this, and that it's the fundamental point of the story. In particular, Polyaris' cry of 'Danger! Fire! Flood!' made me think of the apocalypse.

Alga:
Definitely a macaw, on the first page: 'like all macaws, Polyaris doted on microgravity.'

Ron:
I thought what was happening here was that the yolk was just part of the shadow matter (a theorised form of dark matter that only interacts with our universe through gravitation, hence the fact that it can only be seen using visualisers linked to the gravity field generators) the crew picked up before they got the shadow man/demon.

One final quote:
"Marilyn asked, 'How does he look, Ky?'. 'Like hell,' Kyle muttered."

And one question:
What's the film Kyle is thinking of when he tells Skip that for as long as he is ill, 'I am the master.'?

1997-07-08 James Jordan #

Proposes that Kyle has been lying and arranged Skip's death aboard the mother ship rather than in the small ship, and asks which film Kyle is quoting. Answers Nutria's question by citing Farewell to the Master, its Gort ending, and The Day the Earth Stood Still as a source for the story.
proposes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Kyle lied and arranged Skip's death aboard the mother ship; Gort is the true master of Klaatu

If I'm right (hey, who knows?), Kyle has been doing a lot of lying. But Skip claims to have died on the mother ship, so if Kyle did it (or arranged for it -- I note that Skip does not accuse him directly), he did it there.

"The Day the Earth Stood Still," from "Farewell to the Master," by -- uh, oh, my 47 years are catching up with me. But hey, is "I am the master" from the film? I remember it as the last line of the story, but not from the film. Did Wolfe slip up? (The story is in the classic anthology of the golden age, *Adventures in Space and Time* by McNealy and Comas (?)). If you don't know, Klaatu is the ambassador to earth from the galactic civilization, and he is accompanied by a robot named Gort. Everyone thinks Gort is just a servant, but he is actually a member of the robot-warrior corps that rules (supervises, polices) the galactic civilization -- in the film, anyway. The original story has an O'Henry ending, in which Gort informs us that we have mis-read the story all along, and that he is the master of Klaatu (the humanoid ambassador). Very Wolfean.

Nutria. (Klaatu, verada niktu!)

(Listen to the thereminvox melody and chord progressions in "Day the Earth" and you'll hear them again occasionally in "Mars Attacks!")

1997-07-09 RRHMAH #

Corrects Nutria's citation of a golden-age story: notes that "Farewell to the Master" is by Harry Bates and gives the correct title and editors of the anthology Adventures in Time and Space. Supplies the bibliographic facts in reply to Nutria's uncertain attribution, without tying them to any reading of Wolfe's text.
informs

"Farewell to the Master" was by Harry Bates, I believe. And that marvelous anthology is _Adventures in Time and Space_, edited by Raymond Healy and J. Francis McComas (the latter a founding editor of F&SF).

Rich Horton
