gene wolfe and cyberpunk

Urth list · 7 messages · 2005-06-12 to 2005-06-19

2005urthturinDavid KirbyDanthapaguruJeff WilsonMaru Dubshinki
The thread: The thread asked whether Gene Wolfe belongs to the cyberpunk genre, prompted by a comparison with Oshii's anime. Thapaguru argued he does not, since his main works are set in the far future or mythic past and cyberpunk centres on near-future technological societies. Jeff Wilson countered that crime and drug use run through Wolfe's heroes, citing The Fifth Head of Cerberus, and Dan agreed Wolfe should not be pigeonholed as cyberpunk while noting Philip K. Dick influences. David Kirby then complained about unexplained abbreviations, and Maru Dubshinki explained them. The thread drifted from the genre question into a dispute over posting clarity without resolving the cyberpunk question.
  • thapaguru: Wolfe is not cyberpunk, since his works are far-future or mythic and cyberpunk is near-future technological society
  • Jeff Wilson: Wolfe is cyberpunk enough, given crime and drug use among heroes in The Fifth Head of Cerberus
  • Dan: Wolfe should not be pigeonholed as cyberpunk, though some stories lean that way, with Philip K. Dick influence
  • David Kirby: posters should spell out abbreviations so newcomers can follow the thread
  • Maru Dubshinki: GitS and AS are Ghost in the Shell and Adult Swim, explained with links

Themes: Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Robots, androids and artificial life, Books, libraries and writing

2005-06-12 thapaguru replying to turin #

Argues that Wolfe is not a cyberpunk writer, since cyberpunk centres on near-future or alternate societies shaped by technology while his main works are set far in the future or the mythical past. Notes that There Are Doors is more alternate universe than cyber, and that Oshii's work would resonate more with readers versed in Japanese culture, responding to Turin's comparison.
disputes · genre-and-craft

I doubt Wolfe is cyberpunk. CP mainly deals with a
near future or alternate reality where technology has
created a bizarre society or people. His main works
are either set so far in the future that the trappings
of modern society no longer exists (BoNS,LS,SS), or in
the distant and mythological past (TWK, Latro). There
Are Doors is somewhat in the present, but it's purely
a Philip K. Dick writing excercise, and it's more of
an alternate universe than cyber-related.

Oshii isn't of a Christian or American/European
background. I bet his stuff would mean much more to a
person familiar with Japanese
culture/traditions/beliefs. I believe your take on
Wolfe to be another ode to the man's aptitude at
creating a flexible framework for us to bring
experiences and thoughts to.

Thapa

2005-06-16 Dan replying to turin #

Argues that Gene Wolfe should not be pigeonholed as a cyberpunk author, though some of his stories may carry a cyberpunk bent, and that cyberpunk's roots lie in 1970s short fiction. Notes Blade Runner's influence on anime, Wolfe's reading of Philip K. Dick, and the PKD flavor in Free Live Free and There Are Doors.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe is not a cyberpunk author; Cyberpunk roots lie in 1970s short fiction

I think the art direction of David Snyder and his dark depiction of the future probably had more of an impact on anime than did Scott's ponderous hollywoodization of DADoES. Certainly GitS was influenced by Blade Runner's theme. I'm looking forward to watching Stand Alone Complex, as soon as AS will reair the first four episodes so I can see it in order.

Not sure I understand, but I recall reading in one of the interviews with Gene that he had read (and I believe liked) PKD. In fact, I'm currently reading Free Live Free and there also seems to be somewhat of a PKD flavor, but not as obvious as in There are Doors.

I agree to the degree that it's a testament to Wolfe's ability to stretch his craft into other themes and genres. But I wouldn't pigeonhole him as a cyberpunk author or see how cyberpunk could claim him as one of their own. What little I recall of a class on the advent of cyberpunk left the impression that every so often there's some type of backlash in the literary world that spawns anew, reinvents or reintroduces a genre. The instructors maintained that cyberpunk was one of these waves that actually had it's roots in SF and fantasy shorts that appeared in '70's glossies. I think that some of Wolfe's short stories may have a CP bent, such as The Other Dead Man or the HORARS of War but I'm not sure I would include them in a cyberpunk anthology unless it's titled The Tertiary Roots of Cyberpunk: The stories that influenced the well-read authors who influenced the authors that rode the crest of the Cyberpunk wave.

Dan
