Baldanders' dream

Urth list · 9 messages · 2014-08-20 to 2014-08-24

2014urthThe Book of the New SunGerry QuinnJeff WilsonLee Berman
The thread: Lee argued that Baldanders' dream and Urth's cave imagery point to a link between the Cumaean of The Book of the New Sun and Echidna of The Book of the Long Sun, via a plural, Tzadkiel-style identity drawn from myth. Gerry Quinn repeatedly disputed this, saying the two share no clear personality traits and that snakes, caves and witchcraft are too weak to identify a character. Lee answered with mythological parallels, Typhon and Pas as fallen-angel demons, and differences in speech explained by circumstance. The thread ended in impasse, with each side restating its position without persuading the other.
  • Lee: Cumaean and Echidna share a plural mythic identity, with Tzadkiel pattern and Whorl gods ladder linking Urth figures
  • Gerry Quinn: Cumaean and Echidna differ in personality, speech and conduct; shared snakes and caves are too weak to link them
  • Lee: mythological parallels, not personality matches, justify the link; speech differences reflect changed circumstances
  • Gerry Quinn: Wolfe carefully differentiated voices in Long Sun, so identifying the two is unsupported; Typhon never asks after his wife

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Doubles, twins and clones, Names and their meanings

2014-08-20 Lee Berman #

Argues that Baldanders' dream of stone teeth and chains draws on Severian's future in the Saltus mine rather than his past as torturer, and that the giant presence beneath the mine is underrated. Responds to Marc Aramini by citing Urth's cave imagery, including the Ouroboros, Jurupari and the Cumaean's cave, and linking the Cumaean to Echidna, Typhon's wife.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Baldanders' dream comes from Severian's Saltus future; Megatherian presence beneath the Saltus mine; The Cumaean is Echidna, wife of Typhon; Wolfe named Echidna in Long Sun deliberately

On first reading, the dream influence of Severian's past as a

torturer would seem to be the likely inspiration for Baldanders'

dream.

But given the futuristic nature of Severian's dream, Severian's

future in the Saltus mine seems a more likely source. "Stone

teeth" seem very allusive to stalagmites and stalactites. The

"chains" may symbolize the servitude of the man-apes and of course

Severian does quite a bit of arm dismemberment in his time there.

I think the gigantic presence beneath the mine has not been given

its due attention. Most thoughts regarding the alien megatherian

presence on Urth rightfully involve the water, given the focus on

the giant eel-like Abaia and his aquatic undine daughter-brides,

corroborated with "Great Scylla" in RttW.

But there is much cave imagery present in Urth's mythology also.

There is the Ouroboros story in which people are ingested, mistaking

its mouth for a cave. There is the mention of Jurupari, a monster

with a cave-like mouth for its victims. Fomalhaut, the "mouth of

the fish" is mentioned (by the Cumaean). Then there is the cave of

the Cumaean, herself.

This cave hearkens to the cave dwelling of the Roman Cumaean Sibyl

while the snake-like nature of The Cumaean ties her to the Pythian

Oracle at Delphi. Snakes and cave-dwelling also hearken back to the

"Mother of All Monsters", Typhon's wife, Echidna.

Though "Echidna" is not mentioned by name in BotNS, it would seem

Wolfe thought "snake" and "cave dwelling" would be enough clues to

tie the character of The Cumaean to the wife of Typhon. Since few

got the reference, Wolfe made sure the name Echidna was front and

center in Long Sun.

She shares no obvious personality characteristics with Echidna.

Argues that the Cumaean of The Book of the New Sun and Echidna of The Book of the Long Sun do not seem to be the same person, citing their differing interests and conduct. disputes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Cumaean is Echidna

Note the plural in "characters". It seems to bear out what I said; snakes and caves do not uniquely identify a character.

Frankly, it does have to be explained - it is certainly not a direct link. In any case, human sacrifice was not unique to a single god in Long Sun.

As for personality characteristics, I mean things like the Cumaean's interest in witchcraft and archeology, and Echidna's rather prudish interest in the virginity of the priesthood, and her killing of Pas to preserve her rule over the Whorl. They don't seem at all like the same person.

It would take a lot more in the way of 'clues' to convince me that two seemingly unrelated characters, who never appear in the same series of books, are one and the same.

And as so often, I find myself asking: Why? What purpose of Wolfe's would it serve to make them the same anyway? Even if you identify them, where's the moment you say "ah, that's why the Cumaean did X, in order to fulfil Project Y of Echidna's?"

- Gerry Quinn

2014-08-24 Lee Berman replying to Lee #

Argues that mythological parallels, not one-to-one personality matches, link Tzadkiel and the Cumaean, and that Typhon and Pas are linked as fallen-angel demons in The Book of the New Sun. Defends the analogy by citing shared traits like caves, snakes, marriage to Typhon and human sacrifice, and explains differences in speech as changes in circumstance. Responds to Gerry Quinn's objection that no common personality traits connect the characters.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · myth-and-structure · theories: Typhon and Pas are linked fallen-angel demons; Aliens on Urth represent fallen angels; Personality differences reflect transformation into Whorl god

You are looking for simplistic one to one correspondence. Mythology (and Wolfe

being highly influenced by mythology) do not work in such an orderly and

direct manner.

Pagan gods were exported and imported from other cultures and separated and

conflated in ways which make it impossible to identify most of them as simply

one god by name or "personality". Hence the need for multiple epithets for the

various gods, as Wolfe emphasized in Short Sun.

Given that they are both aliens who have more than one, sometimes animalistic,

shape, I find the deduction to be heuristic. If one is familiar with the Judeo-

Christian principle that some angels fell to earth to become demons and that

these demons usually have counterparts in pagan gods, (e.g. Lucifer, the "light

bringer"and Prometheus), the connection becomes more clear.

Aliens such as Tzadkiel and B, F and O represent angels of the Urth story

so one may deduce that the aliens residing on Urth represent fallen angels or demons

such as Abaia,Typhon, The Cumaean and Father Inire. I, personally, find the analogy

useful to understanding the BotNS story.

I am unclear as to why football is pertinent to this discussion. But I find cave

dwelling, marriage to Typhon, snake association, witchcraft and human sacrifice

to be a sufficient constellation of traits to link these two Wolfean literary

characters via their mythological connections.

Likewise Pas and Typhon possess strikingly different "speech patterns", "personalities"

and other traits. Are you suggesting that Pas and Typhon are unrelated characters?

Spring Wind also differed from Typhon, just as young Alexander differed from middle-aged

Alexander. We must expect personality changes when a character finds him/herself in

different circumstances.

I think the transformation from Urthly demon to electronic Whorl god is the likely

source of such differences and, as such, fully intended by Wolfe.

2014-08-24 Gerry Quinn replying to Lee #

Disputes Lee's mythological link between the Cumaean and Echidna, arguing that the Cumaean's personality does not match Echidna's and that Typhon never asks after his wife when Severian meets him. Argues that vague shared traits like cave dwelling and witchcraft are too weak to carry meaning unless the text's personalities and storyline support them.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Cumaean is modelled on Echidna

Football is as pertinent to the Cumaean as virgin acolytes and a desire to rule. That is to say, the personality characterics of the Cumaean as written resemble those of Echidna about as much as they resemble those of Lionel Messi.

Nor do I recall Typhon asking after his wife when Severian subsequently encountered him.

Should not these chains of allusion, if they are intended to be meaningful, dip at some point into the personaities or storyline encountered in the text?

It's true that in the promiscuously retconned mythologies of Ancient Greece and elsewhere, characters absorbed characteristics from each other such that probably no two characters in myth can be seen as entirely separate. That seems reason enough in itself to take the position that a relatively vague link has no significance in terms of identifying characters associated with the particular mythological characters.

- Gerry Quinn
