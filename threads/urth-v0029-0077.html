The Barnables

Urth list · 4 messages · 2000-06-24 to 2000-06-27

2000urthWilliam H. AnsleyAdam Stephanides
The thread: Adam Stephanides and William Ansley debated whether Smoky's father was named Evan in Wolfe's The Barnables, based on the 'Anonymity' section and the passage about three suburbs of the same name. Stephanides argued the father was not Evan and that relatives may have first called Smoky by his name, while questioning the family detail used to read Smoky as the positive pole against the Drinkwaters. Ansley mapped the three names onto Evan, Barnable and Smoky and argued the family would not give a father's first name to the boy. Stephanides countered with the narrator's plain wording and the fact that the father had already died; Ansley conceded the surname reading is weakened but defended it as admissible. The thread ends without full agreement, with Ansley partly yielding.
  • Adam Stephanides: Smoky's father was not named Evan; relatives may have first named Smoky, and the thin family detail weakens the Drinkwater contrast
  • William Ansley: the three suburb names map onto Evan, Barnable and Smoky, so the father was probably not Evan
  • Adam Stephanides: the narrator's plain vocabulary rules out a surname reading of 'his own' name, and the father was already dead
  • William Ansley: the surname reading is weakened but still admissible, since relatives may be confused

Themes: Names and their meanings, Fathers, mothers and children, Memory and forgetting

2000-06-24 Adam Stephanides #

Argues from the 'Anonymity' section that Smoky's father was not named Evan, since Smoky's relatives used different names, and that Smoky may have been first called Smoky by relatives. Cites the passage about three suburbs and names, and notes thin family detail, which the poster uses to question reading Smoky as the positive pole against the Drinkwaters.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Smoky's father was not named Evan; Smoky is the Drinkwaters' negative counterpart

I haven't reread the whole book in a long time, but the only evidence bearing on these questions I can find offhand is in the section dealing with Smoky's childhood: Smoky "lived a lot in three different suburbs with the same name in three different cities, and in each his relatives called him by a different name--his own, his father's, and Smoky--which last so suited his evanescence that he kept it." ("Anonymity," I, 1, p. 6 in Bantam TPB.) Which seems to mean that Smoky's father was not called Evan (since his name was "different"), and suggests that Smoky was first addressed as Smoky by his relatives and not by his father, though Smoky may still be his middle name.

Actually, they were Smoky's half-brothers and sisters.

There is more information on Smoky's family and childhood, but very little; which is kind of peculiar, come to think of it, given the amount we're told about the Drinkwaters. This would seem to argue against the view that sees Smoky as the positive pole of the book and the Drinkwaters as the negative pole. The Drinkwaters are important in themselves; Smoky is only important in relation to the Drinkwaters, poignant though his story is. (It's late at night, though, so I may be all wrong about this.)

--Adam

2000-06-26 William H. Ansley #

Argues that the three names Smoky received in three suburbs of the same name in Crowley's The Barnables map onto Evan, Barnable and Smoky rather than onto his own name, his father's name and Smoky. Supports the view that the father was probably not named Evan, reasoning that the family would not have let relatives use a father's first name for the boy.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Smoky's father's name was Barnable, not Evan

I re-read the whole book recently and rather carefully and so feel fairly confident when I state that this is the only part of the book where the origin of the nickname "Smoky" is discussed.

However, I disagree somewhat with your interpretation of the passage you quote above. I think that when Crowley says "his own, his father's, and Smoky" he means that the first set of relatives in the first city called him Evan, the second set Barnable (his father's name in the sense of "Mr. Barnable? That's my my father's name.") and the third set Smoky. It may be peculiar for the relatives of a young boy to call him by his last name, but it is not unheard of; it is much less likely (to me at least) that both Smoky and his father would allow their relatives to call Smoky Douglas (for example) if that was really his father's name. It would have caused a lot of unnecessary confusion, if nothing else.

This still makes it seems likely that Smoky's father was not named Evan, but not certain.

William Ansley

2000-06-27 Adam Stephanides #

Disputes William Ansley's reading that Smoky's father was not called Evan, arguing the narrator's plain vocabulary rules out a surname meaning for 'his own' name in The Barnables passage. Grounds the objection in the text's wording and notes that the sentence describes a time after the father has died.
disputes · close-textual-reading

I thought of this interpretation, but rejected it on the grounds that the narrator's vocabulary is (or seems) straightforward, without paraphrases of this type. My feeling is that had Crowley meant "surname," he would have said "surname." And after all, "Barnable" is as much Smoky's "own" name as "Evan" is. I do admit, though, that I don't know why Smoky's relatives would have called him by his father's first name.

Actually, the sentence we're discussing refers to a time after Smoky's father has died.

--Adam

2000-06-27 William H. Ansley #

Concedes that a reading of the phrase "father's name" as meaning surname is weakened because Smoky's father was already dead, while still defending the interpretation as admissible. Responds to Adam Stephanides's objection in a thread on The Barnables, granting that relatives may be confused.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: "father's name" means surname in The Barnables

Well, I guess that serves me right for bragging about how carefully I re-read LB. But I did read it carefully, it's just that my memory is shot.

Even though the fact that Smoky's father was dead at the time completely vitiates my argument, I still find the use of "father's name" for surname to be less of a stretch than you do and feel my interpretation is at least admissible. But you may well be right. Relatives can become confused.

William Ansley
