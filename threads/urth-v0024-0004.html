Two Wolfe Comments

Urth list · 5 messages · 1999-02-25 to 1999-02-28

1999urthC R CulverTony EllisWilliam H. AnsleyAlex David Groce
The thread: Readers debated whether T.S. Eliot's Prufrock inspired Wolfe's Peace, whether Peace implies Weer is dead, and what Madregot means. CRCulver argued for a Spanish etymology and read Prufrock as an embittered older man losing sexual power, while Tony Ellis countered with a Kabbalistic reading of Madregot and rejected the Prufrock link, citing the line about disturbing the universe. Alex David Groce reframed Prufrock as a spiritual hell in life and said Weer's story is really other people's story, offering more hope than Prufrock. William H. Ansley praised Groce's remark as the most insightful comment on the novel. The thread ended in partial agreement on Groce's reading rather than resolving the etymology or the Prufrock question.
  • CRCulver: Madregot is Spanish and Prufrock inspired Peace, with Weer possibly dead
  • Tony Ellis: Madregot is Kabbalistic (Briah-Yesod); Prufrock does not inspire Peace
  • Alex David Groce: Prufrock's failure is spiritual hell, and Weer's story is really other people's story
  • William H. Ansley: Groce's comment is the best insight on Peace and worth rereading

Themes: Names and their meanings, Books, libraries and writing, Ghosts, dreams and visions

1999-02-25 C R Culver #

Proposes a Spanish etymology for the name Madregot, linking it to the word for dawn and to the solar motif in the Book of the New Sun. Also argues that T.S. Eliot's Prufrock inspired Peace, suggesting that both the narrator and Weer may be dead, citing the poem's old-age theme.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Madregot derives from Spanish madrugada meaning dawn; Prufrock is an influence on Peace; Weer and Prufrock may be dead

1) I don't know the origin of the word "Madregot" (the name of the Brook beyond Briah), but perhaps it is related to the Spanish word "Madrugada," which means "dawn." This seems to fit, being another example of the solar motif which is predominant in the Book of the New Sun. Maybe Mantis could clear this up.

2) We've discussed before the influence of T.S. Eliot on Wolfe, particularly from "The Waste Lands." However, the poem "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock," also by Eliot seems to be an inspiration for _Peace_. The narrator of "Prufrock" is an elderly gentleman who is looking back on his life, and the message is that old age is basically a living death. However, I've always favoured the notion that maybe Prufrock is actually dead, as Weer is. For those interested, the J. Alfred Prufrock Company, www.prufrock.org, has the poem on their website, where it can be viewed for free (under "Poem"). However, the rest of the site is retail tripe.

Cheers,

1999-02-25 Tony Ellis #

Argues that Madregot is a Kabbalistic term meaning "the stages" or "the steps" that ties to the Briah-Yesod scheme, and disputes Culver's claim that Eliot's "Prufrock" inspired Peace. Rejects the reading that Prufrock's message is old age as living death, citing the line "Do I dare disturb the universe?" and contrasting Prufrock's hesitation with Weer's restless life.
disputes · theology-and-religion · source-and-allusion · theories: Madregot derives from Kabbalistic stages or steps; Prufrock is not an influence on Peace

Madregot is a kabbalistic term which can be translated either as "the stages" or "the steps". I think. Ties in with the whole Briah-Yesod kabbalah thing, clearly.

I couldn't agree that the message of "Prufrock" is "old age is a living death". If it's summed up anywhere, it's in the lines "Do I dare\Disturb the universe?" It's about a life wasted in hesitation and self-doubt, and for that reason I don't really see it as an inspiration for Peace: Weer never seems to leave the small town where he was born, but surely he's more dynamic than poor old spineless Prufrock?

My English teacher and most of the literary criticsm about the poem think that "Prufrock" is about the an "embittered and horny old man" (I can provide the source for this tomorrow once I get to the library) who cannot accept his lack of power when it comes to seducing women. 1999-02-28 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Prufrock's failure is a spiritual vision of hell in life rather than a sexual one, tying the poem's epigraph and its line toward The Waste Land and Four Quartets. Compares this with Peace, suggesting Weer's story is really other people's story and offers more hope for his soul than Prufrock does, responding to a post that read Prufrock as an embittered, horny old man.
disputes · theology-and-religion · source-and-allusion · theories: Prufrock is a vision of hell in life; Peace offers hope for Weer's soul

Well, I don't really think that Prufrock is about anything so Freudian as inability to procreate or seduce--Prufrock's failure to declare his love for the "That is not what I meant at all" woman is merely symptomatic. I think the epigraph from the Inferno is telling--this is a vision of Hell in life. Prufrock is incapable of "disturbing the universe," of offering love, in general of facing the transcendent--"I have seen the moment of my greatness flicker,/ And I have seen the eternal Footman hold my coat, and snicker,/And in short I was afraid."
The poem is in the line that develops into "The Waste Land," a far more complete vision of Hell in life, but with a hint of possible redemption, to the explicitly religious vision of "Four Quartets."
As to relevance to PEACE, there's probably some in that Wolfe and Eliot would have somewhat similar views of the theme of a man exploring his life, but beyond that I'm not convinced. It seems to me that PEACE is not concerned precisely with Weer's inability to act or love, but with something more difficult to grasp--something about the way the story of his life is really other people's story. I also would say that PEACE offers more hope for the repose of the soul of Weer than "Prufrock" does for poor Prufrock.
