The irrigation of Lune

Urth list · 23 messages · 2002-03-01 to 2002-03-11

2002urthThe Book of the New SunMatthew MalthouseSteve StricklandJerry FriedmanAndy RobertsonTimothy ReillyJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-OakesRoy C. LackeyMichael Straight
The thread: Readers debated whether the 'irrigation of Lune' and the sun's wounding in Wolfe's New Sun books were literal events and who caused them. Roy C. Lackey and Michael Straight argued over whether Typhon's astronomers or Abaia's group were responsible, and whether the Increate's agents acted as unwitting instruments or morally guilty ones; Timothy Reilly backed Typhon's lack of motive. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and matthew.malthouse split over whether the wounding was retribution or a goad toward redemption, and Lackey and malthouse ended in disagreement over whether redemption and retribution differ. Jeff Wilson and malthouse separately explored Lune's metal supply and irrigation, and Steve Strickland and Jerry Friedman debated whether the lunar forests were literal or hints of the sun's decline. The thread ended without agreement on any point.
  • Roy C. Lackey: the Increate's agents wounded the sun as retribution and Typhon lacked motive
  • Michael Straight: Abaia and Erebus caused the wound and are morally responsible, with the Increate turning their act to good
  • Timothy Reilly: Typhon's advisers only underestimated the sun's decline and Typhon lacked motive to wound it
  • matthew.malthouse: the wounding was a goad toward redemption, though redemption does not fit Lune's suffering
  • Jeff Wilson: Lune's irrigation could be literal and Whorl's return may be a planned Increate warp
  • Jerry Friedman: lunar forests may be only Severian's belief, since moonlight is not green

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Gods, angels and the Increate, Suffering and sacrifice

Responds to Andy Robertson's objection about atmospheric gas by defending the idea that the hierodules and Yesod's machinery could alter gravity and travel through space and time, citing Planet of the Apes and the Palace's zero-gravity area.
supports · chronology-and-plot-logic · theology-and-religion · theories: Lune was built as a forested moon-world; Yesod is an artificial planet; Hierodules can alter gravity and time

So it makes it pointless to build the moon to the size of Mars when you already have a Mars-sized body, namely Mars.

Rudesind and others of his society can't be expected to be cognizant of what else besides mere irrigation would be required to cover the moon with forest. After whatever other modifications it would take to keep an atmosphere in place, yes it would have to be irrigated. And while it does not sound like something of ineffable, recall that Father Inire's Gardens allow a confluence of Gyoll and the Lake of Birds; for all we know he diverted a tributary of the Brook Madregot to water Green/Lune.

In addition, this sort of sweeping up and piling of asteroids would relieve the metal scarcity problem supposedly caused by all the ships and ordnance leaving Urth, never or seldom to return.

The Yesod planet most certainly is; it's either an artificial planet or one so heavily modified it might as well be. It also seems to have normal gravity despite its interior being machinery with access spaces, rather than solid rock.

I don't follow you. They seem to be involved in numerous other unspiritual undertakings, like trafficking with Baldanders.

perhaps, he may have had help from Dark Lords / Great Beasts / whatever.

So which is it? Small peas, or beyond the scope of anything I could list? The alteration of gravity could be easy for the hierodules, and there's already an example of an area of the Palace that has zero gravity permanently or at least at will, and almost every appearance of hierodules and the other ilk of Yesod features some kind of bending of the rules of time and space.

Have you not read _Planet Of The Apes_ or seen the original film? The Whorl doesn't need any special qualities to travel through a warp in space and time any more than anyone visiting the Botanical Gardens do. If the servants of the Increate put it there, it's there for the Whorl to go through, with no more trouble than the old man's raft had reaching the location just right for Severian to fall in and find Dorcas.

My position is this.

In TBOTNS there are three levels of technology:

1) the "medaeval" or blacksmith level, to which most of the world has been
relegated by resource poverty:

2) the "terran" level, which humanity still has mastery of, but only the

2002-03-04 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that the black hole in the sun was caused by agents of the Increate as retribution rather than by Typhon or humanity's arrogance, and that the fifth volume supports this. Rejects the idea that Typhon had the ability or motive to wound the sun, noting the decline left him besieged and his cronies fled.
supports · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Agents of the Increate caused the black hole as retribution

I agree. Not only is there no apparent motivation; I doubt that Typhon had the ability. Technology on Urth in Typhon's day was considerably more advanced than in Severian's, but was but a shadow of what mean-old, nasty-old Man possessed in its heyday of galaxy hopping and creating new life forms. The sun's decline led to crop failures, riots, and revolt. Things were so bad that Typhon's cronies left the planet, taking all available transport, and left Typhon besieged on his mountain.

Who is responsible for the black hole in the sun is an important plot point, it seems to me, because it bears directly on the ultimate goal of the Urth Cycle; the pretext for, and the coming of, the New Sun. If Man, in his presumptive arrogance, caused the wounding of the sun, that is one thing. If agents of the Increate caused it, as retribution, that is something else entirely. I have always adhered to the latter view, and I think the weight of evidence from the fifth volume of the series supports that view.

-Roy

2002-03-04 Michael Straight #

Argues that Typhon's astronomers never detected the black hole's effects and only described the sun's normal life cycle, so the wounding of the sun was not their doing. Rejects Roy C. Lackey's reading of the SOTL quote and offers a third option: extraterrestrial enemies of Man, Abaia and Erebus, who swim between the stars, caused the wound.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Typhon's astronomers did not detect the black hole; Abaia and Erebus wounded the sun; Abaia's sister is the Mother on Blue

I read this to mean that Typhon's astronomers hadn't even detected the black hole's effects, that they were telling him about the sun's normal life-cycle as a star, until it was too late.

You've left out the third option, which I think is the correct one, that the wounding of the sun was caused by extraterrestrial enemies of Man, Abaia and Erebus, who swim between the stars (and whose sister, I believe, is the Mother on Blue).

-Rostrum

2002-03-04 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Typhon could not have wanted the sun wounded, since he aimed to rule a new empire centred on Urth, so Abaia and her cohorts would at most have been unwitting agents of the Increate. Supports this by citing Typhon's own words about making Urth his capital and Tzadkiel's account of the death agonies offered to the Increate.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Abaia and her cohorts were unwitting agents of the Increate; Typhon did not wound the sun

Revenge for what? Typhon didn't want to pull the Temple down; he wanted to create a new empire with Urth, the ancestral home of mankind, as its symbolic center. He would have been cutting his own throat to ally himself with any party bent on the future of ice.

"I have told you that I was autarch on many worlds. I shall be autarch again, and this time on many more. This world, the most ancient of all, I made my capital. That was an error, because I lingered too long when disaster came. By the time I would have escaped, escape was no longer open to me . . ."

The Red Sun wasn't part of his plans.

As for Abaia & Co. being responsible for the black hole: if they were, then it was only a symbolic gesture. Man had long ago dispersed across the galaxies. Whatever happened to Urth, a used-up backwater planet, would have no impact on the species, was of no real consequence for Man, at least in this iteration of the universe. Urth's only importance, even for the Increate, was symbolic. If Abaia & Co. were responsible for the wounding of the sun, then they were unwitting agents of the Increate, who were doing His bidding, whether they would or no. The entire purpose, in Wolfe's universe, of the wounding of the sun, of Sev's sham trial, and the coming of the New Sun, was to satisfy the whims of the Increate. As Tzadkiel told Sev:

"Or if you wish to put it another way, you have already passed your testing, which was an examination of the future you will create. You are the New Sun. You will be returned to your Urth, and the White Fountain will go with you. The death agonies of the world you know will be offered to the Increate. And they will be indescribable--continents will flounder, as has been said. Much that is beautiful will perish, and with it most of your race; but your home will be reborn."

-Roy

2002-03-04 Michael Straight #

Argues that Abaia and his group are morally responsible for the dying sun, and that the Increate turned their evil act to good purposes rather than using them as unwitting agents. Answers Roy C. Lackey by comparing the reading to the crucifixion, where the Romans bear guilt while God brings good from it, and notes that the result depends on views of foreknowledge and free will.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Abaia's group are guilty, not unwitting agents; The Increate turns evil acts to good purposes

Yet they continue to act against the Autarchy and to try to prevent Severian from bringing the New Sun.

Here we get into deep philosophical/theological waters. This is like saying that the Romans didn't really crucify Jesus, or if they did they were the unwitting agents of God, who were doing his will whether they would or no. The entire purpose, on this reading, of the crucifixion and the resurrection was to satisfy the whims of God.

And there are certainly people who read the New Testament that way.

But there is also the reading that the crucifixion was an evil act committed by the Romans et. al. for which they bear guilt and responsibility, yet God twisted and turned that evil thing and brought good out of it. I submit this is probably the closer to the sense Wolfe intends, that the dying sun is a bad thing, caused by the evil of Abaia et. al., and probably also in some sense the result of humanity's own sins coming home to roost, yet the Increate takes this and turns it to his own purposes.

How much all this ultimately boils down to satisfying the whims of the Increate depends on one's views of divine foreknowledge, omnipotence, predestination, determinism, free will, etc.

-Rostrum

2002-03-10 Steve Strickland #

Asks whether the irrigation of Lune mentioned in The Book of the New Sun is only a reference to the sun's weakening, with the moon's light turning darker green, or a literal afforestation project. Proposes that the folklore arose from a real change in the moon's color and asks whether other passages about Lune's forests settle the question.
questions · myth-and-structure · close-textual-reading · theories: Irrigation of Lune is folklore for the sun's weakening

Is the scene with Rudesind, the only one in BOTNS in which the irrigation of Lune is discussed?

I'd always assumed--I'll admit without much thought on the subject-- that the discussion of the "irrigation of Lune" in the early part of TSOTT was sort of an oblique reference to the sun's weakening. Since the light of the moon is a reflection of the sun's light, the pale green light of the moon would have given way, I suppose, to a darker green, as the sun began to weaken. This historical fact, that over time the moon changed from pale green to darker green, became, in the folklore of a civilization whose science was declining, the afforestation of the moon, which of course would require water to make it work.

This also seems to me to be consistent with Mr. Wolfe's interest in folklore as being derivative of some truth or half-truth (in this case, that the moon had darkened required explanation).

Of course, it is also possible that through some advanced technology the moon had actually been made into a forest. Are there other references in TBOTNS to the irrigation of Lune, or its forests, that might shed more light on whether this is merely a matter of folklore arising from the weakening sun, vs. a fact of science?

Steve Strickland
