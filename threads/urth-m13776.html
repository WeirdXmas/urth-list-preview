On Pedophillia and Homosexuality

Urth list · 17 messages · 2010-07-21 to 2010-07-22

2010urthThe Book of the New SunNicholas JostJerry FriedmanJosh GellerAdam ThorntonGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffJeff WilsonMatthew Weber
The thread: The thread asked how to read Baldanders' sexual relationship with a modified child in The Book of the New Sun, and whether modern categories of pedophilia or sexual orientation apply to a classical-style episode. Nicholas argued that preference was not fixed in antiquity and that the child's nature is unclear, while Matthew Weber supported him with Foucault and Victorian sources. Jerry Friedman disputed this with the Symposium, the word paiderastes, and Severian's remarks on impotence, and Gerry Quinn doubted that classical tolerance of adolescent pederasty was historical fact. Jeff Wilson and Adam Thornton offered speculative accounts of Baldanders' drives and priorities, and the thread drifted into an off-topic exchange about whether engineers are real engineers, with no clear resolution.
  • Nicholas: sexual preference was not fixed in classical antiquity and the child's nature is unclear, so modern categories do not apply
  • Matthew Weber: modern ideas of childhood innocence and fixed orientation are historical constructions that do not fit the classical setting
  • Jerry Friedman: classical Greeks did not assume fixed preference, and Nicholas's own points undercut his reading
  • Jeff Wilson: Baldanders may have a weak sex drive and may have helped Dr Talos's makeover
  • Gerry Quinn: a small child may be human and male without being a man, and classical pederasty is scholarly nostalgia
  • Adam Thornton: Baldanders channeled his energy into study for longevity and power rather than desire

Themes: Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Beasts and the animal in us, Robots, androids and artificial life

2010-07-21 Nicholas Jost #

Argues that Baldanders' sex with a modified child cannot be judged by modern categories, since preference was not fixed in classical antiquity and the child's nature is not clearly human and male. Disputes an unnamed poster's claim that the child is not a man, contrasting Greek views with Christian ethics; suggests Baldanders' flirtations with Severian reflect their rough equality.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Baldanders is an idolater by Christian standards; Baldanders' interest in Severian reflects rough equality; Sexual preference was not fixed in antiquity

" don't see where you get this from. Granted Baldanders is not shown as having any sexual interest in women, and by default one might assume that such a character might (or might not) be gay. But the modified child in his bed hardly qualifies as a man."

You make two bad assumptions that the classic world didn't. The first is that sexual preference is fixed. As I pointed out this is a new (and I think very wrong) idea. Two, that the boys essential nature isn't human and male. What is he, a female mouse? A Greek wouldn't understand the fine difference you're driving at to protect modern sensibilities (sensibilities trained by a halting assumption of Christian ethics). What difference does it make when I sleep with a slave that is three or thirty?

Seen from a Christian perspective Badlanders is an idolaterer of the worst and primal sort. This might explain his strange flirtations with Severian, he's the only man that can be seen as a rough equal. They are almost foils of each other.

2010-07-21 Matthew Weber replying to Nicholas #

Argues that modern assumptions about childhood innocence and fixed sexual orientation are historical constructions that do not fit the classical world assumed in Wolfe's Baldanders episode. Supports Nicholas's comments on sexual orientation and points to Foucault's History of Sexuality and Victorian child prostitution literature as grounds.
supports · genre-and-craft

I wouldn't even say a halting assumption of Christian ethics; it's the influence of the late 19th-century construction of the innocence of childhood (of course, only middle- and upper-class children were innocent, as a perusal of the literature on Victorian child prostitution will show).

Nicholas' comments about sexual orientation are spot-on--see Foucault's History of Sexuality for some very illuminating thinking on this subject.

2010-07-21 Jerry Friedman replying to Nicholas #

Disputes Nicholas's reading of Baldanders and Severian on sexual attraction, arguing that the classical world did not assume fixed sexual preference and that Nicholas's own points undercut his case. Grounds this in the Symposium, the Greek word paiderastes, Severian's remarks on impotent men, and the overgrown boy in Baldanders' bed.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion

From: Nicholas <[email]>

Specifically, not in the endlessly desirable Jolenta.

You did say specifically that Baldanders liked the attentions of "men", not "men and boys". And where should I have noticed that's what he liked? Also, you said that Severian noticed and grokked that men are attracted to other males. Where did I miss that? I remember he mentions some men who are impotent save with men like themselves.

Whether it's right or wrong, isn't it in the Symposium? Some of the two-backed beasts are male-female, some female-female, and some male-male? And Severian seems to believe that at least some people's sexual preference is fixed.

Sure a Greek would understand it. There's a reason for the word paiderastes (if I got that right), boy-lover, not man-lover. And Severian says he doesn't believe Baldanders practiced pederasty with the overgrown boy. As far as I can tell, you're assuming that people are sexually attracted to other people's essential nature, whatever that is. I think age and other superficial features have a lot to do with it. If A is attracted to B when B is thirty, it doesn't follow that A is attracted to B when B is three (or ninety). Most people find secondary sexual characteristics attractive. (Of course, Severian may have meant that Baldanders might have been waiting till the boy developed those characteristics. It's easier to believe B could be attractive at both thirteen and thirty.)

...

They're both rape, and I believe they're both evil, but I imagine it makes a difference to the victim. And as I said above, it probably makes a difference to the slaveowner, too.

I think you're right to call attention to the differences between ancient and modern beliefs, and to the possibility that the latest belief might not be the ultimate truth, and to the possibility that Wolfe didn't believe the latest belief of the time when he wrote BotNS, but I think you're taking things too far.

Jerry Friedman

2010-07-21 Jeff Wilson replying to Jerry Friedman #

Argues that Baldanders may have a weak or absent sex drive, given his age and prolonged adolescent growth, and that he may have helped with Dr Talos's makeover, giving him associations with her. Offers the King's College DNA researchers' treatment of Rosalind Franklin as a comparison for a possibly dismissive, sexist attitude toward women.
proposes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Baldanders assisted Dr Talos's makeover

Baldanders likely assisted Dr Talos with her makeover, and so would have a lot of associations with her that others would not. Also, Baldanders is really old and despite his prolonged adolescent growth, he doesn't seem to have the teenage horniness one might expect, so he could be "done" or just have a low sex drive.

At first I read this as "impatient", and perhaps he is indeed the sort of sexist pig who doesn't have time for women, despite being otherwise enlightened about the human condition. A similar attitude was prevalent at King's College among the mostly male DNA reasearchers at the expense of Rosalind Franklin.
