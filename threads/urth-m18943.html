the atmosphere on the bridge

The thread: Jason H. asked why the crowded bridge at the start of Severian's exile in Shadow chapter XIV sits beside a near-empty Water Way, and later proposed that the bridge lies on a higher street reached only by broken steps, so carriages cannot descend. Jeff Wilson, Son of Witz and David Stockhoff explained the crowd through the bridge as an urban bottleneck, a makeshift night bazaar, or a dividing line between slum districts that guards can close. Lee Berman and Ryan Dunn offered readings in which Severian acts in a staged play under hidden strings, while Ryan Dunn also defended the lochage's harsh discipline as a response to crowds. Jason H. then said the western-side traffic answered his question, and the thread ended without any agreement on the play theory.
  • Jason H.: asked about the crowded bridge versus empty Water Way, then proposed the higher street blocks carriages
  • Jeff Wilson: sparse crowd explained by bridges as urban bottlenecks
  • Son of Witz: the bridge is a gritty midnight bazaar
  • Lee Berman: the bridge crowds may be a play Severian unknowingly acts in
  • Ryan Dunn: guards are harsh because hordes are hard to control; Severian is a puppet of hidden strings
  • David Stockhoff: the bridge divides a dying slum from a livelier district and guards can close it

Themes: Stories within stories, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Colonization and the native

2011-05-17 Jason H #

Asks whether the crowded bridge scene at the start of Severian's exile in Shadow of the Torturer chapter XIV makes sense, given the near-empty Water Way and the quiet night. Points to the contrast of few passersby, a song heard a league away, and then carriages, vendors and a hundred people surrounding Severian at the guard post.
questions · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic

I'm confused about the atmosphere on the bridge that Severian crosses at the very beginning of his exile (Shadow chapter XIV).

Previously he had been walking on the Water Way, and I get the impression there are few people about. He says he no longer appeared a torturer "in the eyes of the few who passed me"; also, he was able to hear the song from a ship, carried by the wind, from a league away.

But when he steps on the bridge, the scene is suddenly crowded: there are carriages, there are vendors crying out, and people "thronging" the walkway. When he stops to talk to the guard, he is suddenly surrounded by a hundred people. All of this in the dead of the night.

Does this make sense? Why would the bridge be so crowded, but the walkway on one side of the river be practically deserted? Aren't the throngs of people *crossing* the bridge?

It's not a very important point, I'll grant. But I think that one of the things that makes Wolfe special is that he tends to think through these things carefully beforehand. His scenes feel real because he's taken the time to make sure there are no internal contradictions. I'm sure he drastically changed the atmosphere on purpose, but I can't make the scene work in my mind, because I can't visualize it as really happening. Any thoughts on this minor point would be appreciated. Thanks.

-Jason

2011-05-17 Jeff Wilson replying to Jason H #

Argues that the sparse crowd Severian meets on the bridge in Shadow chapter XIV is explained by how bridges work as urban bottlenecks, not by the scene being empty. Supports the idea with analogies to the bridge in Saving Private Ryan, the broad bridges of Nessus, London and Paris, and how decadent cities clutter their bridges with stalls.
supports · genre-and-craft

Bridges are very often bottlenecks for traffic; there are generally at least three lanes of various sizes leading away from a bridge footing in a city, but the bridge is generally only as wide as it has to be due to the expense of construction and maintenance, and as a defensive measure. The spacing between bridges also factors in, there are many choices to cross between streets on dry land but only so many bridges between the river banks. Consider the important of the bridge at the end of SAVING PRIVATE RYAN.

OF course, big cities like Nessus of have luxuriously broad numerous bridges, not unlike the various bridges across the Thames in London, but in times of asuterity, decadence, and/or corruption, these inevitably become considered additional real estate and become covered with stationary concerns until again there is a bottleneck only as wide as it has to be. The relatively uncluttered bridges across the Seine in Paris are a rare sign of enlightened self-interest on the part of the city government.

Or that's my impression, I'm not a civil engineer or anything.

2011-05-17 Jason H replying to Jason H #

Proposes that the crowded bridge in Shadow chapter XIV does not spill onto the Water Way because the bridge sits on a higher street reached only by broken steps, so carriages cannot descend there. Grounds this in the text's description of climbing from the Water Way to the elevated bridge street, offering a parallel populated road as the answer to the deserted-waterfront puzzle.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Bridge street is elevated, parallel to a populated road

Thanks for the interesting replies. I agree that the bridge must be some sort of mall-like bazaar. One particular point that bothered me though was why this traffic didn't flow over onto the Water Way on the other side. People and carts are presumably crossing the bridge, so how could the Water Way remain so deserted?

I think there's a hint of an answer in the text (of course): "Leaving the lapping tongues of the river, I mounted a flight of broken steps from the Water Way to the more elevated street of the bridge..." The carriages from the bridge wouldn't go down a flight of stairs to the Water Way. There's probably a more populated elevated road, parallel to the Water Way, which takes on the masses of people leaving the bridge.

-Jason

2011-05-17 Ryan Dunn replying to Son of Witz #

Argues that the lochage's hordes on the bridge in The Book of the New Sun are hard to control, so guards meet disturbances with harsh discipline, as Severian nearly experienced at the bartizan. Supports this by quoting the lochage's speech about the city's beggars and the impossibility of tolerating disturbances, taking him at his word.
supports · close-textual-reading

Also, if you take the lochage at his word, the hordes are likely hard to control and often met with similar discipline to what Severian nearly encountered at the bartizan. Fortunately for Sev, he was clearly no ordinary disturber of the peace, even though the guards would have been all too happy to throw him in the dungeon.

"The city grows and changes every night, like writing chalked on a wall. Houses are built in the streets by clever people who take up the cobbles in the dark and claim the ground - did you know that? The exultant Talarican, whose madness manifested itself as a consuming interest in the lowest aspects of human existence, claimed that the persons who live by devouring the garbage of others number two gross thousands. That there are ten thousand begging acrobats, of whom nearly half are women. That if a pauper were to leap from the parapet of this bridge each time we draw breath, we should live forever, because the city breeds and breaks men faster than we respire. Among such a throng, there is no alternative to peace. Disturbances cannot be tolerated, because disturbances cannot be extinguished. Do you follow me?"

...ryan

2011-05-17 Ryan Dunn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Severian is an unknowing actor whose encounters in The Book of the New Sun are guided by hidden strings, citing the stage-play imagery such as Dr. Talos's plays and Severian's dreams of wooden puppets. Responds to Lee Berman's remark that the bridge atmosphere resembles a staged play, extending it with a theory of a time-traveling master manipulating Severian as a vessel.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Severian is an unknowing actor manipulated by strings; A master time traveler orchestrates Severian's encounters

Don't get me going on the puppet metaphor here, I have strong suspicions there are marionette strings all throughout this story, some hidden better than others.

It justifies the chance encounters, and explains the small and large stage play metaphors that permeate the pages, whether it be Dr. Talos's plays or Severian's dreams of the wooden puppets.

And it's what might happen if you had a master time traveler who had a vested interest in saving the world, and a subject in Severian who was the perfect (literally) vessel.

...ryan
