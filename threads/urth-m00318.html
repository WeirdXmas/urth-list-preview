Death and Destruction

Urth list · 10 messages · 2005-04-03 to 2005-04-08

2005urthThe Book of the New SunJames WynnChris PTony EllisDan'l Danehy-OakesMaru Dubshinki
The thread: The thread asked whether the Biblical remnant concept illuminates death and destruction in Wolfe's work. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes proposed the remnant idea and suggested Wolfe may use it unconsciously. Maru argued the remnant reflects us-versus-them thinking that makes survivors seem purer, and later defended measuring morality by worlds rather than individual lives. Tony Ellis disputed that the commonwealth is doomed, citing ordinary lives and Ascian weakness, while Maru countered with the sailors' fate and the New Sun fork. Chris challenged the world-counting argument as resting on hidden premises that make present people worthless, and the exchange ended unresolved with Maru's replies largely unclear.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the remnant motif in Wolfe may be an unconscious Biblical echo
  • maru: the remnant is an us-versus-them effect, and worlds should be the moral unit
  • Tony Ellis: the commonwealth is not doomed and its ordinary lives matter
  • Chris: individual lives, not worlds, should be counted, and the world-count view rests on hidden premises
  • James Wynn: us-versus-them thinking is universal, not tied to any camp
  • Crush: ironic jab at us-versus-them thinking, quoted but not argued at length

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Power, rulers and politics, Deep time and ruins

2005-04-03 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Tony Ellis #

Proposes that the Biblical concept of the remnant, a faithful few preserved after divine punishment, sheds light on the thread's question of death and destruction in Wolfe's work. Grounds this in recurring Biblical examples such as Noah's sons, the Benjaminites and the Babylonian returnees, and suggests Wolfe may use the theme unconsciously.
proposes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Wolfe's destruction motifs follow the Biblical remnant pattern

I've been too busy (and still am) to comment on this
thread; but...

It seems to me that a bit of light might be shed on the
whole subject by attending to the Biblical concept, of
_the remnant_. This is a recurring theme throughout:
the idea that though Mankind or Israel sins, and God
chastises, He always brings forth a remnant to carry
on. This applies to the sons of Noah, to the Benjaminites, to the returnees from the Babylonian Exile, and in smaller ways to many other things. You find the theme all through the prophetic books of the Bible.

I suspect strongly that Wolfe has this theme in mind,
though perhaps not consciously.

--Dan'l

2005-04-03 Maru Dubshinki replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the biblical idea of the remnant, where a purified survivor group carries on after divine punishment, is better explained as a natural result of us-versus-them thinking that makes the surviving group seem more pure. Grounds this in the observation that the remnant is always smaller and 'more pure' than the people it replaces, and extends the point to Robert Jordan's Wheel of Time series as well as Wolfe.
proposes

I personally suspect, since the remnant is always
'more pure' than the previous population, this is merely natural fall out of the 'us vs them' way of thinking, where 'us' is always smaller than 'them'. Fires up the ol' zealotry spirit even in cases where thinking so is absurd. (Witness how Christians in the US feel presecuted!)
That, and 'we were spared, so God must like us better!'.

But Wolfe is not alone- look at Jordan's WOT series, and tell me if you can't see it there as well.

~Maru

disputes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Undines chose slow freezing and doomed their progeny

I recollect the Undines said that they could have, at any moment taken the Commonwealth
but they forebore, to try and stop the New Sun. I find one of the interesting things in Urth of the New

to let Urth die a slow death, than a violent rebirth. 70-odd years later the Commonwealth is still there
because as Severian says, the 'time when my death could have prevented the coming of the New Sun was past',

equal or be less (!) than N-1 worlds of people.

2005-04-08 Chris P replying to maru #

Argues that counting future human potential by number of worlds rests on hidden premises, such as humans filling every world and Urth being unrecoverable, and that this view makes present people worthless. Raises the idea that Urth is treated as a microcosm whose death and rebirth stand for everything, asking whether this allegory belongs to Wolfe or to Severian, responding to maru's quoted argument about worlds as the measure of human potential.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Urth's death and rebirth stand for everything

Measuring by worlds is at best one step removed from what you're really trying to get at. There are hidden premises required which can at best be contingently true, if that. For one thing, to make "worlds" be the limiting factor it would require that humans find and eventually fill to capacity all available worlds. For another thing, why couldn't the planet be "rehabilitated" after the ice future comes to pass and repopulated by human cultures that left earlier? All they need is enough science to whip themselves up a white fountain.

This perspective perhaps treats all humans as equal, but just in the sense of being equally valueless. No number of presently existing human beings, no matter how large, is capable of outweighing infinite "potential" future generations. Actual human beings, in this view, can count for nothing.

On another note, I think that to some extent we are not thinking of Urth as just a planet like any other. Through the framing device of the play, we are at some level being led to treat Urth as a sort of microcosm. Its death and rebirth are in some way treated as the death and rebirth of *everything*. I'm not sure if this sort of allegory should be attributed to Wolfe, or if it would be better to attribute it to Severian.
