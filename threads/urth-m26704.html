2018-05-22 Marc Aramini #

Argues that Goldwurm in The Sorcerer's House is the Lamia, linking the strangler and butler Nicholas to Greek lamia and empusa myths, the Kikimora and Knecht Ruprecht, and the Corinthian coin. Builds the case from the lamia's blood-drinking, sleepless flaming eyes, ambiguous gender, and the illusory mansion that vanishes when Apollonius exposes Lamia at Corinth, concluding that this bodes ill for Bax.
proposes · source-and-allusion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Goldwurm is the Lamia; Nicholas and Goldwurm share the Lamia's blood-sucking; The Sorcerer's House mansion is an illusion like Lamia's

I was hung up on who Goldwurm was ... Wolfe did a number on us. There are three spirits with three manifestations. The Kikimora, Knecht Ruprecht (Robin Goodfellow/The Devil) and ... finally, the one who is the strangler Goldwurm and the butler Nicholas, who sucks the blood of children. There is one monster who was used as a bogeyman for children, who sucked their blood or ripped them from the womb. The lamia, who, not always a serpent, was a shapeshifter. When her children died, killed by the jealous Hera there are several features that she has. One is that she could never sleep, tormented, so she could remove her eyes and possess vessels through them. The flaming eyes of Lupine and Nicholas are mentioned. In addition, in some myths, she was ambiguously gendered, as one noxious item was lamia's testicles (see the Greek gold coin on which the obverse side is a woman who looks like a man to Doris and the fact that Winkle is called a HE by Iuean).

Finally, and the seal of the deal, is this, from Philostratis and the Life of Appolonius about Lamia of CORINTH - note that the coin is CORINTHIAN!!!!!!!!:

Lamia is the vulgar term, empousa the proper term:

"The *empousa* admits in the end to fattening up her victim (Menippus of Lycia) to be consumed, as she was in the habit of targeting young men for food "because their blood was fresh and pure".[36]
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamia#cite_note-philostratus-tr-phillimore-43> The
last statement has led to the surmise that this lamia/empusa was a sort of blood-sucking vampire.[44]
<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamia#cite_note-dict-grbm-empusa-53>

Another aspect of her powers is that this empusa/lamia is able to create an illusion of a sumptuous mansion, with all the accoutrements and even servants. But once Apollonius reveals her false identity at the wedding, the illusion fails her and vanishes"

There we have it - the house as illusion of the ambiguously gendered and fierce eyed-lamia, in conjunction with the stories of Knecht Ruprecht and the Kikimora, means this bodes very ill for Bax. Goldwurm is the Lamia. Her rival is Hera, but does Hera appear to strike down the illegitimate children of Zeus in the text?

2018-05-23 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that in this SH retelling Apollonius (Ambrosius) never sees through the illusion and Bax never does either, which explains the ghost's crying at the end. Supports the claim with the manjushri advice to team with Kate Finn, and asks how Wolfe's Jesus-like master figure relates to resurrection.
proposes · puzzle-solving · allegory-and-typology · theories: Bax never sees through the illusion; Ghost cries because Bax stays deceived; Manjushri illusion ties Bax to his brother's death

In this ?retelling?, Apollonius (Ambrosius in the text) never figures it out and gets killed. Bax never sees through the illusion, and that?s why the ghost is crying at the end. He almost sees through the manjushri illusion to cut through the duality at one point (manjushri advises him to team up with Kate Finn to ?find his brother?, but that results in a way to literally and metaphorically take his brother?s life).

I wonder how Jesus like Wolfe wanted to make that master figure in regards to resurrection, etc.
