The thread: The thread opened with Eric Bourland reading the phrase "blue of cheek" as pallor, linking it to Severian's unusual paleness and the wan light, and answering Antonin Scriabin's blue-blood reading. Jeff Wilson countered with an 1897 slang dictionary entry for "blue cheek" as a shaved-whisker style and noted that cheeks are usually the last place to turn blue from cold, citing Outcasts of London. The discussion then shifted to condolences after Rosemary Wolfe's death, when Bourland proposed a card or flowers sent via Tor Books to respect family privacy. Craig Brewer backed Sergei Soloviev's collective-card idea over a flood of mail. The phrase debate was not resolved, and the thread ended in agreement on the card.
  • Eric Bourland: "blue of cheek" means pale, fitting Severian's pallor and the wan light
  • Jeff Wilson: "blue cheek" was a whisker style, and cheeks rarely turn blue from cold
  • Eric Bourland: send a card via Tor Books to respect the Wolfe family's privacy
  • Craig Brewer: supports a simple collective card from concerned readers rather than many letters

2013-12-16 Eric Bourland #

Proposes that the phrase "blue of cheek" in the Chinese editor's query means pale, since Severian is unusually pale and the wan light could make a cheek look blue. Responds to Antonin Scriabin's blue-blood reading by offering a simpler explanation grounded in the context of Severian's own paleness.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Blue of cheek means pale

Blue of cheek ... pale. That is how I read it. Since Severian is unusually pale, I would suppose she would be commenting on that paleness. In certain wan light the cheek might look blue.

2013-12-16 Jeff Wilson replying to Eric Bourland #

Offers a dictionary citation from an 1897 slang dictionary for 'blue cheek' as a style of shaved whiskers that leave the cheek colored, rather than a sign of paleness. Responds to Eric Bourland's reading of 'blue of cheek' as pallor by noting that cheeks are usually the last place to turn blue from cold, citing Outcasts of London as the source.
informs · source-and-allusion · theories: Blue of cheek refers to a whisker style

A Dictionary of Slang, Jargon & Cant Embracing English, American ..., Volume 1
edited by Albert Barr?re, Charles Godfrey Leland (1897) cites Outcasts of London by J. Greenwood for blue cheek as a style of wearing whiskers, shaved but leaving enough to color the cheek.

Pale skin is also given to being called blue when chilled, but the cheeks are usually the last place in that condition, IMO.
