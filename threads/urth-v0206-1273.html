The thread: The thread asked whether mythological names like Typhon's show an alien origin on the starcrosser Whorl, and whether the saints naming convention applies to him. maa32 argued names signal difference rather than origin, citing Talos as a mechanical man from Urth, while Dan'l Danehy-Oakes held Typhon falls outside the saints convention because he is not a saint. Jerry Friedman and James Wynn pressed on what "alien" means, distinguishing non-Urth-born, non-human, and foreign to the Commonwealth, and Michael Straight clarified the Commonwealth-citizen reading. Matthew Malthouse added the question of whether hierodules count as created like humans. The thread drifted into definitions without settling a single reading, and Alice K. Turner separately raised Roman and Germanic names in the story.
  • maa32: mythological names signal difference, not alien origin, citing Talos
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Typhon falls outside the saints convention since he is not a saint
  • Jerry Friedman: a Saint Piaton proves little, and "alien" is used ambiguously
  • Michael Straight: the point is that Typhon is not a Commonwealth citizen, not that he is not a saint
  • James Wynn: asks whether alien means not Urth-born, not human, or foreign to the Commonwealth
  • matthew.malthouse: Typhon is human but not of Earth, unlike created Talos

Themes: Names and their meanings, Robots, androids and artificial life, Beasts and the animal in us

2002-06-20 Marc Aramini #

Argues that mythological names like Typhon's do not prove an alien origin, since Talos is a mechanical man from Urth and the name may instead signal difference. Responds to Borski's claim by citing the Talos example and comparing Typhon's name to assumed papal and regnal names like John Paul II and Henry IV.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Mythological names signal traits, not alien origin; Typhon's name may be an assumed title

Mr. Borski has recently made the point that Typhon's name is mythological, implying that he should be of alien stock rather than simply of "other", urthly origin. However, aren't there mythological names, like Talos, that reveal traits rather than place of origin? Talos is a mechanical man, but he is not necessarily extra-terrestrial - indeed, he is from Urth. It does imply that he is "other" - and while I CAN see a slight difference between Baldanders and the mythological names, I don't find the presence of a mythological name as complete evidence that Typhon must be extraterrestrial - rather, it could be an assumed name that shows his difference and superiority to his "normal" subjects (much as Karol Wojtyla would choose the name John Paul II or Bullingbrook would name himself Henry IV - it is common to choose a name of distinction to establish the authority of "otherness", and insinuate that you are of uncommon stock). Just a note - I think we need slightly more than a mythological name to definitely brand someone as Alien, since I don't think all the characters with mythological names are from the outer reaches, especially as evidenced by Dr. Talos.
Marc Aramini

2002-06-20 Jerry Friedman #

Points out that a Saint Piaton existing proves little about naming conventions, since many people shared names like Simon before saints existed, and questions whether posters use "alien" to mean non-Urth-born, non-human, or foreign to the Commonwealth. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's remark that Typhon does not fall under the saints naming convention, offering his own distinctions instead of a firm reading.
disputes · author-and-biography

There's a Saint Piaton (which proves nothing, since there were lots of people named Simon--Shim`on, I think--before anyone was in a position to be a Catholic saint).

There seems to be some confusion, by the way, over whether people are using "alien" to mean "not born on Urth though possibly human" or "not human". Or maybe just "foreign to the Commonwealth", like Loyal to the Group of Seventeen, and presumably Ash.
...

Jerry Friedman

Observes that Vodalus and his followers, along with Juterna, have Roman or Latin names, suggesting a simple division between Romans and Christians in the story. Notes that the renegade warriors in the forest have Germanic names and suggests the Lexicon Urthus already covers this material.
