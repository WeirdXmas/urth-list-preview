flying ship

Urth list · 9 messages · 2007-11-29 to 2007-12-06

The thread: Ashley Crill proposed that the flying ship on Severian's tomb is a flying tender, not a sailing ship, and Jeff Wilson offered a related reading with a seaworthy hull that appears to fly above the rose and fountain. Matthew asked what the fountain in the ship passage is, citing heraldic fountains, and Andy Robertson identified it as the Vatic fountain in the House Absolute. Tony Ellis disputed this, arguing the reference is to the White Fountain and that symbolism matters more than appearance, which led Jeff Wilson to insist symbols must be recognizable to convey meaning. Tony Ellis then argued that Severian's self-chosen coat of arms has valid in-fiction context even if other characters cannot read it, and the thread ended with the builder of the tomb left open and the disagreement over symbolism unresolved.
  • Ashley Crill: the tomb's flying ship is an aerial tender that carried Severian to Tzadkiel's ship
  • Jeff Wilson: the ship is a seaworthy-hulled tender that appears to fly above the rose and fountain; Wolfe would not write symbols without valid context
  • Matthew: questions whether an heraldic fountain fits the image, citing Runcorn arms
  • Andy Robertson: the fountain is the Vatic fountain in the House Absolute
  • Tony Ellis: the fountain is the White Fountain; symbolism matters more than appearance, and Severian's chosen arms need no outside recognition
  • Ashley Crill: symbolism must be identifiable to convey meaning

2007-11-29 Ashley Crill #

Argues that the flying ship graven on Severian's tomb in The Citadel of the Autarch is a flying vessel, not a sailing ship, and may be the tender that carried him to Tzadkiel's ship. Grounds this on the text's wording, noting that none of Severian's sailing vessels have wings and that the tender looked like a terrestrial ship to a young Severian.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: The graven ship on Severian's tomb is the tender that flew him to Tzadkiel's ship

'Ship volant' is not the only piece of evidence we have concerning the nature of the ship on Severian's tomb. Consider this bit: I know too in whose mausoleum I tarried as a child, that little building of stone with its rose, its fountain and its flying ship all graven. I have disturbed my own tomb, and now I go to lie in it." Citadel XXXVIII

Since none of the sailing vessels that Severian uses in the narrative are mentioned as ahving wings, I think its safe to assume that he does mean a ship that flies. The ship is graven on his tomb as one of the symbols of his life, and to those that built the tomb, Severian disappeared while traveling on a space ship. Its possible that the graven ship is actually the tender on which Severian flew to Tzadkiel's ship. The tender looks like a terrestrial sailing vessel according to Book of the New Sun, which could have led to the ambiguity about its nature, especially in the mind of a young Severian who may have originally thought that it was a terrestrial vessel.

2007-12-03 Jeff Wilson replying to Ashley Crill #

Argues that the flying ship on Severian's tomb would be a tender with a seaworthy hull for water landings, resembling a sailing vessel and perhaps lacking a waterline. Responds to Ashley Crill's use of the tomb passage in Citadel XXXVIII, reasoning that the ship's whole hull shows above the rose and fountain so it seems to fly above them.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: the tomb ship is a tender with a seaworthy hull

I think the tender would primarily resemble a sailing vessel in that it would be likely to have a seaworthy hull for water landings and taking advantage of less-sophisticated port facilities.

Perhaps the notion of a ship with no waterline would apply. in part. If the ship appears above the rose and the fountain, with the entire hull visible, it might seem to be flying above the other two.

2007-12-03 Jeff Wilson replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that a symbol must be recognized and correctly identified before it can convey its intended meaning, pushing back on the view that the appearance of the White Fountain or the flying ship does not matter. Responds to Tony Ellis by insisting Wolfe is too careful to write symbolism that has no valid context in the fictional setting.
disputes · genre-and-craft

However, a symbol has to be recognized and properly identified in order to convey the intended meaning. I suppose the symbolism could be intended solely for the reader and have no valid context in the fictional setting but I think most of his readers would agree Wolfe is a bit more careful than that.

2007-12-05 Tony Ellis replying to Ashley Crill #

Argues that the symbols on Severian's coat of arms have a valid context within the fiction, since Severian himself chose them, so their meaning need not be recognizable to other characters. Responds to Ashley Crill's claim that symbolism must be identifiable to convey meaning, and that Wolfe would not leave it solely for readers.
disputes · close-textual-reading

The symbolism does have a valid context in the fictional setting. Severian, a character in the fiction, has chosen these symbols. The fact that other people won't recognise the meaning of one of them is irrelevant. He's under no obligation to create a coat of arms that makes sense to anyone but himself.

2007-12-06 Tony Ellis replying to Ashley Crill #

Argues that whoever built the tomb need not be Severian, but that the coat of arms on it must have been designed by Severian or someone who knew the cosmic secrets of his life. Responds to Ashley Crill's question about whether Severian was both interred in the tomb and its builder, drawing on the tomb and its heraldry in The Book of the New Sun.
answers · puzzle-solving · theories: Severian or an informed person designed the tomb's coat of arms

Anyone could have built the tomb, as far as I can see. But either Severian, or someone else who knew the cosmic secrets of his life, must have designed its coat of arms.
