2017-10-13 incanto #

Proposes that Morcaine's transformation into her brother's face in the feast scene is a glimpse of her sorcery, with her intercepting a communication meant for Arnthor. Grounds the reading in the quoted line where the speaker finds herself addressing him after turning, and notes that how much Arnthor knows remains unclear.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Morcaine's face turns into Arnthor's through sorcery

The transformation of Morcaine's face into her brother also reduces them into a "sibling" character, though it only seems to make thematic rather than surface plot sense. "[Morcaine's] face became that of her brother, I cannot say how. I was not conscious of having turned, yet it was to him I spoke." I think GW is giving us a glimpse here of Morcaine's sorcery. She "intercepted" the communication meant for her brother. How much of that Arnthor knows about we don't know. Classic Wolfe. FW.

2017-10-13 Marc Aramini replying to incanto #

Argues that Morcaine and Arnthor merge into one dream-like figure, a pre-birth struggle between siblings who become a single twin to be born into America, in The Wizard Knight. Grounds this in the feast scene's quoted lines about her face becoming her brother's and the mother's dream, and compares it to There Are Doors as an internal landscape.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · myth-and-structure · theories: Morcaine and Arnthor are one pre-birth twin figure

If you followed the other posts, I was getting at something else, of course: Morcaine and Arnthor (and everyone, really) are collapsing into one dream-like figure engaged in a pre-birth struggle, that between siblings fighting for resources who will soon become one chimeric twin to be born into America - "how far to the dream my mother had?" - simultaneously vastly and impossibly distant while shockingly omnipresent. Of all Wolfe's works, I think this and There Are Doors reduce to almost entirely internal landscapes.
