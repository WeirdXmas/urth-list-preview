The thread: Antonin Scriabin argued that the technological Singularity is a lazy trope in mainstream science fiction, where tech becomes magic and expert characters are never mystified. 2013-12-29 Antonin Scriabin replying to Gerry Quinn #

Argues that the technological Singularity is a lazy trope in mainstream science fiction, citing Charles Stross's Accelerando and Harrison's Light as examples where tech becomes magic. Grounds the criticism in the claim that expert characters who are never mystified let authors easily explain away narrative problems; it responds to a Gwern Branwen quote about Gene Wolfe's supernatural nature and Damon Knight's bean remark.
disputes

The Singularity is a pretty popular trope in modern, mainstream sci-fi. Charles Stross' "Accelerando" and Harrison's "Light" come to mind. I didn't particularly care for either one, I think it makes for lazy writing. Tech becomes magic, but characters aren't mystified, they are experts. It is easy to explain away problems in the narrative.

2013-12-30 Lee Berman replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that Wolfe's most autobiographical characters, Weer, Severian and Horn, express self-criticism that moves toward confession and self-condemnation, and that the author may be likened to the monstrous gods of the black-bean origin story. Responds to Gerry Quinn's joke about the black beans by reasoning from the poster's view of Wolfe as genial but sharp, linking Scylla, Typhon/Pas and Echidna to the Cumaean.
proposes · author-and-biography · myth-and-structure · theories: Weer, Severian and Horn embody Wolfe's self-condemnation; Wolfe the author resembles monstrous giant gods; Black beans origin includes Scylla, Typhon/Pas and Echidna

With recognition of the lightheartedness of this reply, I truly do not think that Gene Wolfe considers himself to be the "nicest" of people.

I haven't met him in person but I know he is often described as "genial". Still in the tone of some of his answers to direct questions, I know he has a sharp side. But these are all external judgements anyway.

I find Wolfe's self-assessments best exemplified in his most autobiographical characters (Weer, Severian and Horn, imho). In these characters I find self-criticism which goes beyond acknowledgement of imperfection and which drifts toward true confession and self- condemnation.

Moreover, if the role of author may be considered as demiurgical creator, as has been discussed in other threads, perhaps the comparison of Wolfe himself to giant god-like monsters is apt. If "black beans" hold the origin of "Abaia, Erebus and the rest", then Scylla must be included and thus, by extension, Typhon/Pas and Echidna (whom I find difficult to disconnect from The Cumaean).

2013-12-30 Gerry Quinn replying to Lee #

Replies to Lee by imagining Wolfe's sea monsters as nanotech-built singularity beings rather than sharing Typhon's origin, and rejects Lee's view that Typhon is relatively human. Cites the author-as-demiurge idea raised in an earlier thread and the 'black beans' origin of Abaia and Erebus as the context for the sea monsters' nature.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Sea monsters are nanotech-built singularity beings; Typhon did not share the sea monsters' origin

From: Lee

Yes, he comes across as genial but often suddenly prickly at times.

<Shrug> I love the books, I'm not actually all that concerned about the author.

I don't believe Typhon had the same origin as the sea monsters. Typhon is relatively human, I think.

I doubt Wolfe would approve, but I do actually imagine the sea monsters in terms of 'singularity' concepts - they are creatures that can reconstruct themselves on a molecular level, and they have brains or other devices capable of powerful computations. They are gods [or demons] by our standards. I think the 'seeds; were nanofactories that built them. But that is equally true of all the seeds of living organisms.

- Gerry Quinn
