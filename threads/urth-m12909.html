The Politics Of Gene Wolfe

Urth list · 16 messages · 2010-07-02 to 2010-07-06

2010urthThe Book of the New SunMatthew KeeleyRebecca Bushong-TaylorLee BermanDave LeblingJack SmithJosh GellerAdam ThorntonGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffJames WynnJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: The thread asked whether Gene Wolfe's politics show in his treatment of weapons, particularly the Second Amendment. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Jack Smith argued that Wolfe arms his characters across many settings and does not blanket-condemn lethal force, with David Stockhoff stressing violence and oppression beyond guns. Lee Berman, Jeff Wilson and James Wynn fought over the amendment's militia clause and its meaning, with little reference to Wolfe's texts. Replies from Rebecca Bushong-Taylor, Matthew Keeley and Gerry Quinn mostly took the knife-throwing anecdote as light social talk, and the thread ended with a late off-topic exchange about the Federalist papers, so it drifted away from the books.
  • Jack Smith: Wolfe arms characters across settings, so he sees weapons as usable for good and evil
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: guns are morally neutral, with ethical weight in their use
  • Lee Berman: the militia clause supports a state-defence reading, linked to van Vogt
  • Jeff Wilson: well regulated meant working order and militia meant the armed populace
  • David Stockhoff: the deeper point is a violent world where the strong oppress the weak
  • James Wynn: mock-serious irony that the Second Amendment protects state legislatures

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Justice, torture and mercy, War and soldiers

2010-07-02 James Wynn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues, with heavy irony, that the Second Amendment's 'right of the people' should be read as the rights of state legislatures, not individuals, by parallel reading of the First Amendment's assembly and petition clause. Responds to Lee Berman's reading that the militia wording shows arms are for state defense; the post is mock-serious and not about Wolfe's books.
disputes

An illuminating reading. And since the amendment addresses "the right of the People", we get a deeper understanding of this text:

"Congress shall make no law [snip] abridging [snip] the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances."

Obviously these Rights of the People refer merely to the rights of state legislatures to assemble in session and for them to petition the Federal government .

2010-07-02 Jeff Wilson replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that in the Second Amendment "well regulated" meant kept in proper working order and "militia" meant the armed able-bodied male populace rather than a formal organization, correcting Lee Berman's reading of the amendment. Responds to Berman's quoted claim that the wording makes the militia a state function, offering historical sense of the terms rather than citing Wolfe's books or text.
disputes

At the time "well regulated" meant kept in proper working order, like a clock that kept accurate time and chimed at regular intervals. Militia referred to the armed, able-bodied male populace rather than a particular organization. Drills and deployment were arranged at the state and local levels out of practicality.

2010-07-02 Lee Berman #

Argues that the Second Amendment's wording, read through its militia clause, supports a state-defence reading rather than the libertarian individual-rights reading, and links this to A.E. van Vogt's story The Weapon Shop. Suggests Wolfe may have read that story, since he mentions van Vogt in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, but the post offers no textual evidence from Wolfe's books.
proposes

There is a classic A.E. Van Vogt story called The Weapon Shop which addresses this issue using that interpretation. http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Weapon_Shop Great pulp SF story. I loved it.

And didn't Gene Wolfe mention Van Vogt in Fifth Head of Cerberus? I bet he has read that story.

Anyway, the wording of the Second Amendment is-

To me this wording suggests an interpretation counter to the standard Libertarian view which seems to focus on the word "free" and ignores the other 26 words. Bearing arms would seem to be meant for the defense of the state not to attack the state or defend against it. A "well regulated militia" sounds like a state organized function to me not some sort of individual defense against the state or lawless brigands or wild animals.

Argues that Wolfe repeatedly arms his characters, from Severian's sword and Silk's weapons to guns in Castleview and Free Live Free, and so suggests he sees weapons as usable for good as well as evil. 2010-07-02 David Stockhoff replying to Jack Smith #

Agrees with Jack Smith that Wolfe arms his characters across many settings, but argues the deeper point is a violent, chaotic world where the strong oppress the weak, with weapons not always the main tool of oppression. Cites the uhlans on the high road and the electric whips of the armigers as examples of violence beyond guns.
supports · close-textual-reading

Absolutely. Nor any blanket condemnation of city governments who try to keep violent crime down by banning deadly weapons too small to use to any military effect even in the 18th century.

But definitely a depiction of the world as a rough, violent, chaotic place few would want to live in without an armed guard, where the strong routinely oppress the weak, without arms necessarily even appearing as a primary means of achieving said oppression, aside from the uhlans on the high road and the electric whips of the armigers.
