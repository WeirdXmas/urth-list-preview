a question, some Cthulhiana, and some EG spoilers (maybe)

Urth list · 6 messages · 2008-10-12 to 2008-10-15

2008urthDuncan TruterGreg BatesAdam ThorntonThomas BittermanDave Tallman
The thread: Readers discussed whether Wolfe's An Evil Guest (Eyes of the Overworld) is a Lovecraftian or Cthulhu Mythos work, and who its characters map onto. Duncan Truter argued Gideon Chase, if an Elder God, could only be Nyarlathotep, and asked whether Wolfe was working in the broader Mythos or strictly Lovecraft-Cthulhu terms. Adam Thornton drew a Chandler noir comparison and also tied Chase to Nyarlathotep. Thomas Bitterman offered a Lovecraftian reading of double identities, naming Chase as Cthulhu and favouring Shub-Niggurath's Black Ram for a second figure. Dave Tallman pushed a separate link, identifying Randolph Carter with Jack the Ripper in a different Wolfe story and rejecting a Robert Chase connection. The thread ended in scattered proposals rather than agreement or a settled reading.
  • Duncan Truter: Gideon Chase can only be Nyarlathotep among the Elder Gods; asks whether the book is broader Mythos
  • Adam Thornton: the novel is noir under a pulp surface, echoing Chandler; Chase resembles Nyarlathotep
  • Thomas Bitterman: Chase is Cthulhu; the second double is likely Shub-Niggurath's Black Ram, based on initials and Encyclopedia Cthulhiana
  • Dave Tallman: Randolph Carter is an alias for Jack the Ripper in Wolfe's Game in the Pope's Head, not Chase's father's link

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Myth and folklore sources

2008-10-12 Duncan Truter replying to Thomas Bitterman #

Argues that if the character GC is one of the Elder Gods, only Nyarlathotep fits, since Cthulhu sleeps for long periods and does not take other forms. Asks whether the book is a broader Mythos work or a Lovecraft-Cthulhu one, noting that the Elder Gods would be absent in the latter, and asks whether Wolfe has given recent interviews. Responds to Thomas Bitterman's post about Evil Guest.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: GC is Nyarlathotep

Just a quick note here. if GC is one of the elder gods, the only one it makes sense for him to be is Nyarlathotep. Cthulhu is only awake for brief periods, and never takes other forms as far as I know. Also, I wonder if this is a mythos book or a lovecraft-cthulhu book. if it's a lovecraft-cthulhu book then the elder gods aren't involved. Has Wolfe given any interviews recently?

Duncan

2008-10-12 Adam Thornton replying to Thomas Bitterman #

Suggests that Eyes of the Overworld (An Evil Guest) echoes the noir conversations in Chandler's The Big Sleep, citing Marlowe and Sternwood's orchid and spider lines, and that the novel may be noir despite its pulp surface. Also proposes that Gideon Chase resembles Nyarlathotep rather than Great Cthulhu, while the Haunter in the Dark and the Shark and Volcano gods are lesser earth gods.
proposes · source-and-allusion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Gideon Chase is Nyarlathotep not Great Cthulhu; Bill Reis and Shark God are lesser earth gods; An Evil Guest is noir in pulp clothing

For some reason, this reminded me of the conversation between Marlowe and Sternwood in _The Big Sleep_. You know, "Orchids remind me too much of dead men's flesh." Also, of course, "I exist largely on sleep, like a newborn spider."

I don't know *why* the conversation triggered that memory, but when reading Wolfe, often my hunches turn out to be at least sort of right. I think there's something to that, and something, perhaps, to the fact that the novel smells like pulp but might be noir.

As far as Gideon Chase being Great Cthulhu: the dude seems much more like Nyarlathotep to me. But...well, the Haunter In Darkness is the only one of that crowd with much actual interest in humanity (admittedly, the interest of a cat with a mouse). But I wonder if Bill Reis, and the Shark God, and the Volcano God, are mere Gods of Earth, while Gideon Chase and the Squid God are something much larger. Of course, if Gideon is driven off-world by the efforts of the U.S. Navy....hmm.

Adam

2008-10-12 Thomas Bitterman #

Asks why Klauser in the final conversation with Cassie talks about women's monthly needs and historic rags, and proposes a Lovecraftian reading of the characters' double identities, naming Gideon Chase as Cthulhu and offering ranked candidates for the second figure. Grounds the reading in the repeated initials of the male protagonists' names and entries from the Encyclopedia Cthulhiana, favouring Shub-Niggurath's Black Ram as the most likely identity.
proposes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Gideon Chase is the Great Cthulhu; Gideon Chase's second identity is Shub-Niggurath's Black Ram; Shub-Niggurath gets the US Navy to nuke Cthulhu

First, the question. In the final conversation, between Cassie and Klauser (which
really needs extended explanation), Cassie asks Klauser what to take on the trip
to Woldercan. He says, "Women have monthly needs. You could use rags, and our great-great-grandmothers did, but if you would prefer not to...". WTF? Who
talks like that? Why would an old man tell an old woman about menstruation and
the historic ways to deal with it?

Now, Cthulhuiana and spoilers (if I'm lucky). The two major male protagonists have
multiple identities (Wallace Rosenquist/William Reis and Gideon Chase/Gil Corby)
but the names keep the same initials. If someone can have two identities, they
can have more. Since it's a Lovecraftian book, let's look there.

The first one is easy. Gideon Chase is the Great Cthulhu.

The second one is tougher. I can't be sure, but I'll go in order of descending likelihood.
I would like to thank my copy of _Encyclopedia Cthulhiana_, which comes in handy
more often than you'd think.

1. "Black Ram with a Thousand Young". The male aspect of the Outer God Shub-Niggurath. My favorite for plot purposes.

2. "Wza-y'ei Rhan-Tegoth" The first word is the start of the chant that summons
Rhan-Tegoth, a Great Old One who came to Earth. It is said that the destruction of
Rhan-Tegoth would prevent the awakening of the Great Old Ones.

3. "Watery R'lyeh". Cthulhu's underwater city.

4. "Beyond Rigel". Rigel is a star that is associated with the Elder Gods, enemies (more or less) of the Great Old Ones.

I like 1 best because it turns the whole story into infighting between the bad guys with Shub-Niggurath getting the U. S. Navy to nuke Cthulhu. Cassie is just a pawn in this game and is going to get her just "reward" on Woldercan
from the Great C himself.

Enamel

2008-10-15 Dave Tallman replying to Thomas Bitterman #

Argues that Randolph Carter, the Lovecraft character from The Dream Quest of the Unknown Kadath, is an alias for Jack the Ripper in Wolfe's story "Game in the Pope's Head" in Starwater Strains. Responds to Greg Bates's suggestion that Gideon Chase's father is Robert Chase, the name tied to Randolph Carter, by pointing to the heavy Lovecraft references in that story.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Randolph Carter is an alias for Jack the Ripper

Randolph Carter and "The Dream Quest of the Unknown Kadath" are heavily referenced in Wolfe's story "Game in the Pope's Head" in "Starwater Strains." Carter seems to be an alias given to Jack the Ripper in that story, though interpretations may vary.
