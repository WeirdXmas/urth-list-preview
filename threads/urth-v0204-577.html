2002-04-10 Marc Aramini #

Muses that Wolfe's poetry introduction and Castle of Days essay reveal a sad, self-abnegating author, and asks whether Wolfe is a happy man. Also calls Fifth Head of Cerberus the hardest Wolfe novel and jokes that twelve Sun books spoil a Kabbalistic tree of eleven stations, replying to Blattid.
questions · author-and-biography · myth-and-structure

I was going to comment on the inclusion of Fifth Head of Cerberus in Mr. Gevers' fine article as well, Blattid, but then I decided against it. Since you brought it up, I am also sure it's not in the same "universe", but it does have some interesting thematic connections. And I think it is the "hardest" single Wolfe novel around. I just didn't get it the first time through - and you are spot on with the depressing aspect (oddly enough, I got the same feeling from Soldier of Arete, and a little from Urth of the New Sun, too.)
Too bad there are 12 novels in the Sun series (for sure): it screws up the 11 stations of the Kaballah tree! Damn you, Urth of the New Sun! Hey, a tree! The Briah cycle! The trees are inescapable. I'll shut up now.

Speaking of depressing:
And anybody read the introduction to Wolfe's poetry collection, "For Rosemary"? He really does seem sad most (or, a lot of)the time. His personal comments are a strange mixture of extreme self-abnegation coupled with an obvious pride that sneaks out every once in a while. Does he really believe what he writes won't get published? Sometimes he sounds so sad, other times he makes comments like: "everything I write is great". Remember that one essay in Castle of Days ( I think) where he talks about where he gets his inspiration from? Waking up in the middle of the night in a cold sweat and realizing that your parents and your friends are long dead, calling out in dreams to a mother who can no longer hold you ... > I have no training in psychology or anything, but sometimes I wonder if he is a happy guy.

Marc Aramini

2002-04-11 Matthew Malthouse #

Playfully counts the novels in Wolfe's Sun series against the Kabbalistic Tree of Life, noting that 5+4+3 makes twelve but that the tally breaks down under a different count. Argues that each multi-volume sequence (the New Sun, Urth, Long Sun, Short Sun) should be treated as one novel because of its continuous narrative, and contrasts this with Le Guin's Earthsea, whose books lack that contiguity.
disputes · genre-and-craft

[Advance warning, this is probably trivial nonsense ;-) ]

12.

5 + 4 + 3 which is neat.

Except that 4 + 1 + 4 + 3 ain't so.

But if we're counting, what's 'A Novel'? If asked I'd have said that TBotNS was a novel in four volumes, TUotNS in one, TBotLS in four and TBotSS in three.

So 1 + 1 + 1 + 1

One might argue (although I would not) that Severian and Silk = 5 + 7.

In each multi volume case there's a contiguity of narative that makes them single entities. Contrast with Le Guin's Earthsea which I'd say was three (now four) novels becasue although they all feature Ged, and in the first 'trilogy' he's the protagonist, they are not contiguous.

Matthew
