The thread: Readers discussed how Silk's enlightenment in The Book of the Long Sun is staged. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued its non-accusatory tone and Silk's later remark on free will as co-operating with fate fit a Catholic reading of grace. Alice Turner proposed that the scene of Silk struck during a game echoes John Bunyan's conversion in Grace Abounding, asking whether the parallel goes beyond coincidence. Endymion9 supported the portrayal by noting that Silk's inner conviction changes while his circumstances stay the same and that he does not try to persuade others. The thread ended with supportive posts and no recorded disagreement.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Silk's enlightenment is non-accusatory and fits a Catholic reading of grace and free will
  • Alice Turner: the struck-during-a-game scene echoes Bunyan's cat-game conversion, possibly deliberately
  • Endymion9: the enlightenment is convincing because inner conviction changes while outward circumstances stay the same

Themes: Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Free will and choice, Catholic faith and sacraments

2001-05-29 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Builds on Alga's remarks about Silk's enlightenment in The Book of the Long Sun, noting its non-accusatory tone and that Silk goes on to win his game. Links this to Silk's later remark that free will is the grace of co-operating with what will happen anyway, and to a Catholic reading of grace.

Alga:

This is cool. Whether or not it is an actual "reference," it's interesting to observe a few contrasts - aside from the non-accusatory nature of Silk's enlightenment, for example, the fact that Silk then continues and wins his game. I don't know if this is exactly a Catholic difference, but it does seem related to Silk's later observation -- from memory, my copy is at home -- to the effect that "perhaps that is what free will is: the grace of being permitted to co-operate with what is going to happen anyway." There is a kind of casualness to Silk's enlightenment, the sense that, okay, he's been enlightened, and now everything is different (for him), and yet everything is still the same...

--Blattid

2001-05-29 Alice Turner #

Proposes that Wolfe's scene of Silk struck during a game echoes John Bunyan's conversion in Grace Abounding, where a voice interrupts a game of cat. Quotes Bunyan's account of the Sunday game and defines tip-cat from the OED, asking whether the parallel is more than coincidence.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk's struck-in-game scene alludes to Bunyan's conversion

This is from -Grace Abounding-, the autobiography of John (-Pilgrim's Progress-) Bunyan. This happens on a Sunday: "as I was in the midst of a game of Cat, and having struck it one blow from the Hole, just as I was about to strike it the second time, a Voice did suddenly dart from Heaven into my Soul, which said, -Wilt thou leave thy sins and go to Heaven, or have thy sins and go to Hell?- At this I was put to an exceeding Maze. Wherefore, leaving my Cat upon the ground, I looked up to Heaven, and was as if I had, with the Eyes of my understanding, seen the Lord Jesus looking down on me, as being very hotly displeased with me, and as if he did severely threaten me with some grievous Punishment for these and other my ungodly Practices."

Bunyan is famously Protestant, of course, but very famous too. Leaving aside the accusatory nature of his revelation, doesn't it seem more than coincidence that Silk, too, should be struck just in the middle of a game? The game is called "tip-cat" according to the OED and is played thus: A small piece of wood tapering at each end, used in the game of tip-cat, etc.; it is hit at one end by the cat-stick, and made to spring from the ground, and then driven away by a side stroke. Bunyan is among the citations.

-alga

2001-05-30 Dennis G. Berdanis #

Argues that Silk's enlightenment in The Book of the Long Sun is convincingly portrayed because it changes his attitude and inner conviction while his outward circumstances stay the same, and that he does not try to persuade others. Grounds this in the poster's own experience and a quoted remark by Blattid, offering a personal analogy about lived spiritual conviction rather than argument.
supports · theology-and-religion

This seems very accurate from my experience. It shows me that Wolfe knows how *true* enlightment really affects people.

1. Silk is changed and knows he is changed. Althought externally nothing has really changes, it is very hard to shake his belief in what he knows has happened to him because as Christian author/speaker Leonard Ravenhill used to say, "You have your theories, but I've lived it, man!". Silk sees things differently now. It's his attitude that is affected.

2. Silk is not intent on explaning his enlightment to others or convincing them to see and experience his enlightment. Althought he can't help talking about it to others due to his excitement, his enlightment is for him and affects him. It affects others through his actions, but not through his argueing, debating or cajoling others to also accept it.

Dennis/Endy
