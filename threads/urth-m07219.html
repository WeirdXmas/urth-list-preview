RttW discrepancy?

Urth list · 5 messages · 2008-10-25 to 2008-10-28

2008urthThe Book of the Short SunThe Book of the Long SunthalassocratDave TallmanRoy C. LackeyMatthew Groves
The thread: Matthew Groves asked whether the Return to the Whorl opening, which describes a 'darkest night ever known,' conflicts with In Green's Jungles, where Horn and Silk leave the house around midday. Roy C. Lackey read the darkness as a figurative image of Silk's spiritual crisis, and pointed to hot sunshine through an open door in the same scene as proof. Dave Tallman offered two explanations, narrative compression or Silk's forgetting, and cited a genuine Long Sun blackout in a later section. Mr Thalassocrat weighed the textual evidence and came down in favor of compression. The exchange ended without a settled consensus, with Lackey still holding the figurative reading.
  • Matthew Groves: noticed a timing conflict between the two passages and wondered if it was just compressed narration
  • Roy C. Lackey: the 'darkest night' is metaphorical, Silk's soul-darkness, not an astronomical or literal blackout
  • Dave Tallman: either compression skips a lighted interlude or Silk forgot it; a real Long Sun blackout occurs later
  • Mr Thalassocrat: the text alone suggests the sun went out, but compression is the better explanation once In Green's Jungles is considered

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Light, darkness and colour

2008-10-25 Matthew Groves #

Asks whether the Return to the Whorl opening, where Hoof and company describe a man stumbling into the darkest night, conflicts with Horn/Silk's account in In Green's Jungles of leaving the house around midday. Quotes both passages to point out the apparent discrepancy and wonders if it is just compressed narration.
questions · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic

There is a possible discrepancy between HornSilk's description of his arrival in the Whorl in In Green's Jungles, and that by Hoof, Daisy, Hide, and Vadsig at the beginning of Return to the Whorl. In HornSilk's version, as he leaves the house, "It was a little after midday then, as well as I could judge from the narrowing line of the Long Sun." (*IGJ* 127) In Hoof et al., "He backed away from the knife and stumbled through the open door into the darkest night ever known." (*RttW* 13-14) Is this merely a compression of events by Hoof et al.?

Matt

2008-10-26 Roy C. Lackey replying to Matthew Groves #

Argues that the 'darkest night ever known' in Return to the Whorl refers to the dark night of Silk's soul rather than to any astronomical event involving the Long Sun. Responds to Matthew Groves's report of a timing discrepancy between Horn's arrival in In Green's Jungles and the opening of Return to the Whorl, offering a figurative reading instead of a chronological one.
disputes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: the darkest night refers to Silk's spiritual crisis

The "darkest night ever known" had nothing to do with the Long Sun. It was the dark night of Silk's soul.

-Roy

2008-10-27 Dave Tallman replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Proposes two explanations for a discrepancy in Return to the Whorl: that Matthew compresses the narrative so a lighted interlude is skipped, or that Silk forgot it when retelling events to Hoof, Hide, and Daisy. Answers Roy C. Lackey's claim that the darkest night is Silk's spiritual crisis, citing a real Long Sun blackout in the next section.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Matthew's narrative compression explains the discrepancy; Silk forgot the lighted interlude when retelling

That's plausible, but Silk does run into a genuine Long-Sun blackout in the next section of their narrative, so Matthew's narrative compression idea may be right. It could also be that Silk forget about the lighted interlude when he told Hoof, Hide, and Daisy about this later.

2008-10-28 Roy C. Lackey replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the phrase "darkest night ever known" in Return to the Whorl is a metaphor, not a literal blackout, answering Dave Tallman's suggestion that Silk's night corresponds to a genuine Long Sun blackout. Grounds this in the passage itself, noting that two inches above the quote the scene is lit by hot sunshine through an open door when Silk sees the knife.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: darkest night is metaphor for Silk's soul

The "darkest night ever known" bit is a metaphor. Two page inches above the quote mentions "the hot sunshine streaming through the open door into the poor little room." It was by that light that he saw the knife that he recoiled and backed away from and "stumbled through the open door into the darkest night ever known."

-Roy

2008-10-28 thalassocrat replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Return to the Whorl alone would suggest the sun went out as Silk left the manse, then prefers the compression explanation once the In Green's Jungles passage is considered. Responds to Roy C. Lackey's quoted discussion of Dave Tallman's reading of the "darkest night ever known" as Silk's soul-darkness versus a real Long Sun blackout. Weighs the textual discrepancy.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Matthew's narrative compression explains the sudden darkness

But if you looked only to RTTW surely you'd assume that the sun went out just as he was leaving the manse. Bright sunlight when he found himself in Silk, switching to dark as he left, groping blindly amongst trees in the next scene. Taking into account the IGJ passage, I like the compression explanation best. But maybe it's not that important.
