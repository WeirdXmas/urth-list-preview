The thread: Thread debated whether Horn's narration and the Council's behavior in The Book of the Long Sun point to deeper readings. Michael Andre-Driussi argued Horn is a Greek hero modeled on Odysseus and that Silk's identity is hinted at through the narrator's confusions. Patri10629 read the opening summons letter as lies possibly from the Inhumi and asked why the Leaders fear written history. Jim Jordan read the Book of Silk as a gospel with Silk as the nearest Christ figure, and later conceded that the Council sought to stop copies rather than destroy Horn's paper. Kevin J. Maroney disputed the suppression theory, arguing a paper thief is plausible and the Council would have destroyed Horn's personal copy instead. The thread ended with the paper-theft question partly settled, while the Christ-figure and Silk-identity readings were left open.

Themes: Unreliable narration, Christ figures and messiahs, Stories within stories

1999-10-03 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Horn in The Book of the Long Sun is a Greek hero modeled mostly on Odysseus, and that the Long Sun's inset tale and the narrator Horn's confusions point to Silk's identity. Supports these claims with comparisons to Prester John's letter, Don Quixote, the Arabian Nights, and Vance's science fiction, citing specific details like the one-eyed narrator and the twins' names.
proposes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Horn is a Greek hero modeled on Odysseus; Horn the writer is Silk in some way

Well, what a novel!

My first impressions:

Horn is a Greek hero, mostly Odyesseus. How wonderful! I suppose I might have anticipated this, with all the Greco-Roman grounding of the Long Sun, but that series had a gloss of Father Brown modernism. Visiting the witch; insisting upon hearing the song of the siren; feeding cow blood to "the dead" for tricks and treats. Taking an eye from an ancient woman, that's Perseus.

The letter of summons to the lander reminds me quite strongly of the Letter from Prestor John to the Pope. And as in that case, there is much speculation on where the kingdom is located--it has =moved= over the centuries!

(But in the beginning it was thought to be on the . . . Silk Road!)

The inclusion/intrusion of TBOTLS within the tale reminds me strongly of the second part of DON QUIXOTE, where similar things happen. Rather than THE 1001 ARABIAN NIGHTS, where there is tale-within-tale-within-tale, but nobody ever says, hey, I read about you in ARABIAN NIGHTS, or hey, buy my book.

I, too, felt a very Vancean current to ON BLUE'S WATERS; and I, too, felt that it was a different one than the DYING EARTH . . . but what, then? Perhaps it is the more "paranoid" Vancean science fiction, like the Durdane trilogy (which has secret alien invaders and a bad ride in starship to alien world where humans end up as slaves) mixed with a bit of NOPALGARTH (where invisible aliens ride humans like psionic vampires, iirc). I think it is something more than just the fairytale "hero sets out on seemingly doomed quest" thing, which Vance often uses as well; it is something more Vancean than just fairytale logic or plot.

Ah, the Horn who is writing (aka "the Rajan of Gaon") is one eyed and white haired, like Odin. But he also has a hurt ankle, is sometimes confused with Silk by others (just because he's wearing the clothes, walking the walk, talking the talk <g>), often confuses the names of his own twins ("Horn [sic] and Hide") as well as writing of Horn in the third person, and even, in the very end, says that =he= caught the ball (at the beginning of TBOTLS)! He =is= Silk, in some as-yet-unspecified way. Not to mention the intriguing "Passilk"!

(An interesting aside: the twins Hoof and Hide have names that form a semi-canting phrase meaning "run away and conceal yourself"!)

Must read again before too long.

1999-10-04 Patrick O'Leary #

Argues that the opening letter of summons in Nightside the Long Sun is a set of lies whose speaker may be the Inhumi, and questions why the Leaders of the Community want Silk and why they fear the writing of history. Grounds the reading in the letter's resemblance to the Prester John letter, the awkward meeting with the Leaders, and the theft of Horn's paper.
proposes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: The summons letter is written by the Inhumi through human agents

<<
The letter of summons to the lander reminds me quite strongly of the Letter from Prestor John to the Pope. And as in that case, there is much speculation on where the kingdom is located--it has =moved= over the centuries! >>

It struck me on rereading the opening letter that it is a tissue of lies. But we know that. Imagine it is the words of the Inhumi--throwing their voices, as it were, through their human agents-- and it becomes an entirely different message altogether. Very creepy.

It also struck me that the initial meeting with the Leaders of the Community was a bit awkward. A clumsy plot device to get the quest rolling. "We need a a god--get Silk. We need better seed--back to the whorl. Our businesses are failing, our crops, too--our society is disintegrating, decadent--we need a moral center that everyone can agree on. We need Silk. We need 'fresh blood.'"

But then there was that unspoken agenda which Nettle intuited--"They don't want us to write anymore." Not sure I get that. We're the Powers that be--secular and sacred: Business, Church, Law (not sure about that one)--conspiring to consolidate their earthly power by removing their one true legitimate rival--the man who wrote their history? Whose only authority was the fact that he tried to tell the truth? And had the means to do so--paper? Or are one or more of this group Inhumi collaborators?

Interesting that Horn's most valuable commodity--paper--is stolen almost immediately. He must rely on other resources.

How like TBOTLS--short-sighted agendas which ultimately are futile. As Horn says from the begining--he failed.

Some of many questions. Amazing that this fragment, this first installment can be so powerful and so incomplete.

best

Patrick O'Leary

1999-10-12 James Jordan #

Argues that the Book of Silk in The Book of the Long Sun works as a gospel, following the gospel narrative closely, with Silk as the nearest Christ figure Wolfe has written. Grounds this in comparison to the early Christian reaction against the apostles in Acts and in the community's hostility toward Horn's writing, replying to Patrick's point about the leaders not wanting Horn to write more.
supports · allegory-and-typology · source-and-allusion · theories: Silk is the closest Christ figure in Wolfe; The Book of Silk follows the gospel narrative

The book of Silk is the Gospel of Silk. Remember that the narrative follows the gospel narrative rather closely. Silk is the closest to a Christ-figure that Wolfe has ever written. Well, did the conservative Jews in the book of Acts like it when the apostles preached about Jesus? Did the Roman Emperors? No.

Yes, and WHO stole it? Surely not the person Horn actually suspected. As you point out, the leaders of the community don't want any more gospels from Horn.

Nutria

1999-10-12 Kevin J. Maroney #

Disputes the idea that the Council stole Horn's paper to stop him publishing more copies of The Book of Silk, arguing that paper's value makes any thief a plausible suspect. Reasons that if suppression were the goal, the Council would have destroyed Horn's personal copy of the book instead, responding to Nutria's post quoting Patrick.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic

I don't think it's necessary to assume that the Council (as we might as well call them) stole Horn's paper to stop him from publishing more copies of _The Book of Silk_. Paper is a valuable commodity--Horn is doing quite well for himself--and would be an obvious target for any thief. If they were really interested in supressing Horn, they'd have taken and destroyed his personal copy of _The Book_.
