The thread: Readers debated whether Horn's dream-travel in the Long Sun and Short Sun books is literal time travel and how Incanto's changes to the stories told in In Green's Jungles work. James Wynn and Marc Aramini argued for time travel, citing Pike's ghost, Oreb's astral flight, and a Bill and Ted comparison, while António Pedro Marques pressed that time travel cannot explain the other stories or characters noticing manipulation. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Daniel Petersen moved toward a textual or postmodern reading in which the phenomena exist only within the books, and Petersen also tested whether a supernatural reading of Pike's ghost was excluded. The thread drifted into a dispute over consensus and into a Protestant-authors aside, and ended without agreement, with Gerry Quinn deferring the time-travel question until rereading.
  • James Wynn: dream-travel is time travel, supported by Pike's ghost, astral Oreb and mismatched timelines
  • Marc Aramini: Horn's abilities work like Bill and Ted time travel, and facts do not depend on consensus
  • António Pedro Marques: time travel does not explain the other stories or characters noticing manipulation; definiteness needs consensus
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the phenomena exist only in the text, and the memory dilemma arises from textual change
  • Daniel Petersen: supernatural and textual readings can coexist, and Horn's dream-stuff bodies are more complicated than disembodiment
  • Gerry Quinn: will defer the time-travel reading until rereading Short Sun

Themes: Time loops and causality, Memory and forgetting, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2011-10-26 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to António Pedro Marques #

Argues that Incanto's dream-travel in The Book of the Long Sun creates a dilemma about memory and time travel, since changed pasts leave characters remembering both versions. Responds to António Pedro Marques's claim that storytellers could not detect dream-travel changes, framing the problem as something only a text, specifically postmodern SF, can produce.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe deliberately poses a dilemma about memory and time travel

Ummmmm.

It seems to me that Wolfe is deliberately posing a dilemma about memory (and time-travel) with Incanto's dream-travel. When he changes something in someone's past, they remember it both the "new" way and the "old" way. Now, if he "really" changed the past, they should remember it "only" the "new" way -- the "old" way now always never happened. But if he doesn't, then what is he changing? "Only" the memories of the person telling the story? Yet, the changes seem to have "real" consequences; and, again, if he really _changed_ their memories, they should not remember the pre-change version. Or should they?

I believe that the point here is that this is something that can _only_ happen in a text...and a text of a particular kind (Postmodern SF) at that.

2011-10-26 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Daniel Petersen #

Argues from a Catholic view that all phenomena, including the world, are textual, so the wild phenomena in Wolfe's stories are wholly textual rather than extra-textual. Responds to Daniel Petersen's remark about an open door to extra-textual reality by asking whether a story told inside an unreliable narrator's story is itself reliable.
proposes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolfe's stories have radically textual internal reality

Remember that for us (I too am an adult convert to Catholicism, among other things) the wor(l)d was spoken -- is in itself a kind of text. The Word of God was made flesh, and indeed has its point-of-origin in some sense in the flesh; it is always already _incarnate_ (i.e., not merely spoken but written). The spoken word of God is the Act of Creation: Let there be <foo> and there was <foo> .

What I am saying is that there _are_ no extra-textual phenomena: all phenomena are in some sense textual. I think that to understand what Wolfe is doing here we must understand not only the textuality of the text but the _radical_ textuality of the "internal" reality of his stories.

Also, Wolfe messes with unreliable narrators. Now, when a narrator inside a story told by an unreliable narrator tells a story, is that story (and that narrator) reliable? Why or why not? What _is_ there for us to interpret, but the text and its intertextuality?
