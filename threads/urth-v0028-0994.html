Wolfe and Crowley, ancient and Renaissance

Urth list · 3 messages · 2000-05-09 to 2000-05-12

2000urthAdam StephanidesJames Jordan
The thread: Adam Stephanides argued that Wolfe draws heavily on classical Greco-Roman material while largely ignoring medieval and Renaissance Europe, unlike Crowley, and pointed to a clash between classical and Renaissance magic in Lucky Bastard via Smoky and Ariel Hawksquill. Jim Jordan questioned what Stephanides meant by his phrase about Renaissance magic and the classics, and used the fairies' supposed descent from Kronos to press a point about Smoky's learning and the Drinkwaters' fate, as well as a hierarchy of worlds above the House. Stephanides replied that he had loosely equated Renaissance magic with belief in the fairies, that Smoky and Hawksquill are both classically trained though the link is unworked, and that the fairies are children of the gods rather than grandchildren of Kronos. The thread ends with the question left open rather than resolved.

2000-05-09 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that Wolfe largely ignores medieval and Renaissance Europe as a source of allusion, unlike Crowley, while drawing heavily on classical Greco-Roman material in the Soldier books, the Long Sun gods, There Are Doors and the Byzantine-modeled New Sun setting. Grounds this in recalled passages such as the witch's conjurations in There Are Doors and Gold's fake Necronomicon in Peace, and notes a conflict between classical and Renaissance magic in Lucky Bastard's Smoky and Ariel Hawksquill.
proposes · source-and-allusion

The Tarot discussion got me wondering whether Wolfe ever alludes to the Tarot. I couldn't recall his doing so at all. Then it occurred to me that not only does Wolfe ignore completely, iirc, the whole field of Renaissance magic which so fascinates Crowley; aside from the anomaly of THE DEVIL IN A FOREST, he pretty much ignores the whole of medieval and Renaissance Europe as a source of allusion or intellectual background (I would tend to see the Arthurian backdrop of CASTLEVIEW as a reference to myth in general, rather than medieval Europe in particular, and the same with the Irish legends in PEACE). This strikes me as being unusual for a self-confessed Catholic author and admirer of Chesterton. On the other hand, the classical Greco-Roman world is all over the place: the SOLDIER books, obviously, but also the pseudo-classical gods and goddesses in BOTLS and THERE ARE DOORS; the setting of BOTNS is modeled after the Byzantine Empire, which was contemporaneous with medieval Europe, but which had much less of a break with the ancient world; even the conjurations of the witch in THERE ARE DOORS and in Gold's fake Necronomicon in PEACE seemed to me closer to ancient Greco-Roman magic than to medieval or Renaissance magic, iirc. Of course, it's been a long time since I read most of these, and I don't have copies close at hand, so I could be completely wrong.

On the other hand, while Crowley is fascinated by Renaissance magic, he doesn't ignore the classical world. In fact, the conflict between the two is a minor subtheme of LB: both Smoky and (or so it seems to me) Ariel Hawksquill have minds molded by the classics, though Ariel is of course aware of the Renaissance tradition; and both are made to unwillingly recognize the truth of the Renaissance magic worldview.

--Adam

Asks what the replier meant by a phrase about Renaissance magic and the classics, and argues that if the fairies descend from Kronos, Smoky's classical learning and love of stars point against the fate of the Drinkwaters. Builds on the claim that a hierarchy of worlds exists above the House, which would revise Mantis's scheme of worlds in the Crowley discussion.

Wondering a bit what you meant by that last phrase.

As there is a hierarchy of worlds/spheres here, it seems that there is a world larger than the one in which the House is situated. 2000-05-12 Adam Stephanides #

Clarifies that the poster loosely equated the Renaissance magic worldview with belief in the fairies' existence, and notes that Smoky and Ariel Hawksquill are both classically trained, though the exact link is unworked out. Responds to Nutria's question about the phrase and about the fairies being grandchildren of Kronos, suggesting they are children of the gods instead.
answers · close-textual-reading · theories: Fairies are children of the gods

I guess I didn't mean anything very precise; I was conflating "the Renaissance magic worldview" with belief in the existence and importance of the fairies. Ariel may or may not be aware of the fairies' existence at the start, but until almost the end, she regards them and their "small worlds" as a sideshow compared to Eigenblick's activities. I still think it's significant that both Smoky and Ariel are classically trained, but I'd probably have to reread the whole book to figure out how.

Now I'm not sure what you mean. If the fairies are grandchildren of Kronos, then they are probably children of the gods.

--Adam
