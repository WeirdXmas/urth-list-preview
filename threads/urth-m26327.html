The thread: Readers debated the Judaism that Latro's Sophist and priests represent, a thread that drifted from Wolfe's novel into New Testament matters. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Frederick Hudson Norwood noted that the one anti-gay statement in the New Testament comes from Paul, not Jesus, and joked about how many wives the Sanhedrin had. Lee argued that Jesus aimed to restore lost purity and divide observant Jews from his followers, citing Luke 12:51 and Matthew 10:34 and contrasting Old Testament law with turning the other cheek. António Pedro Marques countered that the Judaism was not a harsh caricature, that Jesus's proportionate-response teaching was not a sharp break from common Jewish practice, and that the Sanhedrin was not uniformly Sadducean. The thread ended without agreement and with the Latro text largely set aside.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Jesus attacked priestly hypocrisy rather than Judaism itself; anti-gay statement in the New Testament comes from Paul
  • Lee: Jesus sought to restore lost purity and divide observant Jews from followers, citing the sword and division verses and the Sanhedrin's hostility
  • Norwood, Frederick Hudson: The one anti-gay New Testament statement is Paul's, not Jesus's; jokes about the priests' wives
  • António Pedro Marques: Latro's Judaism should not be caricatured as harsh; proportionate response was a divine injunction, not a break from Jewish practice; San

2015-04-24 Lee Berman #

Clarifies that the earlier claim concerned the Judaism administered by Judea's priestly class, and argues Jesus' words imply he aimed to restore lost purity and to divide observant Jews from his followers. Grounds this in the Sanhedrin's hostility and in Luke 12:51 and Matthew 10:34 on the sword and division, and contrasts Old Testament law with the New Testament ethic of turning the other cheek.

I won't take it as argument but as a point of discussion. It wasn't the main point of my post, so

By which I meant the practice of Judaism as administered by the priestly class of Judea. The

Sanhedrin made it clear they weren't happy with Jesus' ministry and considered it a threat. Even

today, there are certain Christians AND Jews who deny Jesus was jewish.

I think there were times when the words of Jesus imply he considered his goal to be bringing

back a lost purity to Judaism. Though the Old Testament consistently implies that the Hebrews were

a constant disappointment to God. (small wonder that guilt is an important part of being jewish ;- ))

But other times Jesus seemed to acknowledge that he was bringing something new to the table

(even if fulfilling ancient prophecy). For some reason Luke 12:51 and Matthew 10-34 come to mind,

where Jesus notes that he has not come to bring peace but rather division and a sword- setting family

members against each other, etc.

I take this as a, if not transparent, then translucent way of Jesus saying that he expected his ministry

to create divisions between the jews who were doing things the current way vs. those planning to follow

Jesus and how he expected things to be done differently in the future.

What changes were expected? No more "eye for an eye, tooth for a tooth" but rather "turn the other cheek"

to use the oft-cited example. There are quite a few homophobic statements, acceptance of polygamy,

glorification of war, use of maiming and dismemberment as legal punshments, etc. in the Old Testament,

but not in the New Testament.

So I have to wonder how many wives those Judean priests had......

2015-04-24 António Pedro Marques replying to Lee #

Argues that the Judaism Latro's Sophist and priests represent should not be caricatured as harsh, since Jesus's teaching on proportionate response was an injunction from God and not a sharp break from the common Jewish practice. Responds to Lee's claim that Jesus opposed the Sadducees and that ordinary Jews were not condemned, adding that the Sanhedrin was not uniformly Sadducean. The post draws on Gospel verses and a Nietzsche aside rather than on the Latro text itself.
