The thread: The thread asked why astral travel and the godlings bother some readers in Wolfe's books. maa32 argued these elements are no less explicable than mental projection and that the sorcerer in Sword of the Lictor is a psionicist, citing psionic rules and Severian's and Silk's powers. The thread ends with the positions still apart rather than resolved.

2001-04-18 Marc Aramini #

Argues that astral travel, time-corridor access and godlings in Wolfe's books are no less explicable than mental projection, and that the sorcerer in Sword of the Lictor is a psionicist. Cites Severian's bond with the new sun, Silk's resurrection in Calde of the Long Sun, and psionic rules in A.D. + D. as grounds.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe's psionic sorcerer rather than magic; Godlings may be mechanical, which doesn't matter

I really don't see why some are so distraught over Wolfe's use of a really big Godling or astral travel. Severian's relationship with the new sun seems equally mysterious. How can a man freely travel back and forth in time just because he has some affinity with a star? without mechanical "SF" enhancement, how does Wolfe really explain Severian's connection? How about silk's auto-resurrection at the end of Calde of the Long Sun? I really don't think that access to the corridor of time is any different than travelling through space with a mental projection. The sorceror in Sword of the Lictor is clearly a psionicist. Is his mental fortitude any different than magic or astral projection? Does a device or genetic alteration allow him to use his mental powers? I don't think so. According to A.D. + D., astral projection is a well established psionic ability, as is conjunction with another mind. The godlings might very well be mechanical. who cares?

2001-04-18 William H. Ansley #

Admits the poster once objected to the godlings' size and astral projection as unscientific but now concedes that objection was silly, while still disliking those elements in The Book of the Short Sun. Says the problem is likely the book's explicit religiosity, given that the poster is areligious and finds little to enjoy in it.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: The Book of the Short Sun's religiosity is why the poster dislikes it

I am one of those who complained about the "impossible" size of the godlings as not being "science fictional." You know what? You are right. This was a silly objection on my part, considering all of the stuff I have swallowed without complaint in other so-called SF. (Time travel, FTL drives and "Psi powers" are just the beginning.)

This however doesn't mean that I don't still object to the godlings' size or the astral projection. Neither of these things added anything essential to the story as far as I was concerned and both created confusion and loose ends (the godlings a little and astral projection a lot).

Now, I can only assume that both of these parts of the story are essential, since Wolfe wrote it and he is very careful about such things, and I am just missing the point. But I am not at all sure I am willing to give this series years as some have recommended. I wouldn't have given TBotNS or Peace years of rereading if there wasn't some aspect of these books I enjoyed from the start. I am hard pressed to find this aspect in TBotSS.

I am becoming convinced that my problem with TBotSS is that it is Wolfe's most explicitly religious (long) work and I am an areligious person. If this is so, I don't think I am ever going to care for it more than I do now.

William Ansley

2001-04-19 Michael Straight #

Argues that the godlings' impossible size in Wolfe's Long Sun books is never explained physically, unlike the sea-bound constraints set out in The Book of the New Sun, and that Typhon's technological wonders contrast with the miraculous powers of Severian and Silk. Grounds this on the New Sun discussion of size and collapse, and tests the Urth-era talus plant and the organic Pig as possible explanations.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft

I thought the main objection to the godlings is that in Act 1 (New Sun) the author goes into detail about why anything that big would have to live in the sea or collapse under its own weight and then in Act 3 he presents us with such a creature without giving us any hint of how it is possible, or even a sense that we're supposed to see this as a physics-defying miracle (and Typhon/Mainframe's wonders seem to be otherwise technological, unlike the "miraculous" wonders of Severian or Silkhorn's astral travel).

(Maybe our hint is in Act 2 at the talus construction plant, but then what do you do with the obvious hints that organic Pig (he eats, his eyes are flesh) is a godling?)

-Rostrum
