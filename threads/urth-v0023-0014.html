The Wolfe Library

Urth list · 4 messages · 1999-01-25 to 1999-01-26

1999urthThe Book of the New SunC R CulverOl' PappyMichael Andre-DriussiAlex David Groce
The thread: Readers proposed a reference list for a Wolfe library, asking what authority an 'essential' list could claim and nominating books such as Borges's Fictions, Nabokov's Pale Fire, Scholem's Kabbalah, Vance, Crowley, Le Guin, Kipling, Poe and Graves. CRCulver argued Borges's echoes in Peace and The Book of the New Sun justified his inclusion, while m.driussi noted that the only Vance title Wolfe has publicly named is The Dying Earth. Alex David Groce grounded picks in Wolfe's own remarks about his reading, such as calling Little, Big an education and disliking The Dispossessed. The thread stayed friendly and list-building, ending with suggestions rather than any settled canon.
  • Fantasma: an essential list needs an authority to approve it, half-joking about a committee
  • CRCulver: Borges's Collected Fictions belongs on the list because of echoes in Peace and The Book of the New Sun
  • m.driussi: Wolfe has publicly named only The Dying Earth among Vance's works
  • Alex David Groce: pick books Wolfe has said he read, pairing Pale Fire with Peace

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Stories within stories, Myth and folklore sources

1999-01-25 Ol' Pappy #

Proposes a list of essential reference and influential books for understanding Wolfe, naming Borges, Vance, Chesterton, Nabokov and Budrys along with dictionaries, encyclopedias and a saints' lives. Asks the list what authority such an essential library could have and jokingly nominates the poster as chairman of an approval committee.
proposes · source-and-allusion

Not to rudely change subject, but since we're in the constructive mood, a question.

I'm new to the list, and though I've reviewed the archives I don't think that this question has been raised, which frankly stuns me. To wit:

What is the Essential Gene Wolfe Library?

I realize that this is as much about authors (and readers, Wolfe is quick to note) as books, but I'm interested in what people think about this and what level of authority is possible. From my limited experience and perception, and straight from Wolfe's own mouth (or letter-writing pen), I could think of five essentials, including other influential books by the same author--

The Book of Imaginary Beings, Jorge Luis Borges (Ficciones, The Aleph and Other Stories)
The Dying Earth, Jack Vance
The Everlasting Man, G. K. Chesterton (The Man Who Was Friday, Autobiography, Father Brown et al)
Pale Fire, Vladimir Nabokov
Michaelmas, Algis Budrys (Rogue Moon, Who?)

This is all in addition to the reference section of our Library. The Compact OED, principally, which has been repeatedly substituted for the minds and careers of the people on this list (just playin', guys, just playin'). A good encyclopedia (my favorite is the rare 11th edition of the Encyclopedia Brittanica, 1910-1911). A good "Lives of the Saints" (the Penguin dictionary is probably the most accurate). Mrs. Byrne's dictionary. *The New York Times' Guide to Misspelled, etc*. I'm sure that people could supplement this with resources outside of language, which is the only thing I am fairly familiar with.

If people really think this is as interesting to establish as I do, then there should be an illustrious, enigmatic approval committee, in which case I hesitantly nominate myself as Chairman. No, really, you're too kind.

-A Slightly Apprehensive Fantasma

1999-01-25 C R Culver #

Proposes adding Borges's The Collected Fictions to the library list because parts of Peace and The Book of the New Sun echo Ficciones and The Aleph, and suggests Scholem's Kabbalah for the multi-universe structure. Replies to Phantasma's mention of Pale Fire, which the poster likens to Borges and Wolfe, and calls the existing library adequate.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Borges's Ficciones influenced parts of Peace and The Book of the New Sun; Kabbalah explains the two-plus universe structure

<Pale Fire, Vladimir Nabokov>

A ha! I've been waiting for someone to mention PF, as I've always thought it similar to Borges who is similar to Wolfe. (Note: it may have been discussed before, but so far back that I had not yet read the book and ignored it all, let me check the archives).

As for the library, I think what you have suffices, though _The Collected Fictions of J.L.B._ could be added to the BoIB, because there are parts of Peace and the Book of the New Sun which are influed by _Ficciones_ and _The Aleph and Other Stories_, not just the BoIB.

For the more esoteric and nutty of us, myself included, you could throw in Gershom Scholem's book "The Kaballah," to see from where the two+ universe structure comes.

Cheers,

1999-01-26 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Recalls the white-fountain vision in Nabokov's Pale Fire and admits the poster's Lexicon Urthus might not have been written had they read it first, then turns to the Essential Wolfe Library and a list of books Wolfe has named. Says the only public title Wolfe has named among Vance's works is The Dying Earth, based on the poster's correspondence with him.
informs · author-and-biography

Lest we forget, PALE FIRE contains a haunting (near-death?) vision of a white fountain . . . and when the artist at last meets another person who has had this vision, he warms up with wonder that another human being has seen it, the fountain is somehow a real thing, and then she murmers that it is like Mont Blanc, which seems like a non sequitur, until she says ah, the white mountain! And within him the ediface of a shared mystical experience comes crashing down.

IIRC.

If I'd read PALE FIRE before I'd written the Lexicon, I probably wouldn't have written the Lexicon at all--the horrible humor of seeing something beautiful (in that case it was poetry) butchered by a highly-inventive-yet-obviously-wrong reading. Well! Ignorance is Strength.

Re: the Essential Wolfe Library. Hard to say--boiled down to five or six titles sounds more like the "quintessential" Wolfe Library! Have to include the Bible, clearly. Here's another application where what would come in handy would be a list of every book he has ever mentioned in interviews (so often they ask him about influencial authors and books!) and in articles. (I've got the start of such a list somewhere around, which is why I keep bringing it up, I suppose. Half-started lists, like a splinter in a hand.)

That Vance entry looks to be dead-on: in talking to him and corresponding with him I get the impression that Gene Wolfe has read a lot of Vance, and what we know of his reading habits (re: Chesterton, for example) we can assume he read everything at one point, but the only title he has ever mentioned in public is that one: THE DYING EARTH.

=mantis=

1999-01-26 Alex David Groce #

Proposes a list of books for a Wolfe library, pairing Nabokov's Pale Fire with Peace as two sides of one coin and suggesting Crowley, Le Guin, Kipling, Poe, Graves and Rex Stout titles. Grounds the choices in what Wolfe has said about his reading, such as calling Little, Big an education in modern fantasy and disliking The Dispossessed, and notes Claudius books as influences on The Book of the New Sun.
proposes · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion · theories: Peace and Pale Fire as two sides of one coin

Also, although I doubt it influenced PEACE (since Wolfe has said he came to read Nabokov only later), I think of PEACE and PALE FIRE as two sides of one coin--Weer turns his story into a story that is about everyone else, while Kinbote turns another man's story into his own.

Other titles for the library:

Crowley's LITTLE, BIG (I've never seen Wolfe mention the AEGYPT or LOVE & SLEEP, but he called LITTLE, BIG "an education in modern fantasy") Le Guin's THE BEGINNING PLACE (and definitely not THE DISPOSSESED, which Wolfe disliked--especially as I share his opinion...)
Kipling's THE JUNGLE BOOK (1&2), COLLECTED POETRY
Poe's short stories
Robert Grave's I, CLAUDIUS and CLAUDIUS THE GOD (highly important, given all the obvious influences on BOTNS)

Perhaps also some representative Nero Wolfe mystery, as Wolfe has said those are his favorite detective stories.
