Vancean influence on Wolfe

Urth list · 12 messages · 2002-05-20 to 2002-05-20

2002urthThe Book of the New SunJerry FriedmanAdam StephanidesAndy RobertsonJason VoegeleDan'l Danehy-OakesMichael Andre-DriussiJames Jordan
The thread: Readers debated whether Jack Vance's The Dying Earth has a Dunsany-like earnest tone and how its irony and humour compare with Wolfe's work. Michael Andre-Driussi and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that Vance's irony is gentle rather than vicious and that the Cugel books are more picaresque than Monty Python-like, while Jerry Friedman found the episodic Cugel books similar to The Book of the New Sun. James Jordan proposed a Mozart-to-Bach contrast between Vance and Wolfe, which Jason Voegele questioned and Jordan explained as stylistic rather than a quality judgment. Adam Stephanides then asked about Andre-Driussi's claims on Vance's pseudonymous Ellery Queen mysteries, and the thread ended without resolution.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: The Dying Earth has a earnest, Dunsany-like tone and Vancean irony is gentle, not vicious
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Agrees with the Dunsany influence and sees Vance's irony as strong but not vicious
  • Jerry Friedman: Vance's Cugel books resemble The Book of the New Sun in their unsympathetic protagonist and episodic structure
  • James Jordan: Vance is a Mozart to Wolfe's Bach stylistically, not a quality judgment
  • Jason Voegele: Asks whether the Mozart-Bach comparison implies a quality judgment and suggests Wolfe's fugal prose
  • Adam Stephanides: Questions Andre-Driussi's claims about Vance's pseudonymous Ellery Queen works

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Humour in Wolfe

2002-05-20 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Jack Vance's The Dying Earth differs in tone from his other Dying Earth novels, with an earnest quality akin to sombre-mode Dunsany that the later books burlesque. Points readers to Wolfe's essay 'The Living Earth' on Vance and agrees with Andy Robertson, offering the poster's wide reading of Vance as grounds.
supports · genre-and-craft

I would not argue too strenously with you. If you want to see Gene Wolfe's take on TDE, see his essay "The Living Earth" in JACK VANCE: CRITICAL APPRECIATIONS AND A BIBLIOGRAPHY.

What Andy Robertson said. In addition, I think it is important to isolate TDE from the other Vancean "Dying Earth" novels: TDE has an "earnest" quality, maybe something like sombre-mode Dunsany, that is burlesqued in the other novels in a way closer to that of joking-mode Clark Ashton Smith (as opposed to decadent-mode, which is the voice CAS used in most of his Zothique stories).

The isolation of TDE could be extended further, to separate it from nearly all of Vance's work--it really is different. But I've read nearly 100% of Vance's fiction (I haven't read a few of the mysteries because I cannot afford them--oh yes, Vance wrote mysteries, too. Even won an award.) and I may be forgetting more than that.

=mantis=

2002-05-20 Jerry Friedman #

Argues that Vance's Dying Earth novels, especially the Cugel books, resemble The Book of the New Sun in featuring an unsympathetic protagonist moving through pointless episodes, and compares them to Pratchett's Rincewind novels without the jokes. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's point about Dunsany's influence on The Dying Earth by stating the poster's own reaction to the series' episodic structure and the plot question that keeps him reading.
supports · genre-and-craft

I feel about them the way people who dislike BotNS feel about *it*: a thoroughly unsympathetic character goes through one pointless episode after another. There was enough invention (I certainly can't criticize Vance on that score!), and of course the questions of whether Cugel and Rhialto would ultimately succeed, to keep me reading.

It's sort of like BotNS without the aspects I like, or Pratchett's Rincewind novels without the jokes (though I think admirers of Vance find the later three Dying Earth novels funny).

Jerry Friedman

2002-05-20 James Jordan #

Explains the Mozart-Bach comparison as a stylistic contrast: Mozart is transparent, sunny and straightforward while Bach is dense, serious and complex, and insists it is not a quality judgment. Responds to Jason Voegele's question about Nutria's earlier remark that Vance is a Mozart to Wolfe's Bach, and says it works for them while admitting it may not suit everyone.
answers · genre-and-craft

'Twas just a thought. Mozart is transparent compared with Bach's density. Mozart is more sunny; Bach more serious. Mozart more straightforward; Bach more complex.
The comparison works for me and you; maybe not for everyone here. Not a quality judgment. Two different geniuses. I love Vance. I'm a subscriber to the Compleat Vance.

Nutria

Yes, but - TDE really is one of those original, once-of-a-kind, novels.   It
seems imitative now only because it has been imitated so often.

For example, there is the matter of Role Playing Games.

The entire treatment of magic in RPGs derives from THE DYING EARTH - the
basic idea that a magician may compress just so many spells into his mind,
and no more.   This was worked out in the original Dungeons & Dragons games
and its influence *since* then has spread everywhere, to fantasy films, to
horror, and of course back into writing.

RPGs are low, of course, but they can be fun  :-)
