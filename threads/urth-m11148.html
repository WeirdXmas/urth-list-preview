Seawrack's name

Urth list · 4 messages · 2010-08-28 to 2010-08-30

2010urthThe Book of the Short SunAntónio Pedro MarquesLee Berman
The thread: Lee Berman argued that Seawrack was a pun on shipwreck rather than a corruption of Scylla, pointing to the silent-c pronunciation of Scylla and the sirens' role in wrecking ships. Entonio disputed this, finding Berman's claim that Horn hears an older source illogical and arguing Horn would have no need to convert Scylla into Seawrack. António Pedro Marques questioned why 'shipwreck' would become 'seawrack', calling the link real but not polished. A later post by Marques noted the narrator's admission that he had not thought of Seawrack as a siren, read as a sign of unreliability. The thread ended without agreement, with the derivation still disputed.
  • Lee Berman: Seawrack is a pun on shipwreck, not a corruption of Scylla
  • entonio: Silent-c Scylla sounds like shipwreck, but Horn would not need to rename Scylla as Seawrack
  • António Pedro Marques: The shipwreck link exists but is not a polished derivation
  • António Pedro Marques: The narrator's denial of Seawrack as siren may signal unreliability

Themes: Names and their meanings, Unreliable narration, Myth and folklore sources

2010-08-28 Lee Berman #

Argues that the name Seawrack was likely chosen as a pun on shipwreck rather than as a corruption of Scylla, since Horn would know the name Scylla. Grounds this in the English pronunciation of Scylla with a silent c and in the mythic sirens' role of causing shipwrecks, citing quotes about Seawrack's purpose.

I hope I am not being insulting in noting that the English pronunciation of Scylla usually involves a silent "c" like scissors or scintillating. This would bring Scylla's pronunciation even closer to Seawrack.

But Horn is very familiar with the female name "Scylla" from his religious studies and I can't see how he could hear that name and feel the need to convert it to "seawrack"

Notes that the narrator says he had not thought of Seawrack as a siren until someone else called her one, and observes that this admission can be read in the opposite direction. Responds to António Pedro Marques's question about why the shipwreck maiden is named Seawrack, adding a point about the narrator's unreliability.
