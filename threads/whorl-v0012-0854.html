Fan fic? Fooey.

Whorl list · 10 messages · 2001-06-19 to 2001-06-20

2001whorlThe Book of the Long SunKevin J. MaroneyDan'l Danehy-OakesWilliam H. AnsleyMichael Straight
The thread: Participants debated whether Hoof and Hide and the Book of the Short Sun count as fan fiction, and what the term means. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued fanfic is parasitic on an original creator's world and proposed a spectrum from pure fanfic to authorized continuations, while Kevin J. Maroney held that fanfic fills needs the original leaves unmet and called Shatner's Star Trek novels blatant Mary Sue fanfic. The discussion drifted from Wolfe into general genre talk, Mary Sue definitions, and whether the list itself is fan activity. It ended without agreement, with William Ansley conceding the list is fanac after Danehy-Oakes's correction.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: fanfic is parasitic on the creator's world and exists on a spectrum, with Hoof and Hide not fanfic
  • Kevin J. Maroney: Hoof and Hide is not fanfic under the strict definition, and fanfic exists to fill needs the original leaves unmet
  • William Ansley: distrusts fan activities and defined Mary Sue for readers, later conceding the list is fan activity
  • Michael Straight: a Mary Sue may be the author's fantasy self, which makes such fanfic off-putting

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

Argues that The Book of the Short Sun is not fan fiction, defining the term as unauthorized amateur stories set in another creator's world. Rejects the idea of a fan writing it, since Wolfe created the world and the editors' material is reconstruction or invention rather than fiction, citing the Oreb-like conversation as an example.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: The Book of the Short Sun is not fan fiction

Or pfui. With all due respect to the various people who've used this term, I'm just about apoplectic from not shouting at you about it, and now I will.

Look. Whatever _tBotSS_ is (or the editors-written third- person parts of it, or even "selected bits" of those), it ain't fanfic. The term is specific, refers to the parasitic creation of new stories in a _fictional_ world _created by some other person or corporate entity_, on an amateur or not-for-profit basis, and usually (but not always) quite _bad_. 2001-06-19 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that fan fiction is parasitic on the primary creator's world in a technical sense, and asks whether franchise novels count as fanfic and whether fanfic is a binary or fuzzy category. Responds to Wombattitude's Mary Sue jab by joking about Mary Janes and admitting past bad fanfic writing, offering a spectrum from pure fanfic through authorized continuations to pure creation. Does not discuss Wolfe's books.
proposes

Responding to my use of the term "parasitic," his

No, no. I meant the word in a strictly technical sense;
the existence of fanfic is completely dependent on, and
draws its energy from, the primary creator of the fictional world. (Or would that be "secondary creator," since the
fictional world is, by definition, a secondary creation? In which case the fanfic writer is a tertiary creator,
and...)

Right. Mary Janes are those shoes. Guess who doesn't read much fanfic? (Though I wrote some, and even got paid for it, a very long time ago. It was very bad indeed.)

In my defense, I was careful to say "usually (but not
always) quite _bad_." I know that good fanfic exists;
I'm just not intrepid enough to wade through the jungles of sludge and dig out the good stuff.

An interesting side note: Are franchise novels fanfic?
Ever? Is "fanfic" a boolean or a fuzzy term? One can
see a kind of spectrum running from purest fanfic; to
things like the "Best of Trek" anthologies; to the more
blatantly fannish Trek novels (i.e., "Spock, Messiah");
to the professionally franchised books; to "authorized
continuations" like the Anderson/B.Herbert DUNE books
and the "New Foundation Trilogy"; to hommage and parody; to "heavily influenced," and finally ending with "pure"
creation (which is _never_ pure).

--Blattid

2001-06-19 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Defends the claim that fanfic depends on borrowed characters and readers' conditioned responses, using biological terms like parasite and saprophyte to describe it. Responds to a quoted post by arguing that fanfic need not draw energy from the original author, citing Kirk and Spock and the hurt/comfort scene from Joanna Russ. The post is off-topic from Wolfe, reaching only a Shatner anecdote.
chats

Me on parasitism:

H'mmm. Perhaps rather than "energy" I should've said "power"; in any case, my point is that fanfickers don't have to create things like characters and settings, they use pre-existing ones and depend on conditioned responses (their own and other readers') to provide much of the buy-in. That is: if you keep the characters' behavioral and speech patterns pretty much consistent with what's in their original shows/stories, you can rely on the reader investing them with a lot of the life (energy, power, whatever) the original writer/director/actor/ whatever gave them. So you don't have to do very much with K and S; set up the right circumstances (the "hurt/comfort scene," a term I'm ripping off from Joanna Russ's essay on fanfic), and let the biology roll.

So are "species" and "organism." ("Ecosystem" and "biome" actually turn out to be a little easier.) Like "parasite," the terms are too damn useful (and too damn well-embedded in biological discourse) to go away any time soon.

Perhaps, though, the right word would be "saprophytic"?
(Though I gather that these days fanfic starts before a
show even finishes its run...)

And I would argue that Shatner probably agrees with you; remember his line in the Shatner-vs-Shatner fight scene in the last TOS movie:

Shatner 1: I can't believe I _kissed_ you.
Shatner 2: It must have been your lifelong ambition.

Whatever else Shatner may be, he is aware of his ego "problem" and not afraid to have fun with it. (See also "Free Enterprise.")

2001-06-20 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the mailing list itself is a form of fan activity, distinguishing fannish partying from serious constructive fandom (sercon) and placing list traffic within the broader sense of fanac. Responds to William Ansley's claim to have kept his distance from fannish activities, using light, joking wording rather than any reading of Wolfe's books.
disputes

William, William, William... I don't know how to tell you this, but this list is a fannish activity, or, as they used to say -- do they still? -- "fanac."

Granted, it's the kind of fanac that isn't usually referred to as "fannish," but only because within fandom "fannish" was (is?) generally used in contrast to "sercon," short for "serious and constructive," where "fannish" was more the "partying with a few thousand of your closest friends" sort of thing.

Much of the traffic on this list tends toward the sercon, but it's all fannish in the larger sense.

--Blattid
