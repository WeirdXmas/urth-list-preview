The thread: Michael Driussi proposed a list round-table interview with Gene Wolfe about The Book of the Long Sun, with questions sent by snail mail and one question per member. Richman suggested asking why Wolfe builds scenes around pretenses that turn out true, citing Silk's pretended sabotage and the staged dinner for Mint. Over the next few days, members posted questions in the thread: Reusch on Quetzal's motives, F.Q. Gouvea on Mint's turn to warlike behavior, Derek Bell on the 'beam' in Kypris's exorcism scene, Doug Eigsti on the Whorl's human cargo, and Marcos Gouvea on Blood's father, Mint's sacrifices, and the sky-object. The thread ends as a collection of separate questions for Wolfe rather than a debate, with no resolution.
  • Michael Richman: Wolfe builds scenes around pretenses that prove true, so ask why he is drawn to them
  • Fernando Q. Gouvea: Asks what caused Mint's warlike change, given Horn's doubt about Silk's explanation
  • Derek Bell: The 'beam' in Kypris's scene may echo the eye-beam verse or refer to the Whorl's network
  • Doug Eigsti: Was the Whorl's human cargo meant to preserve wild humanity or feed the Inhumi

Themes: Hidden identities and parentage, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Power, rulers and politics

1997-05-12 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Announces a proposed round-table interview with Gene Wolfe about The Book of the Long Sun, to be conducted by snail mail with questions gathered from list members. Explains the rules: one question per person, limited to the Long Sun books, citing text where relevant, with a Friday deadline for submissions.
proposes

Attention ladies and gentlemen, lurkers and larkers, et cetera, et ali, and all ships at sea.

In my regular correspondence with Gene Wolfe I asked him if he would have the time for a round-table interview (about THE BOOK OF THE LONG SUN) by snail mail, composed of questions from the Whorl list audience. He has agreed to this scheme, writing, "Collect some questions and I'll proceed to dodge them."

Okay! So here are the rules: interested parties should individually come up with the one question they would most like to ask and post it to the whorl list asap. I would like to get something in the mail by Friday, so we'll assume that is when the train is leaving the station--the response and the next train would come a few weeks later. Please limit the question to THE BOOK OF THE LONG SUN, and (as we normally do anyway), be sure to cite the text if the question is tied into a particular scene or scenes.

I'm thinking that each person can get in one question per round. (Conditional follow-up questions can probably be appended. I'll judge on a case-by-case basis, so go ahead and include the follow-up if you have it in mind.)

Don't be scared, don't be shy!

1997-05-12 Michael Richman #

Argues that Gene Wolfe often builds scenes around pretenses that turn out to be true, citing Patera Silk's pretended sabotage of the Trigivante dirigible and the staged dinner for Maytera Mint in The Book of the Long Sun. Proposes asking Wolfe why he is drawn to such situations, and frames the question as part of a round-table interview from the list.
proposes · close-textual-reading

Gene Wolfe seems to be very fond of pretenses that turn out to be the truth (one might almost call them 'True Lies' if one were extraordinarily brave).

For example, Patera Silk thinks he's only pretending to sabotage the Trigivante Derigible, when in fact instead of cutting off the fuel he's cut off the lubricating oil, causing the engine to freeze. Earlier, he plans to fool the Trigivante general into believing Maytera Mint is alive by setting a place at the table for her, but by the time the dinner begins he's already learned that Mint is in fact alive.

I would like to ask him what is his attraction to situations like this?

1997-05-15 Fernando Q. Gouvea #

Asks Gene Wolfe what caused Mint's change into a warlike character, quoting the narrator Horn's 'Defence' remark that Silk's claim she took her warlike nature from the Goddess of Love seems implausible. Grounds the question directly in the quoted passage from the Long Sun narrator's essay, with no argument offered beyond the quotation itself.
questions · close-textual-reading

OK, here's my question for Gene:

In Horn's "Defence", he writes: "Fewer than I have expected have found fault with Silk's assertion that [Mint] took her warlike character from the Goddess of Love, although it seems implausible to me." What did cause the change in Mint's character?

Suggests a biblical reading tied to the verse about removing the beam from one's eye, and an alternative literal reading linking it to the Whorl's mainframe network.

I can see it as a possible biblical reference - the quote about "removing the beam from your eye", but it a more literal interpretation might be a link with the rest of the Whorl/Mainframe network.)

1997-05-15 Doug Eigsti #

Asks whether the live human cargo of the Whorl was carried to preserve the "wild" half of mankind for a vassal state, or to feed the Inhumi's blood lust. Grounds the question in Cyracia's tale of ancient machine-minded people who bargained away their wild nature, and in the Typhon/Pas link as the only firm connection between the New Sun and Long Sun.
questions · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Typhon/Pas preserved wild humanity as vassal state

mantis,

It is a daunting proposition to boil down all the variables and uncertainities from the Long Sun into just one question. The Whorl is nearly as grand and unsearchable as our world, peopled with characters toiling away in their quarter oblivious to the grand gambits of unseen forces above. Part of the mystique of the series is the mystery. If I know Gene Wolfe, never met him, he will not give away anything he deems important in an answer to a direct question that he has evaded in 1400 pages of fiction. Such things, it seems, are meant to be an exercise for the reader. I almost feel guilty in hoping that his answers are as elusive and diffuse, and delectible, as his text. I am reminded of LeGuin's THE LEFT HAND OF DARKNESS where one must take exacting care in drafting one's question else risk getting an unexpected answer. None the less, the offer is irresistable. Here's hopin':

As has been lamented in these postings the publicized link between the NEW SUN and LONG SUN turns out to be tenuous when all is said and done, Typhon/Pas being the only common character. (Scylla seems only to share the common name).
My search for a more robust connection lead me to the story told to Severian by Cyracia of the "race of ancient days [that] reached the stars, and how they bargained away all the wild half of themselves to do so, so they no longer cared for wind...love or lust...songs or any other animal things they believed they had brought with them out of the rain forests at the bottom of time-though in fact, so my uncle told me, those things brought them." (SWORD, Ch.VI, p. 38, paperback). These machines later tried to restore the "wild" thoughts to men, in order to destroy them, by introducing "artifacts of every kind, calculated by them to revive all those thoughts that people had put behind them because they could not be written in numbers". (SWORD, Ch VI, p.39, paperback).
The necessity for having living cultures on the Whorl segregated in city-states, isolated by topographical barriers fortified by Chem armies programmed to be mutually hostile in order to dissuade an organized revolt against Mainframe seems to be an overly complex means of transport, considering that Typhon obviously had access to "sleeper" technology (Mamelta). My question, in a roundabout way: Was the live cargo of the Whorl only put on board to preserve the "wild" half of mankind so the Typhon/Pas entity could establish a vital human vassal state on the destination world, or did the Inhumi require the "wild" parts to satisfy their blood lust?

=Talon=
aka Doug Eigsti

1997-05-15 Marcos B. Gouvea #

Asks Gene Wolfe a question about the identity of Blood's father in the Long Sun books, and also asks about the five things Mint gave up at fifteen and whether the dirty object Marble/Rose saw in the sky is the Whorl. Notes that the question deadline is near and that he has not contributed enough to ask a big question. Quotes the passage from Calde chapter 8 about dust and the sky-object as the basis for the last question.
questions · close-textual-reading

Gah! I was so busy doing my science fair project that I
didn't check my e-mail until now... I would've been disappointed if I had missed this oppurtunity for some *real* answers to all those mysterious questions. Cutoff is Friday, right? (I hope I hope I hope I hope!)
I don't really want to be attributed with ruining the
mysteries of the whorl, and I don't think I've really contributed enough to justify me asking one of the big ones. It's being knawing on me for a while anyway. So, with that in mind, my question is: "Who WAS Blood's father?" I don't think we ever found it out.

Bloody was a baby,
and Rosey was his Ma,
and though she vowed her secrecy,
We should know who was his Pa!

Sorry. Just feeling a little inspired.

In case anyone who hasn't asked their question yet didn't notice, no one's thought of asking whether Blue/Green was St. Anne/Whatever (I haven't read that series yet, so I wouldn't know). Come on, we neeed to get all of the biggies! Also, did anyone ask specifically for the five things Mint gave up after she became 15? One final loose strand: At the beginning of Chapter 8 in Calde, Marble/Rose remembers having seen an object in the sky and thinking that it was dirty, causing the soldier nearby to remark "Why don't you dust it?" Was the dirty object the Whorl?
"A memory so remote that it seemed to have lain behind her curved metal skull stirred at this, her third view. Dust flew, as dust always does when something that has remained motionless for a long time moves at last."
Thanks!
---Quill
