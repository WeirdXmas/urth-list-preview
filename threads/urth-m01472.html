The thread: Kieran Mullen argued that Severian's choice of the Reborn Urth over the Frozen Future is not simply immoral, since neither future is real until it happens and both have inhabitants, defending this against a poster who called the New Sun's death and damnation unchristian. Chris replied that neither future is the true one, so the immediate harm of Severian's actions should count, and asked why the Hierogrammates would impose a demanding trial for a desirable outcome. Maru pressed whether rejecting a true future leaves any probability-based decision procedure, arguing the trial's choice is effectively binary and comparing it to consent before risky surgery. The exchange ended without resolution, with the debate narrowing to whether the choice can be judged morally at all.
  • Kieran Mullen: Severian's choice is not simply immoral, since both futures are unreal until they happen and both have inhabitants
  • Chris: the immediate harm of Severian's actions counts, and the trial suggests the New Sun was not an unqualified good
  • maru: rejecting a true future makes probability-based decisions impossible; the trial's choice is effectively binary

Themes: Justice, torture and mercy, Free will and choice, Christ figures and messiahs

2005-03-31 Kieran Mullen #

Argues that Severian's choice of Reborn Urth over the Frozen Future is not simply immoral, since both futures are unreal until they happen and each has its inhabitants. Uses the Master Ash and Green Man figures, the battle in The Urth of the New Sun, and a poisoned-bog analogy to rebut a poster who called the New Sun's death and damnation unchristian.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology

Well, as a Catholic and a reader of Wolfe I have found this thread to be rather odd. Your point seems to be that any change in the future of Urth that causes the death of anyone is immoral. The supposition is that the "Frozen Urth" future is the "true future" and that "Reborn Urth" is some choice that Severian makes leading to the deaths of millions.

Neither future is real until it happens. We see people from both (Master Ash and the Green Man). It is true that Severian chooses the cataclysm of rebirth. However, to choose the Frozen Future would be to condemn the Green Man and all those who might live in the future to non-existence. Is that inately better?

I always thought that the inhabitants of Urth got to choose their future - that was the point of the battle in UotNS. Those who opposed the New Sun fought those who favored it. The New Sun won.

For me the analogy is a poisoned bog that has some critters eking out some existence in it. You might choose to clean it up, but in doing so you wreak havoc on those already there, or who are adapted to the poisons. So you should just leave it a toxic dump.

Let's say Severian had to go to Briah to *stop* the arrival of the White Fountain and keep the Urth on its current path of freezing in the dark. Are you arguing that that would be a better choice?

Kieran Mullen

2005-04-03 Chris P replying to Kieran Mullen #

Argues that neither the Frozen Urth nor the Reborn Urth is the true future, so the immediate harm of Severian's actions is what counts, and asks why the Hierogrammates would set a demanding trial for an outcome that was desirable. Responds to Kieran Mullen's moral argument, which he made on the list, and questions whether the trial's inevitability suggests the New Sun was not an unqualified good for them.
questions · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Hierogrammates regarded the New Sun as undesirable

By my reckoning neither is the "true future"; in reality we have no such conveniences to fall back on. If that's the case then you are left with the immediate effect of your actions on people who already exist. The effect, in this case, is devastating.

This sounds just a bit too much like "might makes right". But the trial is an interesting issue to explore in its own right. Someone noted that at the time they held the trial, at least some involved already knew that Severian was the one who was going to bring the New Sun. What *was* the point of the trial? Well, if I remember correctly, for one thing, it somehow sealed the *inevitable* truth of the New Sun future.

This brings up something odd. I don't bring this up as a rhetorical point but as a serious question. Why would the Hierogrammates set up such a demanding test for an outcome which was actually *desirable*? (Assuming the outcome was, in fact, desirable). Why not, instead, offer it to the first Autarch that came along - or just do it on their own accord? The idea that they would only put it in motion when it had reached the point of inevitability makes the New Sun sound as if it were an undesirable outcome for them. Or at least, not an unqualified good.

Or move the critters to new environs.

Are you arguing that these are the only two possible choices?

Civet

2005-04-07 Maru Dubshinki replying to Chris #

Questions whether Kieran's rejection of a 'true future' leaves any decision procedure based on probabilities impossible, and argues the trial's choice is effectively binary. Grounds this in the New Sun's shift from probable to certain after the trial and an analogy to consent before risky surgery.
questions · chronology-and-plot-logic

So you are arguing that any decision procedure based on probabilities is impossible?

I recall the explanation being that the New Sun, before the trial, was *probable* but not certain. After the trial it was dead certain. Why though probably has to do more with consent issues.

If you want to perform some risky, yet necessary surgery (like a heart bypass), you don't simply do it. You get consent.

Do you see any other choices that don't break down into 'New Sun comes' or 'New Sun doesn't come'?
Much as I hate to say, this would seem a pretty solidly binary choice.

~Maru
