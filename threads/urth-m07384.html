An Evil Guest - Big Questions, Major SPOILERS

Urth list · 16 messages · 2008-09-21 to 2010-01-04

20082010urthJohn WatkinsMatthew KeeleyAndy RobertsonJosh GellerBruce HaylesGwern BranwenPedro PereiraMatthew GrovesRussell Wodell
The thread: The thread opened with a flood of open questions about An Evil Guest, including the future-and-1940s setting, the Wolders, the Silent Woman, and the names of Arthur Thomas Franklin and Gideon Chase. John Watkins read Arthur Thomas Franklin as the ATF and the defective child as a naturally born child with a genetic condition, grounded in real abortion practice. Gwern Branwen and John Watkins then pushed the discussion toward eugenics, with Watkins citing Peter Singer and Branwen citing historical infant killing and state programs, while Matthew Keeley disputed that parents would kill such children and argued the book's future is dark. Later posts drifted into the Squid God, Cassiopeia and Gideon names, and Gwern Branwen's Waco reading, with no clear resolution.
  • John Watkins: Arthur Thomas Franklin is the ATF and the defective child is simply born with a genetic condition, a reading grounded in real abortion practice
  • Matthew Keeley: the defective children are manufactured, and most parents would not kill a disabled child after birth
  • Gwern Branwen: 'defective' evokes eugenics and historical infant killing, including state programs
  • John Watkins: the eugenics reading is correct and Peter Singer's views make post-birth killing thinkable
  • Pedro Pereira: Latro's gods are real pagan deities alongside the one God, not aliens
  • Matthew Keeley: the islanders' true god may be a Squid God that ate Hanga

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Power, rulers and politics, Justice, torture and mercy

Argues that the 'defective' children in An Evil Guest are manufactured rather than naturally born, reasoning from the word's connotation and from the idea of post-birth abortion. Responds to John Watkins's suggestion that the defective children are simply born with genetic or inborn conditions, and concedes the ATF identification he had missed.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: defective children are manufactured rather than born defective

Re: ATF: I should have remembered about the ATF.

Re: defective: I considered that, but "defective" has the ring of manufacture about it; as in "I returned the defective TV to the store." And these aren't kids being aborted from the womb: this is "post-parturition" (i.e. after-birth) abortion. Are we supposed to assume that there are people who would a) give birth to a child with Down Syndrome and then b) abort it after living with said child for a few years. I don't think anyone good enough to do a) would be cold-hearted enough to do b).

Argues that the eugenics reading of An Evil Guest's talk of a 'defective' child is correct, and that a post-delivery killing would not be unthinkable to some. supports · author-and-biography · theories: Eugenics reading of defective child in An Evil Guest

"''I do not think it is always wrong to kill an innocent human being,'' Professor Singer told the rapt audience in Harold Helm Auditorium. ''Simply killing an infant is never equivalent to killing a person.''"

If Wolfe is pro-life, as many conservative Catholics are, he'd be quite likely to be aware of Singer's stance on the moral significance of the life of a disabled child.

2008-09-21 Matthew Keeley replying to John Watkins #

Disputes John Watkins's claim that people would readily kill a defective child after birth, arguing that most parents who keep such a child would not later kill it. Grounds this in the eugenics theme of An Evil Guest, questioning whether Wolfe's hypothetical civil rights groups would really seek permission to kill disabled children, and calls the future Wolfe depicts dark.
disputes · theology-and-religion

I know of Singer and his ideas - my point was that most people who would give birth to a child with a genetic defect (instead of aborting it) would be incapable of killing after it was born, after it was more (as it were) than an image on a sonogram. Would Wolfe's hypothetical "civil rights" groups really be looking for permission to kill retarded children five years after birth? How many people would actually take up on that? But if your designer baby didn't come out to spec, maybe you'd eventually decide it wasn't worth it... I think Wolfe's future is pretty dark, once you turn off the Broadway lights.

-Matt

2008-09-22 Gwern Branwen replying to Matthew Keeley #

Argues that killing of infants and children has occurred systematically across history, citing Spartan and Russian exposure, Victorian accounts of China, massacres, and Nazi and American sterilization and euthanasia programs. Responds to John Watkins's eugenics point and his citation of Peter Singer by noting that the choice to kill may be absent under state eugenics programs.
disputes · author-and-biography

On 2008.09.21 20:05:52 -0400, Matthew Keeley <[email]> scribbled 3.2K characters:

I wouldn't be so sanguine about people not killing infants and youngsters. There are many examples of people killing their kids, in a systematic fashion. Wolfe would no doubt be acquainted with the famous Spartan traditions of exposure; I recall similar Russian traditions. You can't read Victorian accounts of China without there being clucking over how those Chinese like to abandon babies to die, to say nothing of how various massacres, wars, purges etc. never respected young humans (and sometimes targeted them specifically; I think I remember Rwandan rhetoric about not letting the children of cockroaches grow up to be cockroaches).

Some of these are economically motivated but many are not. And then we begin to consider all the sterilizations and euthanasias in America and Nazi Germany.

Now, presumably someone who is pro-life before birth will not become pro-choice after birth (and in an extreme Singeresque way); but on the other hand, I've seen more than one fictional work in which a burdensome sibling or parent is quietly extinguished. ("How tragic that our retarded brother passed away last night. I suppose he must have been stifled by the heat.") And we're assuming that that someone has a choice; in some situations like the national eugenics programs, that choice may be nonexistent.

I wouldn't be so sanguine about people not killing infants and youngsters. There are many examples of people killing their kids, in a systematic fashion. Wolfe would no doubt be acquainted with the famous Spartan traditions of exposure; I recall similar Russian traditions. You can't read Victorian accounts of China without there being clucking over how those Chinese like to abandon babies to die, to say nothing of how various massacres, wars, purges etc. never respected young humans (and sometimes targeted them specifically; I think I remember Rwandan rhetoric about not letting the children of cockroaches grow up to be cockroaches).

Some of these are economically motivated but many are not. And then we begin to consider all the sterilizations and euthanasias in America and Nazi Germany.

Now, presumably someone who is pro-life before birth will not become pro-choice after birth (and in an extreme Singeresque way); but on the other hand, I've seen more than one fictional work in which a burdensome sibling or parent is quietly extinguished. Asks whether Gideon engineered the final events of An Evil Guest, including Bill Reis's death, Cassie's rescue by bats, and the storm, or whether those events happened independently. Perhaps owing to lousy reading, I am left with a lot of fundamental questions that no one else has brought up.

Gideon gets the ambassadorship. Does this mean that he was able to get the FBI and the president what they wanted? Bill Reis dies at the end, but the goal was to understand his operations, not kill him. Has Gideon already given the president the information he wants and arranged his death? We know that Gideon has been working for him, and presumably is collecting the information he needs. Or is Reis's death completely independent of Gideon, but he's already found the information he needs? If he is behind it, how could he have foreseen the storm and Kanoa's takeover?

Was Cassie used just to make Bill Reis vulnerable? Did Gideon expect Bill Reis to love her, or did he just hope Reis interacting with her would get him closer to Reis?

The bats that save Cassie and land her on the island--is Gideon's hand in this at all?

Reis caused the storm by dropping the gold over the Storm King and getting the attention of the navy, after all.
