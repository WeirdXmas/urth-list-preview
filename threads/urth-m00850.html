The thread: Readers debated whether Garvaon's killing of the Angrborn king Gilling and his subsequent self-sacrifice in The Wizard Knight are noble. Stilskin argued the concealment makes Garvaon too generously read and that the deed itself is not meant as noble, while Dan'l Danehy-Oakes defended the concealment as necessary to protect Idnn and the knights from slaughter and enslavement. Nathan Spears read the killing as driven by anguish and bitterness over Idnn's marriage, and harlekin saw the sacrifice as both selfish and selfless, sparing Garvaon shame rather than redeeming him. James Wynn countered with a chivalric reading, comparing the act to rescuing a maiden from a dragon. The thread ended without consensus, with several competing readings left standing.
  • stilskin: Garvaon is given too much nobility; the killing is not meant as noble, though the reader may be misreading Wolfe
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: concealment was necessary to protect Idnn and the knights, so the cover-up is defensible
  • Nathan Spears: killing driven by bitterness over Idnn's marriage; death shows he remained a true knight, not redemption
  • harlekin: sacrifice is both selfish and selfless, sparing Garvaon shame rather than redeeming him
  • James Wynn: killing was knightly chivalry, like rescuing a maiden from a dragon, not bitterness

Themes: Justice, torture and mercy, Fathers, mothers and children, Love and loss

2005-01-04 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to stilskin #

Disputes Stilskin's claim that Garvaon's concealment of the king's murder shows little nobility, arguing that he had to hide it. Reasons that discovery would have brought Angrborn slaughter and enslavement of the knights and others, including Idnn, so her fate would have been no better.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Garvaon hid the murder to protect Idnn

Stilskin,

I disagree. Garvaon _had_ to hide what he had done, or it would have had far worse consequences than the ones he
was hoping to avoid - if the Angrborn had known that one of the "hotlanders" (and one of the "honorable" knights no less) had murdered their king, they would have slaughtered all the knights and the men-at-arms, and enslaved the rest, including Idnn, so she would have wound up in the same
position (so to speak) except with someone other than
Gilling, and probabily-possibly not even the new king but just some random Angrborn freeholder who bought her at
auction.

2005-01-04 Nathan Spears replying to stilskin #

Argues that Garvaon's killing of King Gilling was driven by anguish and bitterness over Idnn's marriage rather than ignoble, and that his death was not a redemption but a sign he remained a true knight. Grounds this in Gilling's prior murders, the political pressures of making peace with the giants, and Garvaon's dying words to Able, the only confidant of his love for Idnn.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Garvaon's killing was anguish, not ignobility; Garvaon's death was not a redemption

Yeah, I think the killing was motivated mainly by bitterness; Idnn was marrying an unworthy soulless creature for her father's political gain; Garvaon was unaware of the true nature of the 'gift' to King Gilling until the arrival in Utgard; Idnn never loved Garvaon, and in fact never knew of his love for her. I wouldn't say it was a petty bitterness, but bubbled up from something bordering on anguish. I wouldn't even say it was an ignoble deed. Before the first stabbing, King Gilling had just murdered a man with his fist, and had ordered what he thought would be the murders of two knights for his court's amusement. The final blow was probably less hot-blooded but no less justified. The humans were trying to make peace with the giants because they couldn't kill them all.

But it still wasn't a knightly deed, and one that Idnn would have reviled him for. Garvaon looked at Able as he died and said something like, "You knew," because Able was the only one Garvaon had confided in about his love for Idnn, and the only one would understand all the forces pressuring him to commit such a deed. It was a departure from his nature, not an indicator of it; his death wasn't a redemption but a sign that Garvaon was still a true knight.

2005-01-04 harlekin #

Argues that Garvaon's self-sacrifice in The Wizard Knight is both selfish and selfless, since his death spares him from living with shame rather than redeeming him. Responds to a quoted claim that Idnn would find the sacrifice reprehensible, agreeing the point is that Garvaon gains nothing from it and that his place in the afterlife was assured.
supports · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Garvaon's death is a gift to spare shame

"I'm not sure that Garvaon's sacrifice would have been
appreciated by Idnn had she known of it. In fact, I'm
pretty confident she would find it reprehensible and
offensive."

That's the point. Garvaon gets no benefit from his action. It's a sacrifice of honor on his part. Thus it is selfish and selfless at the same time. Garvaon's death is a gift to him so he won't have to live with his shame, not an act of redemption. His place is skai was assured, IMO.

Don

2005-01-05 James Wynn replying to Nathan Spears #

Argues that Garvaon killed Gilling out of knightly chivalry rather than bitterness, comparing the act to rescuing a fair maiden from a dragon. Grounds this in Idnn's marriage, Able's refusal to rescue her, and the conventions of courtly love, responding to Nathan Spears's claim that bitterness motivated the killing.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Garvaon killed Gilling out of knightly chivalry

Although I agree with much that Nathan says on this subject, I think this statement goes too far.

Garvaon seems to see Idnn's marriage in the same way Idnn saw it as they were approaching Utgard. (In the same way Adam Stephanides described on this list IIRC). I don't think he saw it as any different from rescuing a "fair maiden" from a dragon. Killing such a creature in that circumstance would be knightly in any circumstance...even if it were asleep. Humans and Giants are not the same and the same rules of knightly killing do not apply. Able doesn't refuse to rescue Idnn earlier because the act would be "unkightly". He does it because the act would be impractical for both he *and* Idnn. Able refers to it as equivalent to 'running away to live in Fairyland". Although it appears that he eventually does that, it does not seem that at the time he saw that as easily within his grasp.

Garvaon on the other hand seems to have seen Idnn the way Able saw Disiri. Able would have committed any murder for her sake (and offers to). Was that unknightly behavior? Not as I recall the requirements of medieval "courtly love".

It is true that Idnn would not have thanked Garvaon for his act, but that is because she saw things differently after her marriage. She seems to see marriage -- even one to King Gilling -- as more than a formality. Idnn's and Garvaon's separate viewpoints on this are simply among the paradoxes of the knightly code.

~ Crush
