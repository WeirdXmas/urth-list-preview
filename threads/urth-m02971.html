Career of Severian as Conciliator

Urth list · 3 messages · 2006-10-25 to 2006-10-25

2006urthThe Book of the New SunJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-OakesDan Rabin
The thread: Dan Rabin argued that Severian's career as the Conciliator extends beyond The Urth of the New Sun, pointing to the Conciliator vanishing and returning and to the many replicas at the inn, and asked Christian readers how these reflect religious themes. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes answered with the Catholic idea of bilocation, citing Padre Pio and Jesus vanishing from crowds, and suggested a Heinlein allusion to "All You Zombies--". Jeff Wilson disputed the Heinlein point, noting that "Zombies" never has more than two copies of the protagonist at once and that "By His Bootstraps" reaches three, and mentioned an earlier Poul Anderson story. The thread ends there with the Heinlein reference corrected and the religious reading left as a proposal.
  • Dan Rabin: Severian's Conciliator career continues past Urth, and the replicas and vanishing invite religious readings
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the doubled presence at the inn echoes Catholic bilocation, and Heinlein's time-travel story may be alluded to
  • Jeff Wilson: Heinlein's Zombies has at most two copies at once, so the allusion is weaker than proposed; Poul Anderson came earlier

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Catholic faith and sacraments, Time loops and causality

2006-10-25 Dan Rabin #

Argues that Severian's career as the Conciliator extends beyond what The Urth of the New Sun shows, citing references in The Book of the New Sun to the Conciliator vanishing and returning. Points to Severian's musings in Urth about how many guests at the inn are replicas, and to an inversion where one body becomes many, asking Christian readers how these details reflect religious themes.
proposes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's Conciliator career extends beyond Urth; Severian's replicas appear at the inn; Autarch in one body inverts Urth's dispersal

I don't think the narrative of _The Urth of the New Sun_ covers all appearances of Severian as the Conciliator:

1. I recall a couple of references in _The Book of the New Sun_ (spoken by ?Typhon, ?Agia) to the Conciliator having disappeared and reappeared.

2. In _Urth_, Severian wonders how many of the guests at the inn where he fights the zombie are him. At the time of his writing in Ushas, at least, he seems to assume either that he's going to do a lot more walking in the Corridors of Time, or that more replicas will be constructed in different threads of the tapestry of time.

...and I just noticed a Wolfean inversion between _Book of the_ and _Urth of the_: In _Citadel_, Severian becomes the Autarch, "who in one body is a thousand" (ch. 27); whereas in _Urth_ his one person is dispersed into a succession of bodies.

Could we have the opinions of our knowledgeable Christian contributors on how these two details might reflect religious themes?

-- Dan Rabin

2006-10-25 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Dan Rabin #

Suggests that Severian's doubled presence at the inn, where he wonders how many guests are himself, echoes the Catholic miracle of bilocation, citing Padre Pio and Jesus vanishing from crowds. Also proposes a possible Heinlein allusion to the time-travel story "All You Zombies--" in answer to Dan Rabin's question about religious themes.
proposes · theology-and-religion · source-and-allusion · theories: Severian's inn scene echoes bilocation miracle; Urth alludes to Heinlein's All You Zombies

Well, the first thing that comes to my mind is the 'miracle of bilocation,' whereby various Catholic saints have been known to appear in two or more places at the same time. (The first example I can think of is is Padre Pio.)

Also, Jesus was known to mysteriously disappear from amongst crowds and show up somewhere else.

You've also made me to wonder whether Wolfe, or you, were making a slight Heinlein reference:

Somehow this made me think of RAH's time-travel classic, "All You Zombies--"

2006-10-25 Jeff Wilson replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Corrects Dan Rabin's suggestion of a Heinlein reference by noting that Heinlein's "Zombies" never has more than two copies of the protagonist at once and "By His Bootstraps" reaches three. Adds that the earliest mass time-duplicate story the poster has found is a Poul Anderson short story.

There's never more than two of the protagonist at once in "Zombies", and "By His Bootstraps" only gets up to three. The earliest mention of mass time duplicates I've found so far was a Poul Anderson short.
