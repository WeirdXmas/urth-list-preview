The thread: Dan'l Danehy-Oakes corrected David Stockhoff's claim that the Cumaean is named after a New Testament witch, saying she is named for the Cumaean Sibyl, and suggested she may be a powerful being like Father Inire. Stockhoff proposed she is a different kind of being from the Inhumi and that the winged creature in Inire's mirror book might be Tzadkiel, and Roy Lackey answered that it was Tzadkiel, quoting the text. b sharp offered an Apollo-based analogy for the Cumaean and later argued that the bent man in the House Absolute is Father Inire, a weaker version of Tzadkiel. John Watkins linked the Cumaean to Virgil's Fourth Eclogue. Tim O'Donnell argued Wolfe leaves such powers unexplained, while much of the thread drifted into film recommendations. The Tzadkiel identification was answered from the text, but the Cumaean's nature was left open.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Cumaean is named for the Sibyl and may be a power like Father Inire, offered as an assumption
  • David Stockhoff: Cumaean is a different kind of being from the Inhumi; the winged creature may be Tzadkiel
  • Roy C. Lackey: the winged creature was Tzadkiel, per the Urth of the New Sun passage
  • b sharp: Cumaean is an Apollo-like healing alien analog; Inire and the Autarch are weaker Tzadkiels
  • John Watkins: Cumaean reference may come from Virgil's Fourth Eclogue
  • Tim O'Donnell: Wolfe deliberately leaves beings' powers unexplained and pays off details later

2009-02-05 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to David Stockhoff #

Corrects David Stockhoff's claim that the Cumaean is named after a New Testament witch, saying she is named for the Cumaean Sibyl, the prophetess of Apollo at Cumae. Argues she may be a being of power of the same order as Father Inire, offering this as an assumption rather than certainty.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: The Cumaean is named for the Cumaean Sibyl; The Cumaean is of the same order of being as Father Inire

David,

No; the Cumaean is _not_ named for a witch out of the New Testament. Rather, she is named for the priestess-prophetess of Apollo at Cumae.

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumaean_Sybil

As such, she might well be a "being of power." I had concluded (assumed?) that she was of the same order of being as Father Inire.

2009-02-05 David Stockhoff #

Proposes that the Cumaean is a different kind of alien or powerful being from the Inhumi, and that the winged creature Severian sees in Father Inire's mirror book may be an Inhumu, possibly Tzadkiel. Grounds this on the Cumaean's unmasked face, lack of wings, and her name taken from a New Testament witch, and asks whether there is consensus.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · source-and-allusion · theories: The Cumaean is a different alien from the Inhumi; The winged creature in Inire's mirror book is an Inhumu; The winged creature may be Tzadkiel

Tim

My assumption---or rationalization---has always been that the Cumaean was an alien of a different sort than the Inhumi. Or else just a being of power---she is named after a witch out of the New Testament, after all. The faces and lack of wings or any serious attempt to disguise herself lead me to that.

Beyond that, I dunno. It's possible Wolfe hadn't worked out the details yet. Who would the thing feed on?

I also have always assumed that the great toothy butterfly-creature Severian sees flying through the pages of Father Inire's mirror book was an Inhumu. Is there any consensus on that? Was it Tzadkiel?

As I had suspected, the Cumaean was not a woman at all; yet neither was she one of the horrors I had beheld in the gardens of the House Absolute. * Reptilian in nature

2009-02-06 Roy C. Lackey replying to David Stockhoff #

Asks whether there is any consensus that the flying creature Severian sees through the pages of Father Inire's mirror book was an Inhumu, and whether it was Tzadkiel. Replies to David Stockhoff's earlier point by answering that it was Tzadkiel, quoting the passage in Urth of the New Sun where Severian recounts meeting her between the pages of the book.
answers · close-textual-reading · theories: The creature Severian sees is Tzadkiel, not an Inhumu

Severian sees flying through the pages of Father Inire's mirror book was an Inhumu. Is there any consensus on that? Was it Tzadkiel?<<

No inhumi. It was Tzadkiel.

"And it was pleasant indeed to sit upon that bank, occasionally refreshing my laboring tongue with the cold, clean water of the brook, and recount to Tzadkiel how I had seen her first between the pages of Father Inire's book, and how I had helped to capture her aboard her own ship, and how she had been male and called herself Zak, and how she had cared for me when I was injured." (URTH, chap. XL, p.-283)

-Roy

2009-02-06 b sharp replying to David Stockhoff #

Proposes that the Cumaean is an alien analog of an ancient healing god like Apollo, whose animal alter egos include the old leech and Ceryx, linking her to Father Inire's shapeshifting guises. Supports this with the myth of the Cumaean Sybil who bargained for long life from Apollo and with the meaning of Ceryx as herald, and corrects David Stockhoff's memory of the winged creature.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: The Cumaean is an analog of an ancient healing god; Father Inire appears in many guises as Apollo analog; Ceryx and the leech are the Cumaean's alter egos

I thought the winged creature in the book was more angelic-looking than lepidopterous. I really don't remember it being "toothy". There was a predatory butterfly sort of creature Severian encounters on Tzadkiel's ship. Perhaps there was a mix-up of these two?

I am of the view that Father Inire, as an ancient god of the arts (Apollo)/alien analog, appears in various guises throughout the story, mostly as monkeys or monkey-like and/or artsy human characters such as Fechin, Isangoma, the boatman, the cowled cenobite, the masked jungle guide and perhaps Rudesind.

If the Cumaean is Inire's fellow analog, ancient god of medicine/alien I expect her also to have an animal alter ego and to appear in other guises. Aside from an incidental snake here and there, her most likely alter egos would be the old leech/pedophile and Ceryx as they are all obsessed with raising the dead. Her attraction to young men could be a parallel to Inire's obsession with young women. (so many greek gods seemed to share the same sort of pedophilia)

Moreover the mythical Cumaean Sybil had requested long life (1000 years) from Apollo in exchange for her virginity (when she reneged on the deal, he let her live but shrivel). Apollo is, of course, also associated with prophesy. Also, Ceryx means "herald" and the Cumaean Sybil was known as an early herald of Christ.

2009-02-06 John Watkins replying to b sharp #

Suggests that the Cumaean reference in Wolfe's story may come from Virgil's Fourth Eclogue, which contains the Sibyl's prophecy of Christ and also praises Apollo. Supports b sharp's Apollo analogy by noting that Severian has as much Apollo imagery as Christ imagery, making the Virgil allusion especially tempting.
supports · source-and-allusion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Cumaean reference drawn from Virgil's Fourth Eclogue; Apollo imagery made the Virgil allusion tempting

Virgil's Fourth Eclogue, which contains the Sybil's supposed prophesy of Christ, also praises Apollo. Severian has as much Apollo imagery around him as Christ imagery, so the reference may have been especially tempting.

2009-02-06 O'Donnell, Tim (BOSI) replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that Wolfe introduces Cumaeans and other beings with unexplained powers and never explains them, letting readers accept them as part of the setting, and notes how details like Tzadkiel are laid down and paid off books later. Responds to David Stockhoff's remarks about Cumaeans and the Inhumi, and also recommends the film Primer to fans of Wolfe's time-loop puzzles.
supports · close-textual-reading

Thanks for that!

On the Cumaean I always think it is great that Wolfe introduces these beings with various powers but doesn't bother chasing them down, explaining them, etc. They are accepted as part of the milieu and the reader can get on with it. But yeah, it would have been ambitious indeed had it been an Inhumu, I don't know how many of the details of the Inhumu Wolfe had in mind when writing the original series, nevermind BOTLS and BOTSS!

On the fact that it was Tzadkiel, I think Wolfe often lays down these details without necessarily following them up. Then 5 books (in his good time) later you get a pay-off. Wonderful! 5th pass through BOTSS and I am still only getting to grips with some of the details in there.

May have been mentioned here before but has anyone seen the film "Primer"? All about time loops and whatnot, it is not amazing but a fan of Wolfe may enjoy untangling the various strands laid down.

2009-02-10 b sharp replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that the bent little man in the Urth of the New Sun House Absolute scene is Father Inire, and that the Autarch and Inire are weaker versions of Tzadkiel through shape-changing and multiple roles. Responds to David Stockhoff's doubt by pointing to matching physical description and Inire's location in the House Absolute, while conceding the Cumaean fits less well.
proposes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: The bent little man is Father Inire; Autarch is a weak imitation of Inire; Inire is a weaker version of Tzadkiel

Well I have always thought this was the most obvious incarnation of Father Inire- the physical description matches and they are in Father Inire's portion of the House Absolute. The Cumaean seems to be more of an outdoor dweller. The other little, bent monkey-like and/or artistic guys are a bit harder to identify as Inire but it helps make sense to the story for me if they are.

As I see it, the old Autarch with his multiple roles in Commonwealth society is a weak imitation of Father Inire, whose shape changing ability allows greater diversity in his various roles. Likewise Father Inire is a weaker version of Tzadkiel, who has even greater shape changing ability and multiple roles to be played.

-bsharp
