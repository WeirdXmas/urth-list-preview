1998-10-31 Kieran Mullen #

Argues that Hy is loved despite her faults and that Silk's relationship with her fits the church-as-bride allegory tied to Mary Magdalene imagery. Answers Greg Neyman's question about why Silk is drawn to Hy, citing Silk's mission to save her and his changed state after the Outsider's second illumination, and closes with a joke about Nightside the Long Sun.
answers · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Hy represents the worldly person loved despite her faults; Silk is a redemptive figure who saves Hy; Silk and Hy allegorize the church as bride of Christ

[deletia]

Well, she is beautiful and passionate, a combination that is pretty attractive. If you want a more literary interpretaion, I figured that it was related to the allegory of the church as the bride of Christ which is also tied up with Mary Magdelene imagery ("whoring after false gods" sort of thing). Silk is a redemtive figure (though not exactly Christic) and Hy seemed to me to be the worldly person who is loved, for all of their faults and failings. Certainly Silk is on a mission to save her - especially at the very end of the book.

I'm still not sure I understand the change in Silk that takes place after he and Hy sleep together and he has a second illumination from the Outsider. (It's also been a while since I read that part.) Silk certainly seems changed to me. But the storyline also seems to get murkier.

1998-10-31 Greg Neyman #

Asks whether chems in The Book of the Long Sun can access their assembly information and how they could keep running, raising doubts that Maytera Marble has survived 300 years on original fuel. Also replies to Keiran about Hy and defends the idea that puns and alliteration in the text are lost in translation, citing the use of shag.
questions · close-textual-reading · theories: Chems can assemble fusion packs themselves; Maytera Marble is not running on original fuel

Thanks everyone for responding to my questions ... here goes ...

Rostrum -
Chems must be able to access this information ... it's not like they shove the raw material in one end of them, and little chems come out (like bios) ... they've gotta assemble all the stuff themselves, by hand ... it's true that each has only half the plans, but if a rich person wanted fusion packs, all he'd have to do is take a male and female chem, and ask them to build fusion packs for him. If they were building a child, they'd get to that step eventually.
Hey, something just occurred to me ... Hammerstone mentions on many occasions that chems don't eat at all ... even potto says that, when alluding to the fact that his tunnel army can go a long time on little supplies cuz most of them are chems ... but even fusion packs don't run forever ... energy has to be input at some time ... I refuse to believe that Maytera Marble, of Moly, or whatever her name is, who's lived for 300 years, is still running on original fuel. Thoughts?
Thank you Keiran for replying to my question bout Hy ... as for English, two things ... first of all, as Ranjit points out, French and Latin are mentioned BY name .. second, poetic effects like puns and deliberate alliteration (Overseeing Outsider, marvelous molpe, etc) are bound to the language they originated in, and would be lost in translation. Plus note the use of shag as their "f" word, a throwback of British English. that's it for now ...

1998-11-01 Alex David Groce #

Argues that the future people's British slang and puns imply the whole narrative is a translation, so such terms should be read as analogues of the original idioms. Grounds this in translation problems from Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton Beau de Marot, and closes with a John 8:32 quotation in reply to Greg Neyman's thread.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: The narrative is a translation of a fictional book

Hmmm... But, unless we assume that the people of the future are ALSO speaking British slang that's no longer used in 1999, we have to assume that the whole thing is a translation, and that slang terms, puns, etc. are analagous to the original (although, of course, really non-existent because Wolfe wrote it in English, but means it to be a translation of a fictional book) ones. This can be done, although it's very hard (see Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton Beau de Marot for a beautiful book on translation issues, such as these, although I don't recall him really getting into the issue of translations of fictional books...)

1998-11-01 Matthew Malthouse #

Argues that the Long Sun books' English stands for the Common Tongue, as Tolkien said his English represents the Common Tongue of Middle-earth, so names like Merriadoc and Samwise were changed to work in English. Responds to Greg Neyman's reply to Keiran about translation, citing his point that puns and alliteration such as Overseeing Outsider would be lost in translation and that French and Latin appear by name.
supports · genre-and-craft · theories: English in the Long Sun books represents the Common Tongue

Tolkien, in his exposition upon the Common Toungue and the languages of Middle Earth, says that he uses English to _represent_ the Common Tounge and as such names like Merriadoc, Samwise and Brandywine/Branduin have been changed to "work" rather than leave them in the "original". I haven't got a copy but the "real" names are given.

I've usually taken this to be a reasonable way of looking at much fantasy/sf so that the language doesn't intrude. It certainly covers the alliterative phrases in Whorl and the French & Latin as "analogue foreign/ancient languages in Urth.

Matthew

1998-11-02 Michael Straight #

Proposes that chems build themselves in a trance-like altered state after a male and female pair come together, so they cannot later explain how they made the fusion packs or other technology. Responds to Greg Neyman's point that chems must have access to the plans and assemble everything by hand, offering Typhon's secrecy plan as the reason for the gap.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon keeps chem technology secret through amnesia

I guess I'm imagining that a male and female get together and start making the little chem and go into an different mode of consciousness, after which they couldn't really tell you what they did or how they did it. All part of Typhon's plan to keep technology out of the wrong hands.

And OF COURSE they would demand privacy.

-Rostrum

1998-11-04 Derek Bell #

Supports Alex's point that the Long Sun's puns and slang, supposedly a translation, are hard to render, illustrating this with Hofstadter's stepping-stones metaphor of translation. Responds to Alex by citing Heidegger, Nietzsche and Derrida, whose wordplay resists translation, and Derrida's Glas with its French puns on Hegel and eagle.
supports · theories: Long Sun text is a translation within the fiction

You've reminded me of a metaphor that Hofstadter used in one of his books (I forget which). Translation can be thought of as like crossing a river on stepping stones. Here's a diagram:
(This is using a non-proportional font)

o X s

X o s

X s
o
X s
X o s

Imagine the "X"s as representing the spirit of a text to be translated. The "o"s represent words that are close in sense, but that have a different spirit. Meanwhile, there are stones further along the river ("s"s) that have a closer spirit, but a spirit "parallel" to the original.

I agree entirely with Alex that it's very hard to translate text that relies a lot on puns, allusions and other wordplay: some philosophers, such as Heidegger[1], Nietzsche & Derrida[2] have been difficult to translate because of this.

Derek

1998-11-04 Derek Bell #

Argues that Typhon gave the cargo lower technology for two reasons: control, and a survival advantage if the destination planet collapses into war, since old crafts like horseriding and blacksmithing would be slower to rebuild. Responds to a quoted claim about Chems running programs they cannot consciously access, offering the Amish as an analogy and joking about Chem sex manuals.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Typhon kept cargo technology low as insurance against collapse

I think Typhon had more than one motive for giving the cargo lower tech - control is the obvious one, but it might also give them an advantage if things go badly on the destination planet. A lot of knowledge and skills that were relatively common a century or two ago (horseriding, blacksmithing) are scarcer now. They still exist, but if some emergency occured that wiped out the infrastructure (a large, protracted non-nuclear war), it would take longer to get back to the level of technology than if the skills hadn't been cultivated.

Put it another way, the Amish *may* be Y2K insurance policy! :-)

Put it another way - describe how to walk - in detail. Now try to build a two-legged robot that walks like a human.

;-) I have this image of sex manuals for chems - or would that be in firmware?

Tercel
