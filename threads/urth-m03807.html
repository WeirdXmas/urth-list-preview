Respondents mostly gave reading advice: Gwern Branwen suggested Fourth Mansions and The Devil is Dead, Dan'l Danehy-Oakes proposed Apocalypses, Roy C. The thread then drifted into a side discussion of differing editions of The Devil Is Dead, which Gwern attributed to a late-arriving chapter, and into availability via Wildside Press print-on-demand. 2007-07-21 Gwern Branwen replying to Matthew Groves #

Recommends reading order for R. A. Lafferty, starting with Fourth Mansions and then The Devil is Dead and The Annals of Klepsis, warning that Klepsis is the most paranoid and hallucinatory. Responds to Matthew Groves, who noted Wolfe admires Lafferty and asked where a Wolfe fan should begin; the post gives its advice without citing evidence beyond the books' relative tone.
answers

Start with ''Fourth Mansions'' for a good almost-mainstream immersion in Lafferty-style paranoia and story telling, and follow it up with ''The Devil is Dead'' and ''The Annals of Klepsis'' (in that order; Klepsis is even more paranoid and hallucinatory than the previous two put together, so I wouldn't start with it).

2007-07-21 Roy C. Lackey replying to Matthew Groves #

Recommends R. A. Lafferty's novel The Devil Is Dead and its sequel Archipelago as a starting point for a Wolfe fan, noting that Lafferty is strongest in short stories. Replies to Matthew Groves's request for where to begin reading Lafferty, citing the author's own experience of being hooked by the first book.
answers

Echoing what others have said: Lafferty is probably most consistently at his best in short stories, but it was _The Devil is Dead_ that first hooked me. That's a fine book, as is its sequel, _Archipelago_. (Good luck trying to find it! <g>) There are even a couple of related short stories in the collections.

-Roy

2007-07-25 Adam Stephanides replying to Matthew Groves #

Recommends R. A. Lafferty's story collections to a newcomer, starting with Nine Hundred Grandmothers and then Strange Doings, and contrasts Lafferty's exuberance with Wolfe's deliberateness. Notes Lafferty's conservative religious and political views and the out-of-print state of his catalogue, citing the poster's own experience of the books.
informs

Lafferty is one of my very favorite authors. I would start with _Nine Hundred Grandmothers_, his first story collection; it's his most new reader-friendly collection, and probably also his most consistent. Then probably _Strange Doings_, his next collection. _Lafferty in Orbit_ is good too, but SD has the advantage that you can probably find it cheap by hunting in used book stores. (_Ringing Changes_, another collection, is also good.) I'll also second the recommendation for _Apocalypses_.

Keep in mind that Lafferty is a very different writer from Wolfe. If I were going to describe him in one word, it would be "exuberant," as opposed to Wolfe's deliberateness. Conversely, Lafferty's work doesn't require the close reading that Wolfe's does. Also, Lafferty was even more conservative, politically and religiously, than Wolfe (he was a creationist, for example), though this is almost invisible in much of his fiction, including the collections I suggested.

Lafferty's catalogue as a whole is in a sorry state. In the 80s and early 90s, there were several small presses publishing Lafferty. Then, for some reason, they all stopped at around the same time (leaving two series up in the air), and most of what they published is probably unobtainable now except at collector's prices, if at all. There are also a lot of good stories that were afaik never collected. Somebody needs to publish The Complete Stories of R. A. Lafferty. (I'd like to see all the novels in print, too, but that's probably asking for the moon.)

--Adam

2007-07-26 Matthew King replying to Adam Stephanides #

Shares a web introduction to R.A. Lafferty and argues that his fiction is never realistic but surreal, comparing its breakneck, illogical madcap pace to Bugs Bunny cartoons. Offers the quoted passage as the reason he was hooked, and warns newcomers not to take Lafferty's apparently ordinary science-fiction settings as ordinary.
informs

Here is the introduction to Lafferty that hooked me:

http://greatsfandf.com/AUTHORS/RALafferty.php

Lafferty is never "realistic." However ordinary his settings may seem when first we start one of his tales, we must remember that for a certainty they are surreal fantasies in the truest sense of those words. I think the most apt analogy for Lafferty's works is animated cartoons. Things in Lafferty tales happen at the breathless, breakneck pace--and with the madcap ad hoc paralogic--of a Bugs Bunny cartoon. We believe none of it because we are never for a second expected to believe any of it, any more than we are expected to "believe" what happens to Bugs and Elmer; we do not participate to behold "slices of life" but to behold madcap exaggerations and distortions of it. The single worst error a reader new to Lafferty can make is to think that a world Lafferty introduces in apparently ordinary science-fiction terms actually is (even by science-fiction standards) ordinary.
