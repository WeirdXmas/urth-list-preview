source of charge in Jolenta rape

Urth list · 3 messages · 2003-04-21 to 2003-04-21

2003urthThe Book of the New SunDan'l Danehy-OakesMichael Andre-DriussiChris P
The thread: Participants asked whether the charge of rape against Jolenta in Wolfe's Severian books comes from critics or from Severian himself. Michael Andre-Driussi argued Severian uses the word in The Urth of the New Sun, and so it is his own charge, answering Marc Aramini's claim that it is not really rape. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes questioned whether Severian and Thecla were ever lovers in the flesh, suggesting the sexual memories may be Thecla's imaginings, and noted Gurloes' view that abuse by the torturer is shameful. Chris argued Severian raises and then retracts the charge against himself, and that Jolenta is a one-dimensional figure unfairly treated by critics. The thread ended with three separate readings and no clear agreement.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: the rape charge originates with Severian's own wording in The Urth of the New Sun, so it is not a critic's invention
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: uncertain whether Severian and Thecla were ever lovers; the sexual memories may be Thecla's imaginings
  • Chris: Severian raises and retracts the charge against himself; Jolenta is one-dimensional and unfairly treated by critics
  • Marc Aramini (as quoted): the Jolenta rape is not really a rape

Themes: Unreliable narration, Suffering and sacrifice, Women, sex and gender

Argues that the charge of possible rape against Jolenta originates with Severian himself, who uses the word in The Urth of the New Sun, so it is not a fan or critic invention. Responds to Marc Aramini's claim that the Jolenta rape is not really a rape by pointing to Severian's own narration and its wording, then adds a note about eBay auctions and Sirius Fiction booklets.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian himself alludes to Jolenta rape

Before things spin any further away and out . . .

. . . the source of the charge of the possibility of Jolenta's rape . . .

. . . is none other than Severian himself, in URTH (iirc).

Thus it is not something whipped up by fan or critic.

Severian himself, writing his tale, alludes to it as possibly being rape. He uses that word.

2003-04-21 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that it is uncertain whether Severian and Thecla were ever lovers in the flesh, suggesting the sexual memories that appear after the perverted Eucharist in the wood may be Thecla's imaginings. Cites Severian's words 'tribidism' and 'algophilia' for Jolenta's effect, and Master Gurloes' claim that being abused by the torturer is shameful, which matters either way.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Severian's sexual memories of Thecla are her imaginings

It's also (imho) telling that he says -- using Wolfean bigwords -- that Jolenta, who brought out lesbian tendencies ('tribidism') in many women, brought out sadistic tendencies ('algophilia') in the torturer-hero.

In fact I think it's not at all certain whether or not Severian and Thecla were ever lovers in the flesh ... Very probable, but there remains the possibility that the "memories" of their sexual encounters (which only appear in the text _after_ the perverted Eucharist in the wood) may be of Thecla's imaginings rather than specific reality. The whole question must be considered alongside Master Gurloes' claim that to be "abused" by the torturer is the most shameful thing that can happen to a client ... which is relevant to this question no matter whether it actually happened or not.

--Blattid

2003-04-21 Chris P #

Argues that Severian himself raises the charge of Jolenta's rape and then retracts it against himself, and that Jolenta is a one-dimensional figure treated unfairly by critics. Grounds this in Talos's play where characters stand for abstractions, in a confusing passage where Severian says everyone takes advantage of Jolenta except possibly Talos, and in Severian's evasiveness about forcing Thecla.
disputes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: Jolenta is beauty in Talos's allegorical play; Severian forced Thecla

I agree that it's Severian himself who makes the charge, though I think he makes it against himself fairly immediately.

I also think what's been said about Jolenta is a bit unfair. It's a bit muddled to explain why I come to this conclusion, but bear in mind that everyone involved in Talos' play is rather overtly representing something else. Jolenta is "beauty", if you like, and Talos is clearly the "writer/poet" - what Talos does with Jolenta, and what he makes of her, is somewhat telling. This may not seem important, but it helps to explain a passage that is otherwise confusing (and which I cannot reproduce or look up), in which Severian basically says that she can't help the way she is, and implies that *everyone* is taking advantage of/raping her - with the possible exception of Talos.

This may or may not be in the same league, thematically, with the others. In any event, I was very sadly sympathetic to Jolenta considering how one-dimensional (by nature) a character she was.

I believe, however, that at some point Severian lets slip a statement, no longer than a sentence or two, that implies that (despite the fact that they were lovers at some point) he had forced Thecla. I'm not confident of that as an assertion, but there was definitely violence involved; Severian through most of those books is very evasive w.r.t. Thecla, and one is often forced to re-evaluate things he's said earlier in light of things he says later.

Chris
