These are the jokes, pt. 2; more inhumi mysteries

Whorl list · 3 messages · 2000-04-13 to 2000-04-17

2000whorlThe Book of the Short SunAdam StephanidesAlastair ReynoldsMark Millman
The thread: Adam Stephanides asked about two jokes in On Blue's Waters: Horn's pun on "inhumations" and his "sober fact" remark about the swimming woman who turns out to be gilled, plus what Horn means by unearthing an inhumi to warn others and why the Neighbors are suspected. Mark Millman disputed the pun, arguing from Merriam-Webster and 1913 Webster's that inhumation simply means burial and does not imply burial alive, and suggested an inhumi warned by a released inhumu might avoid Gaon. Alastair Reynolds then offered a separate wordplay point that a Dorp sailor's "mer-frau" is really the Dutch "mevrouw", a mishearing joke, without responding to the earlier argument. The thread stayed unresolved, with the inhumation dispute left as a dictionary-based disagreement.
  • Adam Stephanides: sees inhumations as a deliberate pun and asks what Horn's inhumi warning and Neighbor origin mean
  • mark millman: says inhumation just means burial, so the grim pun reading fails
  • Alastair Reynolds: reads "mer-frau" as a Dutch mishearing of "mevrouw" in Dorp

Themes: Unreliable narration, Names and their meanings, Water, the sea and drowning

2000-04-13 Adam Stephanides #

Points out two jokes in The Urth of the New Sun... wait, in Return to the Whorl's predecessor—On Blue's Waters: Horn's use of "inhumations" as a pun and his "sober fact" remark about the wounded swimming woman, which turns out to be a gilled woman under the sea. Then asks what Horn means by digging up an inhumi to warn others and why he suspects the inhumi were made by the Neighbors.
questions · close-textual-reading

There is very little humor in OBW (a trait it shares with most of Wolfe's books), but a while ago Kieran Cleary posted one humorous bit. Here are two more.

1) On p. 126 Horn refers to the burials alive of inhumi practiced in Gaon as "inhumations." I don't know whether this is an actual word meaning "burials alive" or a coinage by Wolfe on analogy with "exhumation." Either way it's a pun, if a grim one.

2) Recalling his speculations about what had gotten on board the boat and been wounded by Babbie, Horn tells us: "For a moment I thought of the woman I had shot, swimming league upon league after our boat, intent upon revenge. If I were spinning a fireside tale for children here, no doubt it would be so; but I am recounting sober fact, and I knew that any such thing was utterly impossible." (136) In "sober fact," of course, it turns out to have been a beautiful naked woman with gills, living under the sea with a sea-goddess who is training her as a siren. This is a joke by Wolfe, on both himself and Horn, and a good one.

The passage on p. 126 in which "inhumations" are mentioned is a cryptic one, in more ways than one. First, Horn ponders: "Would it be effective for us to dig up one of the recent inhumations and release him to warn the others?" Warn the others of what? Of Horn's threat to reveal Krait's secret, presumably. But what does Horn hope to accomplish by this? We have no clue (this is before the war with Han has even begun). Then Horn briefly speculates that the inhumi may not be "natural creatures," but instead may have been created by the Neighbors! What does Horn know about the inhumi, or the Neighbors, that leads him to entertain this possiblity even briefly?

--Adam

2000-04-13 Mark Millman #

Argues that Wolfe's word "inhumations" in Horn's account of living burials is not a grim pun, since dictionaries define inhumation simply as burial without implying burial alive. Supports this with Merriam-Webster and 1913 Webster's entries, and suggests inhumi warned by a released inhumu may avoid Gaon, leaving Krait's secret aside.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading

At 09:20 am -0700 on Thursday 13 April 2000, Adam Stephanides

It is a grim pun, but it may not be quite as bad as Adam suggests. "Inhumation" means "a burial", but doesn't imply that it's a living burial. Merriam-Webster on-line (at http://www.m-w.com) gives:

Main Entry: in·hume
Pronunciation: in-'hyüm
Function: transitive verb
Inflected Form(s): in·humed; in·hum·ing
Etymology: probably from French inhumer, from Medieval Latin inhumare, from Latin in- + humus earth -- more at HUMBLE
Date: 1604
: BURY, INTER
- in·hu·ma·tion /"in-hyü-'mA-sh&n/ noun

and the 1913 _Webster's Revised Unabridged Dictionary_ (at http://humanities.uchicago.edu/forms_unrest/webster.form.html) has:

Inhumation (Page: 764)
In`hu*ma"tion (?), n. [Cf. F. inhumation.]
1. The act of inhuming or burying; interment.
2. (Old Chem.) The act of burying vessels in warm earth in order to expose their contents to a steady moderate heat; the state of being thus exposed.
3. (Med.) Arenation.

Neither dictionary ascribes "inhumation" a connotation of live burial, and I don't recall ever seeing it used that way.

I'd always thought that the inhumu released from his grave would warn the others of the harsh treatment they'd face in Gaon. No doubt an in- humu forewarned could avoid capture and burial, but Horn likely hopes that at least some inhumi will avoid Gaon so as not to face such risks. I don't think that Krait's secret comes into play here.

I suspect that we won't find this out until we read _In Green's Jungles_.

Nacre

2000-04-17 Alastair Reynolds #

Argues that a sailor from Dorp who addresses Seawrack as "mer-frau" in On Blue's Waters is actually saying the Dutch word "mevrouw", a polite form of address for a woman, so the line is a joke on Horn's mishearing. Offers this as a minor wordplay observation about the Dutch-flavoured community of Dorp, with no response to any earlier argument.
informs · source-and-allusion · theories: Mer-frau is a mishearing of Dutch mevrouw

another joke in OBW:

When one of the sailors from Dorp (the community with a Dutch-type feel to it) meets Horn and Seawrack, he addresses Seawrack as "mer-frau" (ie sea-lady). It's a sort of joke because what he actually says, rather than what Horn thinks he says, is presumably the identical sounding "mevrouw" which is simply a polite Dutch way of addressing a woman.

Not hilarious, but worth a mention I thought...

Al
