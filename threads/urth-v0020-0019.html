1998-10-15 Michael Straight #

Argues that a reader may use or ignore an author's explanation of his work, depending on whether the reader wants facts, the author's aims, or fun. Answers John Regehr's question by citing Wolfe's anthology introduction that explains Westwind, which the poster ignores in favor of the story's unresolved ambiguity.
answers · theories: Authors' own commentary is optional for interpretation

I think the lit crit community says yes, no, yes, yes, no, yes, no, no, maybe, fish, power.

I say listen to the author and other people's criticism if it interests you and if it doesn't, ignore it. In the introduction to one of his anthologies, Wolfe pretty much comes out and says what the story "Westwind" is about. I think the story is better with the ambiguity unresolved, and therefore try to ignore what Wolfe had to say.

I guess it depends on why you're reading. If you're trying to learn physics or virtue from an author, paying attention to external helps is probably a good idea. If you want to understand what an author is trying to say, what he writes about his work is probably of interest. If you're reading for fun, I'd go with your criteria.

-Rostrum
