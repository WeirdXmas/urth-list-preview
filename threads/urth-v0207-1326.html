Silkicide

Urth list · 7 messages · 2002-07-11 to 2002-07-13

2002urthThe Book of the Long SunWilliam H. AnsleyAndy RobertsonJason IngramDan'l Danehy-OakesMichael Andre-Driussi
The thread: Participants debated whether Silk's arm wounds in the Long Sun books were self-inflicted mourning gashes or a suicide attempt, and whether Pig or the Gaonese could have killed Hyacinth. Jason Ingram argued the wounds were self-inflicted in grief but short of a real attempt, pointing to the knife memory and Silk's blood for Krait. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes agreed Pig did not kill Hyacinth, citing Silk's absence from his post, and treated the wounds as undecidable. Michael Andre-Driussi claimed the Mainframe actions settle that Silk died and was combined with Horn, and William Ansley pressed him to explain his referee metaphor and the Silk-23 reference. The thread ended with the claim about Silk's fate unresolved and the exchange turning to Andre-Driussi's reading.
  • Jason Ingram: Silk's wounds were self-inflicted in mourning, not a true suicide attempt
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: wounds undecidable; Pig did not kill Hyacinth, Silk gashed himself in grief
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: Mainframe actions show Silk died and was combined with Horn
  • William Ansley: challenges Andre-Driussi to clarify his referee metaphor and Silk-23 claim

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Robots, androids and artificial life

2002-07-11 Jason Ingram #

Argues that Silk's wounds in the Long Sun books were self-inflicted during a mourning frenzy rather than a true suicide attempt, since a real attempt would have succeeded. Grounds this in the narrator's recollection of recoiling from a knife after arriving in the Whorl, which the poster reads as circumstantial evidence the knife was used on Silk.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk commits suicide by self-inflicted wounds

I read Silk's wounds as being self-inflicted; but it seems to me that an actual suicide attempt by Silk would have been successful. I felt that he gashed himself in some sort of mourning frenzy, which was consistent with a substantial self-destructive urge and the loss of a will to live.

At one point the narrator remembers (or is reported to have remembered) that, shortly after his transfer to the whorl, he felt a sort of horror for a knife that he was holding and cast it away. I interpreted this emotion as circumstantial evidence that the knife had been used against Silk.

2002-07-11 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Silk's arm wounds are self-inflicted but cannot be settled as mourning gashes or a suicide attempt, and that the Horn-to-Silk slips in the narrator's speech may be genuine or inserted by the redactors. Supports the view by weighing the depth and the word "some" against suicide, and by comparing the redactors' reconstruction to the historical Jesus problem in the Gospels.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Silk's arm wounds are self-inflicted but ambiguous; Silk persona slips may be redactor insertions

Yes; I reread that passage last night. I don't think there is any real doubt that his arm wounds are self-inflicted; the only serious question is whether they are mourning gashes, as we know to be practiced in Viron, or an attempt at self- slaughter.

In favor of suicide is the observation that some of the wounds are quite deep; against it is the very word "some -- it implies a frenzied hacking and not a concerted attempt to sever the arteries.

I'm not sure whether we _can_ know whether Silk was attempting suicide; I'm watching for any further clues as I read.

Also of note: throughout his parts of the text, beginning in OBW, the Narrator makes occasional slips of the tongue and pen which indicate the presence of the Silk persona -- for example, referring to his sons, Hide and Horn; or talking about Nettle but using the word "Hyacinth." Anyway, the point here is that he begins to make these slips almost immediately after the transference of the Horn persona to the Silk body.

... unfortunately, this being the "reconstructed from his words" part written by the "redactors," we can't know if they are reporting what he has actually said or inserting them for "consistency." There are places in the main narrative when he is recording his own conversations and reports such slips with no comment, and so one might suppose that he reported similarly when talking to the kids about what happened on the _Whorl_; contrariwise, one might suppose that, in reconstructing the events on the _Whorl_ from (there memories of) his comments on them, they inserted such slips so as to be consistent with the primary narrative. Indeed, if one wants to be paranoid enough about it, one can even suppose that they inserted _all_ such slips, because the Narrator (striving to preserve his belief in himself as Horn) would certainly not have kept them.

All of which, again, brings me to something I said months ago: that one of the glories and annoyances of the "Short Sun" books is that our attempts to get from them to "what actually happened" is not a bad analogy to attempts to tease out the "historical Jesus" or the "authentic kerygma" from the
received Gospels...

--Blattid

2002-07-12 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Pig did not kill Hyacinth, and that the Gaonese led by Hari Mau could have been the killers only if they had found Silk, which they did not. Answers Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's question about textual evidence by citing Pig's report that Silk was absent from his place, and concludes Hyacinth simply died and Silk gashed himself in grief.
answers · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Pig did not kill Hyacinth; Gaonese under Hari Mau arrived after Horn's spirit entered Silk; Hyacinth died naturally and Silk gashed himself in grief

Sorry, I had that backwards. Kansas is in sepia.

[...]

Pig mentions that he went to Silk's place and didn't find him there. I regard this as evidence that he didn't kill Hyacinth -- and besides, I don't think his rider would have permitted it.

If one wanted to say someone had killed Hyacinth, one could suppose it was the Gaonese led by Hari Mau, who came out to Endroad looking for Silk and didn't find him; however, I would be inclined to suspect that they didn't find him because they arrived after Horn's spirit entered Silk's body and the newly formed Narrator left the building. At this point, I think Hyacinth just died, and Silk gashes himself in grief.

--Blattid

2002-07-12 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that the question of whether Silk died in Silkicide is ambiguous, but that the Mainframe-linked actions of the figures in the story, such as removing Silk's non-corporeal parts and transferring Horn's, make the outcome clear. Grounds this in the speakers' talk of combining two persons together with those concrete actions, and in the later download of Silk-23 from Mainframe.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Silk and Horn were merged in Silkicide

While we have pondered "did he or didn't he?" over years past, and will do so into the future, still I think we can all agree that it is ambiguous.

Likewise I'm reasonably sure that we can all agree that we are in the bleachers of the arena, and the referees made the call: they said the words (talk of combining two persons), they backed it up with actions (taking out the non-corporeal parts of Silk aged 40-something; transferring in Horn's non-corporeal parts; the download from Mainframe of Silk-23 later on may have been their doing or not, if it happened, that is).

The umpires made their call. This is not ambiguous.

=mantis=
