Piteous Gate- one last rehash

The thread: Participants debated what caused the disturbance at the Piteous Gate in The Shadow of the Torturer, with b sharp arguing it was an attempt on Severian's life by Agia and Hethor rather than merely soldiers blocking the road. mournings glory disputed b sharp's reading of Agia's exchange, proposing that her word "afterward" refers to events after Saltus Fair rather than soon after the avern duel. b sharp rejected that reading, held that the Piteous Gate puzzle is masked on purpose, and later pressed that the Claw of the Conciliator incident be left open rather than settled by Tony's Uhlan explanation. The thread ended with b sharp floating a Baldanders and Juturna theory without a clear resolution.
  • b sharp: the Piteous Gate disturbance was an attempt on Severian's life by Agia and Hethor
  • mournings glory: the word "afterward" points to the time after Saltus Fair, not the avern episode
  • b sharp: Wolfe deliberately masks the Piteous Gate puzzle behind the source of the creatures
  • b sharp: the Claw of the Conciliator incident should stay unexplained rather than accept Tony's Uhlan explanation
  • b sharp: a Baldanders and Juturna mating theory could explain the Piteous Gate incident

2006-05-14 b sharp #

Proposes that the disturbance at the Piteous Gate at the end of The Shadow of the Torturer was an attempt on Severian's life by Agia and Hethor, not merely soldiers blocking the road. Grounds this on an exchange in Sword where Agia does not deny trying to kill Severian between the avern duel and Saltus Fair, and asks whether other interpretations exist.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Piteous Gate disturbance was Agia and Hethor's attempt on Severian

In the vaults of this archinve there has been a fair amount of discussion centered on the disturbance at the Piteous Gate that ends Shadow. I had always felt it was something mysterious and worth knowing, as did some others. Mantis and Tony Ellis and perhaps others argued vehemently that it was merely the action of soldiers blocking the road and I think it was left at that.

I found a passage which convinces me otherwise. When Severian first talks to Agia in Casdoe's cottage in Sword, they have this exchange:

S- "You've been trying to kill me ever since I glimpsed you in the crowd at Saltus"
A- " Is that an accusation? Yes."
S- "You're lying."
A- "What do you mean?"
S- "Only that you were trying to kill me before Saltus"
A- "With the avern. Yes, of course"
S- "And afterward. Agia, I know who Hethor is"

I think this is author misdirection because on first reading I know I was then more interested in Severian's extended insight about Hethor. But the first part of that sentence says that Agia (with Hethor's help implied) tried to kill Severian somewhere in the time between the avern duel and Saltus Fair, which she doesn't deny.

I find no plausible setting for this attempt except the disturbance at the Piteous Gate. So Severian, in retrospect, has deduced that the cause of the disturbance, the appearance of some undescribed creature, was an attempt on his life by Agia and Hethor. Is there another possible interpretation?

-bsharp

2006-05-15 mournings glory replying to b sharp #

Agrees that the disturbance at the Piteous Gate may be a narrative dramatic pause, but argues that b sharp's quoted exchange with Agia does not support it. Proposes that the word "afterward" refers to the time after the Saltus fair, when Severian and Jonas are attacked by Hethor's notules, not soon after the avern episode.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Piteous Gate disturbance is a dramatic pause; "Afterward" refers to the Saltus fair attack

Agreed, and perhaps exactly what is meant when Severian writes about "certain dramatic pauses which shall signal to you that something of import is about to occur."

I believe Mr. Lackey has postulated similarly, and while I think it's as credible as any other theory, I'm not sure your particular quote bolsters it in any fashion. You're assuming, I believe, that "afterward" comes relatively soon after the avern episode, whereas I believe it's referring to "after" the Saltus fair, when Severian and Jonas are attacked by Hethor's notules.

2006-05-15 b sharp replying to b sharp #

Disputes the reading that Agia's exchange with Severian refers to events after Saltus Fair, arguing instead that it means attempts on his life between the avern duel and the fair. Walks through the intervening scenes, including Agia scratching at him in the cell and Hethor asking to carry Terminus Est, and concludes Wolfe masks the Piteous Gate puzzle behind the source of the creatures.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Agia's attempts on Severian occur before Saltus Fair; Wolfe masks Piteous Gate puzzle with creatures' source

mourningsglory responds-

I must respectfully disagree that Severian could be refering to events after Saltus Fair such as the man-ape cave, the notule attack, the antechamber slug and the salamander. If he were, Severian's and Agia's exchange would read, simplified:

Agia: I've been trying to kill you since the Saltus Fair. Severian: Liar, you've been trying to kill me since the Saltus Fair.

Which makes no sense. He must be refering to an attempt or attempts before Saltus Fair but after the avern duel. I think the only legitimate objection to my analysis would be if there was another plausible attempt on Severian's life in that time period. I don't have my books here but the episodes in that time frame I remember are: Severian in the recovery room, Severian with Agia and Agilus in his cell, the execution, walking with Dorcas, performing the Talos play, packing up the play properties and then walking to the Piteous Gate.

The only other candidates for murder attempts in this period I can think of are very weak: 1. In the cell, Agia attacks Severian scratching at his eyes, and grabbing at his sabretache. 2. Later, as they are walking toward the Piteous Gate, Hethor asks if he can carry Severian's sword. I don't think it is in Hethor's repertoire to be swinging swords. But if Hethor knows an attack is coming soon, I can see why he would want to take Terminus Est from Severian.

Severian phrases his statements in the exchange to knock her off balance (as he has physically, a couple times) by calling her a liar after she has told the truth. I think Wolfe's purpose is much the same, to knock his reader off balance so the solution to one puzzle (Piteous Gate) is masked by the solution to another (the source of the creatures).

-bsharp

2006-05-23 b sharp replying to b sharp #

Argues that the Piteous Gate incident in The Claw of the Conciliator is deliberately left unexplained, so readers should look for hidden hints rather than accept Tony's Uhlan explanation as obvious. Responds to Tony's last post and points to Severian's vague references to the monster, man-ape soldiers and the cruel uhlans as the gap that needs filling.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Piteous Gate incident was deliberately blacked out

I think Tony makes good points in his last post. He pokes a few holes in what I thought was an airtight case.

The problem as I see it is that Tony can't see the holes in his own theory, which I believe Roy and others have most eloquently presented (Feb 2006, The Piteous Gate).

I think Tony considers his Uhlan explanation so clear and obvious that it explains why the incident isn't discussed further in detail. So that satisfies him.

But so many others disagree and feel more explanation is needed. Severian doesn't mention the appearance of a monster or man-ape soldiers swarming toward Hethor or the cruel behavior of 5 uhlans who almost got Dorcas killed or any explanation whatsoever. It is always "the incident" or "the disturbance". If we ask "why didn't Severian or anyone else explain the incident?" the only sure answer we can make is because Wolfe won't let them (or per Tony, Wolfe doesn't see any need).

To me it is like seeing a blacked out portion on a page of text. We can dismiss it as nothing, just part of the text. Or we can deduce that someone blacked it out on purpose, wonder about the purpose and wonder what was erased.

If Wolfe is true to form on this mystery, there is one big, fat hint hiding somewhere in the text, out of context, and a lot of small vague hints. I guess if we can't find it then Tony might be right.

-bsharp
Perhaps if it can't be found, then Tony is right.

2006-05-24 b sharp replying to b sharp #

Asks whether a known theory about Baldanders mating with the undine Juturna at Piteous Gate could be the basis of a fan's idea about the incident there. Links the averns to keeping out undines, raises the question of how Juturna passes them, and compares her to the Lady of the Lake.
questions · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Baldanders mates with Juturna at Piteous Gate; Averns keep out undines; Juturna is the Lady of the Lake

mournings glory's theory on the incident at Piteous Gate is a good one.

Could the extant theory it is semi-based be Borski's idea that Baldanders mates with Juturna shortly after the group separation at the gate, thus giving him his underwater breathing ability? Hildegrin calls it "the affair at the gate". Watching Baldanders and Juturna mate might make me run away screaming also ;-).

I think Maxellindas' uncle makes reference to the undines in relation to manatees (and let's not forget nixies!) so I've long thought the averns were to keep out undines. How does Juturna get past the averns? I like the idea that it isn't Dorcas returning his sword. And, in Arthurian terms, Juturna (Diuturna) makes a very nice Lady Of The Lake, doesn't she?

-bsharp
