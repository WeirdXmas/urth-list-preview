PEACE: Smart's relatives

Urth list · 6 messages · 2002-10-18 to 2002-10-20

2002urthThe Book of the New SunStone OxAdam StephanidesAndy RobertsonRoy C. LackeyMichael Andre-Driussi
The thread: Readers debated whether Smart's relatives in Peace were older or younger than him, and whether his family and inheritance make him isolated and self-made. Michael Andre-Driussi argued for older, unknown kin with a rough lifespan calculation, while Roy Lackey argued the parents conveniently died soon after college and that Smart is a cold, manipulative liar who used Tilly, Olivia and Weer. Andre-Driussi and Andy Robertson pushed back with demographic reasoning and the Ohio family network, and Adam Stephanides disputed the inheritance and convenience claims using the text. StoneOx17 added a separate claim that Smart lived with Tilly for months and lied about the salary to hide money. The thread ended without resolution, with the disagreement mostly about how much the text supports these inferences.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: relatives were older, Smart is self-made with unknown kin in Ohio
  • Roy C. Lackey: parents died conveniently, Smart is a liar and cold mercenary
  • Andy Robertson: average life expectancy is not a limit, historical change lies in child survival
  • Adam Stephanides: 'closer to the farm' is ambiguous, farm land in the 1920s did not bring wealth
  • StoneOx17: Smart lived with Tilly for months and lied about salary to hide money, chief suspect in poisoning

Themes: Fathers, mothers and children, Power, rulers and politics, Suffering and sacrifice

2002-10-18 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Smart's relatives in Peace must have been older rather than younger, using a rough lifespan calculation for a man born in 1900 to show any helper from his twenties would be elderly at his death. Builds on this to suggest Smart is self-made like Baldanders, so unknown kin likely exist, and that leaving the factory to Weer serves Smart's interests as an owner and as a tribute to Olivia's family.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Smart's helpers were older relatives, not younger; Smart is self-made like Baldanders; Smart should leave the factory to Weer

I'm re-reading PEACE now, taking a section per day in a Weer-ish sort of way. What a neat book!

Anyway, the question of Smart having closer relatives than Weer. Yes, Smart does talk about the help he had from relatives, but that was when he was 20-something, and it is not likely that those relatives were younger -- rather, that they were older.

Let's say Smart was born in 1900, when life expectancy for a man was around 48 (iirc), and died in 1963 at age 63. Any relative who was old enough to help him when he was 20-something would be 70 or 80-something after he died!

Smart seems like a self-made man (many ways similar to Baldanders). If any of those relatives had managed to put him onto a job, he probably would have maintained ties to those relatives; but since they didn't, he doesn't. He found Mr. Tilly on his own, after riding milk trains; he found Olivia on his own, with some debt to Professor Peacock who knew her/courted her first. From my figuring above there could be three generations of relatives that he knows nothing about, and likewise, those myriads of Smarties don't know a fraction about the factory that even a cold house worker knows, let alone a long-term engineer like Weer.

Smart knows that Olivia loved Weer. Smart knows that Weer is the last of the Weers. Smart knows that he couldn't have made the factory without Olivia's help. However unfriendly Smart has been toward Weer over the last 25 years of Smart's life (make that 35 years!), it seems to me that leaving the factory to Weer is in his best interests as an owner, and in his best interests as a tribute to his wife and her family (i.e., the family network that was able to further his career).

=mantis=

2002-10-19 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Smart's supposed helpful relatives cannot have been older, since his parents seem to have died conveniently soon after college, and that Smart is a liar and a cold mercenary who used Tilly, Olivia and Weer. Grounds the case in textual details: Smart's farm roots and the inheritance that likely funded his plant, his claim of Tilly's generous payment versus the mention of a salary, and Olivia's treatment of Weer.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Smart's parents died conveniently to his advantage; Smart is a cold, self-serving liar

Those "cousins" he mentioned would have been his own age.

We know almost nothing about Smart's life beyond the few autobiographical details he divulges in the course of telling the Tilly tale. We know from nine-year-old Den that Smart couldn't have been much more than twenty-five when he told that story. If we assume that the details are true (and if we don't, where are we?!), then somehow _both_ of Smart's parents conspired to die in the few years since he graduated from college (and I've brought this up before). To me that's a red flag; too many people died too conveniently to Smart's advantage--his parents, Tilly, Olivia, and possibly Peacock.

How is it that Smart even knew Peacock? The most obvious connection is the university at which Peacock taught. Smart had likely gone to school there. The university was just thirty-five miles from Cassionsville, and Smart grew up in the area. We know this because he said he didn't want to buy Tilly's drugstore because he "wanted to get back closer to the farm" (148). Assuming Smart had no siblings, his parent's death left him owning a chunk of real estate that was worth a tidy sum of money to him in the go-go 1920s. That has to be a source of his start-up money for the plant; Smart was no farmer. So those relatives were not "distant", geographically.

BTW, why would Smart have assumed that Peacock might have told Olivia that Tilly died, before Smart had even met her? (148)

More than once in the course of the Tilly tale, Smart remarks upon how handsomely Tilly had paid him. He's a liar. He knew Tilly less than a week, and I doubt that he was paid by the day, because he mentions a "salary" (151). I doubt that Smart ever collected a nickel from him.

I'm not so sure Olivia loved Weer. The first page of the second section doesn't read that way to me. She was stuck with him and tried to make the best of it. And I have a much darker view of Smart. He was a mercenary, cold-blooded s.o.b. who used people for his own ends. I don't think he had a philanthropic bone in his body. When he sold Olivia's house it didn't become the Olivia Weer-Smart Memorial Library. And if he thought well at all of his nephew, he would have advanced his career during all those years, grooming him as his successor.

-Roy

2002-10-19 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Smart could plausibly have a family network in Ohio, so his parents' death and in-laws need not mean he was isolated, and that Smart's connection to Peacock explains how he knows Olivia. Responds to Roy's point about Smart's supposed cousins by working through the parents' ages, Janet Turner's accent, and the in-laws Smart mentions in Peace.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Smart has in-laws and siblings in Ohio

Yes, and if still alive they would be past their life-expectancy age of 50. As for Smart's parents sudden death, if they were in their 20s when they conceived him, then they would be in their mid-40s when he went to Florida. Rather close to the estimated lifespan.

Maybe. With geography, "closer" is relative with regard to scale. He was in Florida at the time! The Smart farm could be in the New England states or in Illinois and he would still be closer to it in Ohio than he was in Florida.

Janet Turner, ala Cleo the Seal Girl, has an accent that places her in Pennsylvania or Ohio. She is from the multi-state region, maybe even from the same state of Ohio.

Ah, but he must have had siblings, no? He mentions "in-laws," after all, among those relatives trying to help him get started.

I thought we were agreed that Smart could not have started up the factory without Olivia: "her" money, her brother's money, her family's influence in the town.

Because Tilly's death is the circumstance that brings Smart, out of a job, all the way from Florida to Ohio. Smart has just arrived in town; he is wearing a suit and shirt that he just bought at Macafee's department store. He is friends with Peacock (just as he wrote letters to his professors looking for a job, he wrote to Peacock, whether Peacock was his professor or just a friend at college -- and Peacock probably mentioned Bledsoe's drugstore), he knows that Peacock is courting Olivia, he knows that Olivia has invited Smart to the birthday party (of Macaffee at Olivia's house -- very Auntie Mame!) at Peacock's request. Thus, he knows that Peacock has spoken of him to Olivia, and supposes that Peacock might have mentioned this detail.

While I agree she was stuck with him and tried to make the best of it, I do not think this cancels out any familial love, or the possibility of the same.

2002-10-19 Stone Ox #

Argues that Smart lived with Tilly for at least six months rather than a week, and that Smart is lying about the salary to hide money from Tilly's medicine business. Supports this with seasonal clues in the Tilly tale: oranges in season suggests winter while 90-degree heat after midnight suggests summer, which the poster calls deliberate and makes Smart the chief suspect in Tilly's poisoning.
proposes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Smart lived with Tilly for six months; Smart poisoned Tilly; Smart lying about salary to hide medicine money

I agree he's a liar, but I think that's not all he's lying about. I think Smart lived with Tilly for at least six months. Correct me if I'm wrong about either Gulf coast weather or seasonal fruit, but in the Tilly tale, oranges are locally in season (and thus it's winter) and it's 90 degrees after midnight (and thus it's summer). Wolfe certainly knows about orange season (recall oranges in Den's Christmas stocking) and also about Gulf coast weather (Wolfe went to college in Texas). I don't think this is an oversight ... I think it's a deliberate clue, as was Hannah's supposed trip to Boston in the diary written by Gold. Tilly died at least six months after Smart arrived, this makes Smart the chief suspect in Tilly's poisoning.

Of course, Smart is likely lying about the handsome salary as well, to explain where all the money he got from taking over Mr. Tilly's carney medicine business came from. After all, even if he kept the books in the pharmacy's regular business honestly, I don't see why he would have kept records of having prescribed illegal medicines.

- Stone Ox

2002-10-19 Andy Robertson #

Argues that life expectancy figures are averages rather than limits, so a man who reaches twenty could still expect to reach around sixty, and that the historical change lies in how many children survive to adulthood. Responds to Roy's Peace-based calculation that Smart's helpers would be seventy or eighty by his death, using general demographic reasoning rather than the novel's text.
disputes

I have been kind of out of things for the past few weeks, but I will just drop a note about life expectancy:

It has **always** been the case that someone who reaches 20 can expect to get to 60. This is so now in very poor countries. Life expectancies are averages, not limits.

What has changed is the proportion of people who get to puberty. There used to be a *lot* of children dying before reproductive age. Fewer now.

hartshorn

2002-10-20 Adam Stephanides #

Disputes Roy Lackey's claim that Smart's parents and Tilly died conveniently for his benefit, arguing that the phrase "closer to the farm" is ambiguous and that Smart could not have foreseen Tilly's relatives keeping him on. Points out that farm land in the 1920s did not bring wealth and that surviving relatives need not have lived near the parents, citing the pharmacy passage on pages 158-9.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Smart inherited wealth from parents' farm

For his parents, see below. As for Tilly, Smart couldn't have known that Tilly's relatives would keep him on at the store; he'd have had greater reason to think Tilly's death would leave him jobless again.

"Get back closer to the farm" is an ambiguous phrase; it could simply mean that he wanted to live in a more rural area. If he were specifically talking about his parents' farm, he'd be more likely to say something like "wanted to go home," imo.

Even if Smart's parents did own a farm, there's no reason to think Smart would have gotten rich by inheriting it; the 1920s were not "go-go" years for agriculture.

Even if his parents did live in the area, his surviving relatives at the time of his death need not have.

A couple of minor points in response to mantis:

Or could that refer to his parents' brothers- and sisters-in-law?

Um, no; as mentioned above, Smart took over the pharmacy after Tilly died; and has seemingly been doing so for some time (158-9, Harper & Row hc).

--Adam
