Typhon sleeping or dead?

Urth list · 4 messages · 2002-07-15 to 2002-07-16

2002urthThe Book of the New SunRoy C. LackeyMichael Andre-DriussiJames JordanAdam Stephanides
The thread: Readers debated whether Typhon in Wolfe's Book of the New Sun is sleeping or dead. Michael Andre-Driussi argued the ambiguity is deliberate, with apparently mundane deaths and revivals proving supernatural and tied to the Claw. Roy C. Lackey countered that Typhon was dead, citing Severian's prophecy that he would die on the red couch and quoting the chapter text. James Jordan conceded Typhon may be dead but argued this does not make him Silk's father, questioning whether death is final given the Increate's plans. Adam Stephanides suggested the prophecy's wording may reflect how an audience would understand it, not literal fact, drawing on Catholic views of Biblical language. The thread ended without consensus, with the textual disagreement left open.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: Typhon's state is deliberately ambiguous; apparent deaths and revivals prove supernatural
  • Roy C. Lackey: Typhon was dead, not sleeping, per Severian's prophecy and the chapter text
  • James Jordan: Typhon may be dead, but that does not make him Silk's father; death may not be final
  • Adam Stephanides: the prophecy's wording may be audience-oriented rather than literal, as with Biblical language

Themes: Ghosts, dreams and visions, Death and resurrection, Catholic faith and sacraments

2002-07-15 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Typhon's state in the Book of the New Sun is deliberately ambiguous, and that the book's recurring pattern is of apparently mundane deaths and revivals turning out to be supernatural and tied to the Claw. Supports this by citing Triskele, Dorcas and Severian as cases where the surface reading proves wrong, responding to Blattid's suspended-animation reading.
supports · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon's state is deliberately ambiguous; the Claw explains apparent deaths and revivals

I would not deny the planned ambiguity, I would not deny the reading that says it is suspended animation, after all, and thus the time of Typhon's revival is purely a fluke, an authorial coincidence to cause a little picaresque action in an otherwise dull wilderness. <g>

OTOH, I think a strong thread in TBOTNS is about the mystery of the Claw, and how a lot of dead/live things that seemed to be more mundane (Triskele, Dorcas, et cetera) ultimately resolve as being supernatural and due to the Claw. So initially Triskele wasn't really dead (but yes, he was recently dead); Dorcas was insane and nearly drowned (no, she was dead for decades); Severian wounded by the avern (nope, he was killed); and so on.

2002-07-16 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Typhon in The Urth of the New Sun was dead rather than in suspended animation, citing Severian's prophecy on Mt. Typhon that Typhon would die on the red couch. Responds to Blattid's remark that the revival is a planned ambiguity by quoting the passage from the chapter as textual evidence.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon was dead, not in suspended animation

Okay, then I will deny it: Typhon was dead, not in SA; the text says so. Sev prophesied (it was easy, since it came from memory) while on Mt. Typhon (URTH, Chapter XXXIX):

"'I'll tell you this. This morning I've been studying this mountain and the speed with which the work here is going forward. From those things, I know Typhon has only a short time to live. He'll die on the red couch where he lies now; and without his word, no one will dare to draw aside the curtain. One after another will creep away. The machines that dig like men will return for fresh instructions, but they won't receive them, and in time the curtain itself will fall to dust.'"

-Roy

2002-07-16 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that Severian's prophecy that Typhon will die on the red couch may be phrased in terms his audience would understand, not literal fact. Offers this as an alternative to Roy Lackey's claim that Typhon was dead and not sleeping, citing Catholic views of Biblical language as the reason for the wording.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Severian's 'he'll die' is accommodated speech for his audience

OTOH, this could also be Severian speaking in terms appropriate to the audience, in line with Catholic views of Biblical language. That is, it would be easier for them to understand "he'll die" than "he'll go into suspended animation for a thousand years, and then be briefly revived, but then I'll kill him."

--Adam
