Publishers Weekly on Home Fires (mild spoilers)

Urth list · 4 messages · 2010-12-17 to 2010-12-17

2010urthDan'l Danehy-OakesJames Wynn
The thread: James Wynn shared a Publishers Weekly review of Home Fires, which describes a romance-mystery with time dilation, cyborgs and brain downloads, and joked that Cory Doctorow seems to be Wolfe's favourite author. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes recommended the novel highly as a family saga around a legally downloadable Singularity, though he admitted he had read it without enthusiasm and forgotten it. Wynn then asked whether he recommended Stross's Accelerando and compared Wolfe's downloaded-backup material to Doctorow's Down and Out in the Magic Kingdom and to other SF about uploaded minds. The thread stayed brief, with no real disagreement and no settled reading.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: recommends Home Fires highly as a family saga around a Singularity, despite a lukewarm first reading
  • James Wynn: relays the PW review and its plot summary, and compares Home Fires' uploads to other SF works

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Fathers, mothers and children, Time loops and causality

/*Home Fires by Gene Wolfe */

/From Publishers Weekly: "Against a backdrop of science fictional elements such as time dilation through space travel, cyborgs, and brain downloads, Science Fiction Hall of Fame inductee Wolfe (The Sorcerer's House) builds a romance and a mystery. Successful lawyer Skip Grison wants to find a wonderful gift for his contracted paramour, Chelle Blue, now 20 years his junior after an interstellar military tour. *After reuniting Chelle with her estranged mother, Vanessa, whose consciousness has been downloaded into a new body*, Skip takes Chelle on a round-the-world cruise that turns into an almost screwball comedy of hijackings and chaos as shadowy people may be trying to get to Skip, Chelle, Vanessa, or someone else entirely. Red herrings and unknown, mistaken, or confused identities saturate the novel, making this well suited for readers, especially mystery readers, who don't often read science fiction."
