Today, a Sea Slug. Tomorrow, ... ?

Urth list · 6 messages · 2010-01-17 to 2010-01-29

2010urthThe Book of the New SunJerry FriedmanDavid StockhoffJeff Wilson
The thread: Readers debated whether the Green Man in The Book of the New Sun could be photosynthetic, prompted by a news report of a sea slug that acquired algae genes and makes chlorophyll. Jerry Friedman argued it was plausible and ran energy arithmetic for a sunbathing man, while Jeff Wilson countered that a man-sized body intercepts too little sunlight to replace much diet, so chlorophyll might serve sensory purposes. The thread drifted into calorie and energy calculations, with Friedman estimating that tenfold photosynthetic gain would cut food needs by about a third and David Stockhoff arguing the figures depend on assumed American intake. It ended without agreement, the discussion mostly about numbers rather than the text.
  • Jerry Friedman: the Green Man could plausibly be photosynthetic, with energy arithmetic supporting it
  • Jeff Wilson: chlorophyll body is implausible as food and may serve sensing instead
  • David Stockhoff: the sea slug report makes the idea more plausible, and food needs depend on the calorie assumptions
  • Jeff Wilson (later): a predator microbe that turns photosynthetic after eating doubtfully efficient

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Beasts and the animal in us, Evolution and the future of humanity

2010-01-17 Jeff Wilson replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that the Green Man's chlorophyll-based body is implausible as a food source, since a man-sized body intercepts too little sunlight to replace much diet, and proposes that chlorophyll may serve sensory purposes instead. Grounds this in a calorie estimate and the Green Man's humanlike teeth, and asks which scene would need light-sensing skin.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Green Man's chlorophyll serves sensory rather than nutritional purposes

There's still a question of him being able to intercept enough sunlight to be more active than a slug. A man-sized silhouette in Earthly conditions intercepts less than 100 calories worth of sunlight a day. From the Green Man's sunny smile, his people apparently still have humanlike teeth, so presumably they can eat a variety of food to make up the deficit, but I'm not sure if reducing your food needs by 1/30 is worth the hassle.

It might be that the chlorophyll isn't strictly for food, but for sensory purposes; can anyone think of an occasion when the Green Man would need to be able to feel a shadow falling on him, or would have to distinguish between bright lights of different character?

Argues that the Green Man's food-energy needs in Wolfe's Long Sun books could be cut by up to half if the calorie calculations assume an American intake rather than a more typical human level. Responds to Jerry Friedman's remark that a tenfold gain in sunlight and photosynthetic efficiency would cut the Green Man's food needs by about a third, a point of calculation rather than textual reading.

Furthermore, if those calculations are based on American calorie intake, you could cut the Green Man's food energy needs by up to 50% just by changing that assumption to a level more typical of the rest of humanity.
