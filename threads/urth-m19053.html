The thread: The thread began with Nicholas Gevers proposing that the title Seventeen alludes to the Dutch East India Company's Heere Seventien, a coded sea reference, and Lee Berman extended the number-17 idea to communist states and mutated humans. Most of the discussion then turned to whether Wolfe's Ascians and the Group of Seventeen model communism. Nathan Tresch disputed that using Wolfe's interview linking Ascians to Byzantine Turks and North American origins, while David Stockhoff pointed to Korean War experience. Gwern Branwen argued for communist China with specific parallels, and Lee Berman and Son of Witz backed a communist or Cold War reading. The thread ended with the communist reading gaining support but without a clear resolution against Tresch's objections.
  • Nicholas Gevers: title alludes to Dutch East India Company's Heere Seventien with a coded sea reference
  • Lee Berman: Group of 17 is communist states; Urth aliens are mutated humans seeking immortality
  • Nathan C. Tresch: Ascians are not simply communism; Wolfe tied them to Byzantine Turks and North America
  • David Stockhoff: Ascians owe more to Korean War experience, especially Chinese troops crossing the Yalu
  • Gwern Branwen: Group of Seventeen and Ascians model communist China, citing parallels like the Little Red Book
  • Lee Berman: Ascia reflects Cold War thinking, since Wolfe said it 'could happen here'

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, War and soldiers, Names and their meanings

2011-05-23 Nicholas Gevers #

Proposes that the title Seventeen alludes to the Dutch East India Company's governing board, the Heere Seventien, and that Wolfe may have intended a coded sea reference. Grounds this in the Company's maritime history and its reclaimed-land origins, and notes the Dutch appear overtly only in Dorp in On Blue's Waters and Return to the Whorl.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Seventeen alludes to the Heere Seventien

A possible reference by Wolfe is to the Dutch East India Company of the 17th and 18th centuries, which was governed by a board of managers or factors called the "Heere Seventien" or Group of Seventeen. Given the Company's association with the sea, and origin in a country partly reclaimed from the sea, Wolfe might have intended a coded pelagic reference, thus to Abaia, Scylla, and the rest. The DEIC didn't have a very savoury reputation. Of course, the only overt reference to the Dutch in the entire Briah Cycle is in *On Blue's Waters* and *Return to the Whorl*, with Dorp and its doughty sailors...

--Nick Gevers.

2011-05-23 Lee Berman replying to Nicholas Gevers #

Proposes that the Group of 17 refers to the communist states of Wolfe's writing period, and that the Urth-cast aliens and megatherians are all mutated humans who grow large in pursuit of immortality. Builds on Nicholas Gevers's and David's remarks about the number 17, and cites Typhon, Baldanders, Tzadkiel, Pig, the Godling, and Great Scylla as examples of the progression.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · source-and-allusion · theories: Group of 17 refers to communist states; Megatherians and aliens are all human in origin; Erebus produced the pale pandours

I like David's theory about 17 being a nice counterpart to 12 in a gnostic parody of Earth (which I roundly think Urth is meant to be).

Still, I don't feel the need to leave it at that. For me the the Group of 17 might also refer to the communist nations at the time of BotNS writing: USSR, China, Cuba, N. Korea, N. Vietnam, Mongolia, Cambodia, Laos, Bulgaria, Romania, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Poland, Hungary, E. Germany,Albania and Yemen. (the # gets bigger if you want to add some short-lived African communist states)

With regard to the origin of megatherians, I'm coming around to the notion that they are all human in origin. Jibes with the "Man is created in God's image" religious idea. Aliens are always some form of mutated human, be they Abaia or Famulimus.

I think Wolfe tries to show us the progression. It always starts with a bid for immortality. Typhon with his body grafts. Baldanders with his eternal growth. Even Tzadkiel with his escape to the next universe.

But by definition, becoming immortal means losing one's humanity. Thus Baldanders' and Typhon's cruelty. Even Tzadkiel must resort to genocide. Baldanders is losing his hearing. Typhon loses his whole body. Pig is blind and the Godling has eyes so small we must guess he will lose his sight with further growth.

We aren't given a lot of face time with the final products, but I think we get enough with Tzadkiel, The Mother and Great Scylla to conclude that when these former human-types get big enough they can pinch off human-sized agents to accomplish what they, in their large bulk, cannot. We never see Abaia but it is implied he can do the same thing. The cold pale warriors, the pandours, are a similar product of Erebus I think.

(James, you implied that there is evidence in BotNS for Erebus being long dead. Could you outline this?)

2011-05-26 Nathan C. Tresch replying to Lee Berman #

Challenges the popular reading that Wolfe's Ascians are a stand-in for communism, citing Wolfe's own interview where he ties them to Byzantine Turks and North American origins. Argues that a slogan-based culture could equally arise from extreme formalism, political correctness, or mass media such as the internet.
disputes · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion · theories: Ascians represent communist totalitarianism

Can someone explain to me why the idea that the Ascians have anything to do with communism is so popular? He attributed his inspiration for the Ascians to the Turks of the Byzantine period of history, and points out that they are north american in origin in this interview:

http://www.depauw.edu/sfs/interviews/wolfe46interview.htm

<http://www.depauw.edu/sfs/interviews/wolfe46interview.htm>Further, the idea that it takes a totalitarian society to produce a culture where only certain slogans can be used to communicate seems specious. I think it's just as likely that formalism and political correctness taken to an extreme could produce such a culture, or that some kind of mass media which spread slogans, (like the internet spreading memes), could also be the impetus.

2011-05-26 David Stockhoff replying to Nathan C. Tresch #

Argues that the Ascians in The Book of the New Sun owe more to Wolfe's Korean War experience, especially Chinese troops crossing the Yalu, than to communism as such. Responds to Nathan C. Tresch's objection by contrasting Communist slogans with advertising slogans as sources of social conformity, and by noting the soldier's tale.
disputes · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion · theories: Ascians modelled on Chinese army in Korea

Well, Wolfe was in Korea, so regardless of the actual political system that created them (and their battle formations don't seem very Communist), one may imagine being impressed for a lifetime by the implacableness of the Chinese army when they crossed the Yalu in winter, and that this might influence anyone greatly.

But since the Internet was not around in the 1970s, we are left with (a) Communist slogans (b) ad slogans as a source of total social brainwashing. From the soldier's tale, Ascia certainly sounds more like China than Madison Avenue, to me.

2011-05-26 Gwern Branwen replying to Nathan C. Tresch #

Argues that the Group of Seventeen and the Ascian society in The Book of the New Sun are modeled on communist China rather than the Turks or Ottoman caliphate, citing Wolfe's comment that Turks were his equivalent for the Commonwealth. Supports the case with parallels: peasant appeals to distant officials, numbered policies, the Gang of Four name, quoting from the Little Red Book, and the phrase 'correct thought'.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Group of Seventeen modeled on communist China

On Thu, May 26, 2011 at 7:51 PM, Nathan C. Tresch

Not quite. He said they were 'my equivalents of the Turk' to the 'stagnant political entity' of the Commonwealth. That is, Byzantium:Turks :: Commonwealth:Ascians. This is *not* the same thing as 'the society of the the Ascians is a version of the Ottoman caliphate'.

Personally, the very first time I read TBotNS, it was obvious to me that the Group of Seventeen's society was heavily modeled on communist China. The parallels are just too many.

The style of the story is focused on an agriculture society on what may be a collectivized farm (China's usual mindset, and especially that of Mao who swam in the sea of peasants), the Group of Seventeen is appealed to like the peasants appealed to distant mandarins and Party officials for justice*, the Group of Seventeen is even named like various Chinese entities - Gang of Four, anyone? -The use of numbers is again characteristically Chinese, if you don't believe me, look at http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Chinese_numbered_policies or look at how many Chinese entries there are in
http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category:Numeric_epithets (This impression is particularly reinforced if you read any substantial amount of ancient Chinese literature/philosophy/history.) The quoting is a clever mockery of communist quoting and especially quoting from the Little Red Book (an approved text if ever there was one). The sentiments are in line with communist theory.

Hell, "correct thought" is literally a China term! Look through Google Books if you don't believe me, eg.
http://books.google.com/books?id=ku5fABfvOGgC&pg=PA45&dq=%22correct+thought%22#v=onepage&q=%22correct%20thought%22&f=false

So there are many many points in the story that reek of Chinese communism. And *not* of Islam or the Turks.

* Not just my observation; see
http://crookedtimber.org/2009/03/07/loyal-to-the-group-of-seventeen/

2011-05-27 Lee Berman replying to Nicholas Gevers #

Argues that Ascia in The Book of the New Sun reflects Cold War thinking, since Wolfe was writing in the 1970s when communism was seen as America's main enemy. Supports this with a recalled Wolfe interview in which he said he set Ascia in North America because "it could happen here," and answers the earlier suggestions of Communist slogans and internet-era mass media.
supports · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion · theories: Ascia reflects Cold War anxieties about communism

Yes, I would agree. Not only was the Cold War on in full force in the 70's, Gene Wolfe can be fairly labelled as having a history of a Cold Warrior. Not to suggest he was a blinder-on American patriot but at the time, communism was considered both a real threat and America's main enemy. In a Wolfe interview, I think he said that he set Ascia in N. America in part because he wanted to show, "it could happen here".

Not disagreeing, necessarily, but that does sound like a very 21st century sentiment. The masses were not so considered as "sheeple" in the 70's. If they chose to adopt a popular slogan, that was free will, the opposite of communism.
