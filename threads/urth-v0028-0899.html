Themes: Books, libraries and writing

2000-04-14 Craig Christensen #

Proposes that a passage in Pratchett's Carpe Jugulum, about a Bishop Horn building a magical boat in forty-three flood accounts, nods to Wolfe's Long and New Sun books. Grounds this on Pratchett's reported habit of hiding book and author references, and on the name Horn, without citing any Wolfe text.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Pratchett's Carpe Jugulum alludes to Wolfe's Long Sun

I have heard that Terry Pratchett has a habit of cramming his books with clever references to other books and authors. Most are too clever for me to catch, but this one in Carpe Jugulum made me think it might be a nod toward Wolfe and the Long/New Sun books.

"On one shelf alone he found fourty-three remarkably similar accounts of a great flood, and in every single one of them a man very much like Bishop Horn had saved the elect by building a magical boat."

2000-04-14 Jack and Melissa Holcomb #

Argues that the Pratchett parody in Carpe Jugulum is less likely a nod to Wolfe than a shared borrowing from flood myth, citing the many Near Eastern Noah-like flood versions as the likelier source. Responds to an earlier poster who saw a Wolfe reference in a library scene and a flood account; the post also praises Pratchett's characters and criticizes Piers Anthony and Robert Asprin.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Pratchett's flood library scene draws on common flood myths, not Wolfe

In my experience, Pratchett's parodies of anything and anybody are anything but subtle -- although as with any parody, you need to have at least casual familiarity with the original in order to "get it." Here, though, I think you may be working too hard to see something that isn't necessarily there. It seems far more likely that Pratchett and Wolfe were working from the same source material -- which is, after all, anything but esoteric. (My kid has one version of it memorized, after all.)

Maybe. The library thing, more than the flood, seems suggestive -- but libraries aren't exactly rare in fantasy lit, either. I'd say the dozens of versions of the flood myth (many remarkably like the story of Noah in the Hebrew Scriptures, especially those from the Near East) seem like a more obvious source of this jab.

Haven't read Carpe Jugulum yet -- but Terry Pratchett is one of my secret vices. He's the one writer I've read who actually makes humorous fantasy work, probably because he doesn't rely on puns to carry the story. He writes genuine and interesting characters. He's head and shoulders above the competition -- Piers Anthony and Robert Asprin leap to mind -- who seem to be writing mostly for thirteen-year-olds with fair reading skills and underdeveloped taste.

Jack
