The thread: Readers asked why An Evil Guest includes a cop cursing a shop girl ('Shut the fuck up') and whether the profanity serves the story. Don Doggett found it jarring and unlike Wolfe, and he and Chris P. argued the cop is a rule-bending figure rather than a Wolfean hero, with Chris P. attributing his politer later manner to greater control. Brunians and Henry Eissler read the novel's inconsistencies and the ice cream shop girl as deliberate, while Mo Holkar pushed back that such defenses are increasingly strained. Later, Stephen Hoy and Matthew Keeley hunted for extra-textual allusions and a hidden pattern among the friends Rita, Amber and Christabelle, and Hoy ended by doubting that the brother clue ties into the book, with no clear resolution.
  • don doggett: the cop's profanity is jarring and unlike Wolfe, and the cop is not a Wolfean hero
  • Chris P.: the cop is a rule-bending figure who grows more polite because he is in control
  • brunians: the novel's inconsistencies are deliberate, and a casual reader would mistake them for carelessness
  • Henry Eissler: the ice cream shop girl is part of the hidden charade around Cassie
  • Mo Holkar / UKG: defenses of the inconsistencies are increasingly strained
  • Stephen Hoy: loose ends are allusions to Coleridge, Zelazny, Lynch and film, and the brother name may be unconnected

Themes: Unreliable narration, Doubles, twins and clones, Books, libraries and writing

2009-03-27 Josh Geller replying to Chris P #

Argues that An Evil Guest is cleverer than it looks: on its surface a badly edited pulp novel, with the heroine's address shifting and characters killed or forgotten. Responds to Chris P.'s remarks on the cop and the murdered doorman by suggesting that a reader unfamiliar with Wolfe would dismiss these inconsistencies as carelessness.
proposes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Apparent editing errors in An Evil Guest are deliberate

It has been growing on me, how clever this novel is. On the surface, it is not only a pulp novel, it is a *bad* pulp novel, with a poor editing job: why the author and editor can't even keep the address of the heroine straight. People are mentioned, killed, forgotten. It is easy to see someone who doesn't know Wolfe saying 'Eh! The author can't even be bothered to keep a bit of consistency? Onto the trash heap with it!'.

.

2009-03-27 Don Doggett replying to don doggett #

Argues that the coarse profanity given to a detective in a Wolfe novel is jarring and unlike anything else in Wolfe's work, and doubts it serves only to show him as tough. Responds to Don Doggett's earlier remark that the cop is not a Wolfean hero by agreeing with the observation and adding that the vulgarity recurs in his later appearances.
supports · close-textual-reading

Doggerel
www.don-doggett.blogspot.com

Yeah, but he appears again several times, but this sort of raw vulgarity is nowhere to be seen, nowhere else in the novel, and nowhere in any of Wolfe's other works that I've read. I'm a big fan of tactical vulgarity in fiction but it was really jarring here, and I can't imagine it was done just to show this guy is a "tough as nails" detective.

Don

2009-03-29 Stephen Hoy replying to Henry Eissler #

Proposes that the loose ends in An Evil Guest, starting with the Baskin-Robbins scene and the three friends named there, are extra-textual allusions to other works. Links Christabelle to Coleridge's Christabel, Amber to Zelazny's Amber series, Rita to Lynch's Mulholland Drive, and the detective's line to John Goodman in The Big Lebowski and One Night at McCool's.
proposes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Loose ends in An Evil Guest are extra-textual allusions; Christabelle alludes to Coleridge's Christabel; Amber alludes to Zelazny's Amber series; Rita alludes to Mulholland Drive

Cassie: "I wish we could be friends."
B-R girl: "I've got three friends...Rita, Amber, and Christabelle." - p28

There's been a bit of discussion about the "loose ends" in AEG, but I think it's possible to read these loose ends as extra-textual allusions that align with the story.

First things first, why does Wolfe set a scene at a Baskin-Robbins? Does it have something to do with the company's 31 flavors? Maybe it's an allusion to Messier 31, the Andromeda galaxy.

Second, why mention these three friends? Maybe they have something in common that pertains to Wolfe's plan for AEG.

Working backwards from least to most obscure -- 'Christabelle' suggests Coleridge's long poem with a similar name, "Christabel." The key aspect of this poem is the way Coleridge explores the character's perception of reality between waking and dreaming.

Similar theme with the second name, 'Amber'. The name suggests Roger Zelazny's Amber series of books and stories. This may be a subtle hint at Wolfe's method, the idea of one True World and an infinite number of shadow worlds, alterable "at will" by an adept.

If we continue along the same theme of altered perceptions, the name 'Rita' suggests the character of Rita/Camilla in David Lynch's "Mulholland Drive." Cassie shares some interesting aspects with one of the actresses in this film, including responsibility for the death of at least one of the other characters.

[I haven't figured out a tie-in for B-R girl's reference to Rita's little brother.]

Finally, at the end of this page we get the detective--"The man who entered was both tall and wide, nearing middle age." And departs B-R with a jarring finger-point: "You stfu."

I wonder whether this is an allusion to John Goodman. Size and shape and age match, and Goodman comically utters "STFU, Donnie" in The Big Lebowski, but he plays a detective in a couple of movies. Specifically, One Night at McCool's has a few parallels with AEG--especially the animal magnetism of the actress Jewel--and this may explain why Wolfe puts the detective's words on the page.

2009-03-29 Stephen Hoy replying to Matthew Keeley #

Argues that the name Rita's little brother, dropped by a character in An Evil Guest, may have no tie to the rest of the story, questioning a prior puzzle idea. Grounds this in the lack of any matching Rita with a brother linked to the book's other names, and compares the shifting reality of the novel to Mulholland Drive.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Names in B-R Girl's list tie into the AEG story

Matt Keeley asks:

B-R Girl: I like everyone of them fifty times more than you. Even Christabelle.
Cassie : Puts me in my place. Got a spoon?
B-R Girl: I even like Rita's little brother better than you. - AEG p28

If those names were rattled off by B-R Girl because they have some connection to the rest of the AEG story, we'd expect to be able to identify a Rita with a little brother who has a similar tie-in to AEG as the other names. None comes to mind, which may mean I'm off-target with one or all of the suggestions I've made. Still, there's a Mulholland Drive quality to AEG. It seems like every time Cassie awakens, 'reality' has mutated.
