banshee and the green dress

He mostly argued by proposal, and several posts drew on Dan French's line about Ireland's first inhabitants and on claims about the Cinderella parallel, timing, and a Clue-style carnival mystery. The posts offered speculation with little textual proof, and the thread shows no reply or agreement from other posters. 2014-01-01 Marc Aramini #

Links the green dress Margeret Lorn wears at Olivia's wedding to the Irish banshee's traditional green dress and to Olivia's own pale green. Asks why the dog boy appears during Den's dreamlike thoughts of Smart's story and why a hunt follows, and whether this echoes the stag-at-bay wild hunt scene.
questions · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Margeret Lorn's green dress recalls the Irish banshee

In addition, the banshees of Ireland normally wear a green dress, and Margeret Lorn wears a green dress at Olivia's wedding when den is reminded of a princess in a tower "like my aunt Olivia's other attendants, Margeret wore a costume of pale green, embellished with daffodils ... It may have been her smile, and something in the way she held her head and looked at me sidelong from those eyes, as though her soul were staring at me out of narrow windows in a tower"

Of course, Olivia's color is the pale green of olive's anyway, and she wears a green dress earlier in the book. More interesting is a few pages later when den imagines retelling smart's story and probably using it to win Margaret just as Smart used it to get Olivia, and then dreamlike thinking of the dog boy piddling on the rug and mounting his leg and "Margaret with his head in her lap while I explained that it did not matter, that my father would take him hunting the next day, that that would make him happy. He rises and begins to clean a gun".

Why the dog boy here, and why a hunt? At all related to the stag at bay wild hunt scene? And why John weer take the dog boy Charles Turner hunting? Sent from my iPhone

2014-01-02 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that the green dress Margeret Lorn wears at Olivia's wedding links her to the Irish banshee, which is traditionally green. Adds that green also marks the fairy book and necronomicon, Ireland's Emerald Isle nickname, and Irish or Scottish origins of names like Lorn, Weer and Boyne as support.
supports · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Margeret Lorn's green dress echoes the Irish banshee

Other reports list different colors besides green for banshees, and the banshee is merely wrapped in grave wrappings in the text, but nevertheless green is also the color of the fairy book and the necronomicon in the text itself. Ireland is also known as The Emerald Isle poetically. The majority of prominent non-color names in the text like Lorn, Weer, Boyne, Doherty etc are Irish or Scottish in origin.

2014-01-02 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Proposes that the Scottish and Irish names in the book may map onto distinct kinds of Celtic fairy folk, such as the firbolg, sidhe and Tuatha De Danann and how they displaced one another. Raises this as a speculation after noting minor characters like Arbuthnot and Tilly, offering no textual proof beyond the names themselves.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Names map onto distinct Celtic fairy peoples

Now that I mentioned it, the names are overwhelmingly Scottish or Irish, even minor characters like Arbuthnot (Scottish) or Tilly ... Wonder if there is a distinction between those families of Scottish persuasion and those of the Irish, like the different types of fairy folk, the firbolg, the sidhe, the tuatha de danann and the manner in which they supplanted each other ...

2014-01-02 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that recurring motifs in Peace, including birds, red deer and the sidhe, come together in the final story and link women described as birds, the banshee turning into a red deer, and deer hunting in the opening and closing sections. Grounds this in Dan French's line naming the wolf, red deer, birds and sidhe as Ireland's earliest inhabitants, and in the Irish origins of names such as Weer and Doherty.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Banshee transformation into red deer links the deer hunting

I was noticing a whole lot of repeating motifs and felt better about them in the final story of Peace, when they are all mentioned together. Dan French, a Doherty descedent, says "before (the Firbolg) came, there was no one (in Ireland) but the wolf, the red deer, the birds, and the sidhe"

Many of the patterns repeated throughout involve women described as birds, and the myth of the baen sidhe (banshee) who transforms into a red deer resonates with the deer hunting mentioned in the first and last sections of the book(even by the female employee who thinks cassonsville is not a great place to raise young but still pipe dreams about having a daughter for a second)

In this section weer says that his name is Dutch, however other research indicates the Weer family migrated from Normandy to Ireland, kind of becoming a transplanted name.

2014-01-03 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that Mrs. Mason's cruelty to Doris in the Peace carnival replays the Cinderella story, so the surname Mason is a transposition of Lady Tremain's mother figure. Argues that timing problems make Doris a plausible mother of Weer's daughter with Sherry Gold only if the child is very young, and notes a further chronology slip about Weer's farm-building memory and the ages in Blaine's account.
proposes · source-and-allusion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Mrs. Mason is a Cinderella stepmother figure; Mason name derives from Tremain stone house

The cinderella story is replayed a bit with Mrs. Mason and her daughters treating Doris so terribly that she ultimately electrocutes herself in the carnival. Some effort has been expended on who Mr. Mason might be, but I feel like in this case the name is a direct transposition from the fairy tale story, in which Lady Tremain is the mother, Tremain meaning "from the stone house" or from the big city, certainly related to a mason. The timing of that event is difficult to make Doris Weer's daughter with Sherry Gold- if they had a child it should only be five to at most ten then. One other time slip involves Weer telling the librarian that the farm she is seeking was built by the Porters thirty years ago as he saw when Peacock took his aunt on expeditions - it seems those expeditions should have stopped by the time he was fifteen years old, but that matches the age that Blaine remembers for "jimmy" weer when "roscoe" mcafee got the egg from Olivia, when in Weer's memory he was nine at that event.
