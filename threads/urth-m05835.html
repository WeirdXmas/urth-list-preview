What's So Green About Ushas?

Urth list · 9 messages · 2008-07-15 to 2008-07-18

2008urthThe Book of the New SunTony EllisAdam ThorntonRoy C. Lackeyb sharp
The thread: The thread asked whether the Green Man whom Severian follows to Ushas is the future god or a descendant of Ushas's new peoples, and whether the Corridors of Time lead to one time-stream or to parallel realities. Roy C. Lackey argued the Green Man is a descendant of Ushas's peoples and that corridors lead to different times, while Tony Ellis argued the Green Man future is not the one producing the Hierogrammates and that corridors open many parallel realities. Adam Thornton supported the parallel-reality reading by tracing the phrase 'a line that seldom exists' to Borges. The debate then turned to whether the brooks between tiered universes are tied to the corridors, with Lackey and Ellis repeatedly disputing the point using the text. It ended without agreement, each side citing passages.
  • Roy C. Lackey: Green Man is a descendant of Ushas's peoples; corridors lead to different times, and brooks are between tiers, not parallel futures
  • Tony Ellis: Green Man future is not the Hierogrammate future; corridors lead to many parallel realities and are tied to the brooks
  • Adam Thornton: 'a line that seldom exists' is a deliberate Borges allusion, so corridors are a Borgesian conceit
  • b sharp: Green Man is an alternate future counterpart to Ushas, reflecting Wolfe's possible faith crisis, with Hierogrammates evolving toward the Increate

Themes: Time loops and causality, The dying sun and the cosmos, Gods, angels and the Increate

Argues that the Green Man future is not the one that produces the Hierogrammates, since Severian returns as a god in a different future. Grounds this in the final chapter of The Urth of the New Sun, where Severian escapes Apu-Punchau's tomb and chooses a corridor of time, and rejects the assumption that the Green Man path leads to him becoming a demi-god.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Green Man future does not produce Hierogrammates; Severian returns as god in a different future

I don't think the Green Man future is meant to be the one that produces the Hierogrammates, because I don't think it's the one to which Severian returns as a god at the end of UotNS, although that sometimes seems to be the assumption.

When Severian escapes from Apu-Punchau's tomb in the final chapter of UotNS and the corridors of time stretch around him, he says: "Bright-winged, the small Tzadkiel fluttered beside one. The green man raced beside another. I chose one that ran as lonely as I..." That's the one he follows back to Ushas.

That seems to suggest that the Green Man future is one that gets the New Sun but doesn't get Severian the demi-god, and humanity works out its own arrangement with the universe.

2008-07-16 b sharp replying to Tony Ellis #

Endorses Tony Ellis's view that the Green Man is an alternate future counterpart to Ushas, and adds that Severian and the Green Man's debate over which is really human reflects Wolfe's possible faith crisis. Proposes that the Hierogrammates evolved into a superior race whose process culminates in the Increate, grounded in the Green Man's disgust scene and a Revelation quotation.
proposes · theology-and-religion · myth-and-structure · theories: Green Man is Master Ash's alternate future; Hierogrammates evolve into the Increate; Wolfe's faith crisis shapes the Green Man

I like Tony Ellis' explanation of the Green Man, being the equivalent of Master Ash- an alternate future to Ushas. It explains why he doesn't seem to fit with the UotNS Ushas and Hiero-dudes. Tony supports the position well with the text. I'll add that in their initial meeting, Severian and the Green Man have a bantering discussion but at one point they are hit by a sudden disgust for each other and debate over which one of them is really human.

I can imagine a Gene Wolfe in his 40's writing this book, having a crisis of conscience. He sees the passive, do-nothing, wait-for-a-savior nature of much of humanity and recognizes there is no real future in that. Having passed through the hippie 60's and granola 70's I can imagine him questioning his own faith and spirituality. I can stereotype vegetarian, secular humanists as, like the Green Man, very nice, honorable, caring people, and I can imagine Wolfe wondering if that was the way to go.

Religion seems to have won the battle though. Wolfe could see an even more noble future for humanity than becoming a giant hippie commune. I've always imagined that the Hierogrammates evolved into or created a superior cognate race that passed on to that universe which is higher than Yesod.

The process would continue until The Increate is formed at the end, which is also the beginning (says God: "I am Alpha and Omega, the first and the last"- Revelation 1: 8). Leads to the interesting idea that while Man was made in God's image, God was made in Man's image also. (is THAT what the Sistine Chapel ceiling is all about? ;-))

-bsharp

2008-07-16 Tony Ellis replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the Corridors of Time in The Urth of the New Sun lead to many parallel realities rather than only different points in one time-stream, citing Severian's plural 'brooks' and the line about a reality that 'seldom exists'. Responds to Tony Ellis's claim by noting that Wolfe's earlier intent may have differed from his later revisions to the idea.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Corridors of Time lead to multiple realities

I think if that was true, there would only have been one corridor, as there was when Tzadkiel first showed Severian the Brook Madregot. Severian is now talking about a multitude:

"All about me stretched the Corridors of Time, waving meadows roofed with the lowering sky of Time and whisperous with the brooks that ripple from the most supernal universe of all to the least." UotNS, LI

Brooks, not brook, you notice. Severian goes on to say "Behind me along a line that seldom exists, Apu-Punchau, the Head of Day, stepped from his house and squatted to eat the boiled maize and roast meat his people had left for him." If the corridors only lead to different points in the same time-stream, I don't see how Severian can look back and see a reality that 'seldom exists'.

The quotes you give where the Green Man talks about his future still make sense if he comes from one of a multitude of futures. For what it's worth, I think that at the time those lines were written, long before UotNS, Wolfe may well have intended them to be read in the stricter sense you suggest. But I also think that by the time UotNS came along Wolfe had changed his mind about a few things.

2008-07-16 Adam Thornton replying to Tony Ellis #

Proposes that the phrase "a line that seldom exists" in Severian's account of Apu-Punchau is a deliberate allusion to Borges' "A New Refutation of Time," which suggests the corridors of time are a Borgesian conceit. Responds to Tony Ellis's objection that the corridors only lead to points in one time-stream, so Severian could not see a reality that seldom exists, by pointing to the literary source of the wording.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Seldom-exists phrase alludes to Borges' refutation of time

Note that something that "seldom exists" within the "Corridors of Time" has just about *got* to be a loving nod to Borges' "A New Refutation of Time."

Adam

2008-07-16 Roy C. Lackey replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the multiple brooks in Severian's vision are the brooks running between the tiered universes, not parallel futures, so the Corridors of Time need not be congruent with them. Grounds this in Tzadkiel's explanation of the Brook Madregot, the statement that Urth and Ushas lie in Briah, and Severian's unexplained return to Urth.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Brooks run between universes, not alternate futures; Green Man did not travel from outside Briah

The multiple brooks refers to the brooks that run between the tiered universes. Small Tzadkiel could show him only the one brook, Madregot, because that was where she had been exiled by her larger self.

"This is the Brook Madregot, and it runs from Yesod to Briah." "Between the universes?" I asked. "How can that be?" "How could it not be? Energy gropes for some lower state, always; which is merely to say that the Increate tosses all the universes between his hands."
"But it's a stream," I protested. "Like the streams of Urth."
Tzadkiel nodded. "Those too are of energy seeking a lower state, and what is perceived is dictated by the
instrument. If you had other eyes, or another mind, you
would see all things otherwise." (URTH, XL, 285)

Since the brooks run between universes, it would not be very meaningful for the Corridors of Time to be merely congruent with them. Urth and Ushas were in Briah. I see no evidence that the Green Man traveled to Urth's past from outside Briah. True, Sev moving along the Madregot (or at least at an angle to it on the bank beside it), somehow ended up on Urth some forty years after he left for Yesod, but I find that inexplicable.

I don't know what Severian meant by that, but if there are a multitude of futures, then trying to understand the Urth Cycle becomes a decent into chaos. If a man from a future that was not Sev's can interact with Sev in a practical way that Ash could not, then that opens up a can of worms better left alone. Any of the story's many mysteries can be explained away by appeals to influences from undemonstrated alternate futures.

-Roy

2008-07-17 Tony Ellis replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the brooks of the Urth of the New Sun are more closely tied to the Corridors of Time than the earlier poster allowed, since several brooks lead to Briah and the Green Man's brook is contrasted with Severian's choice. Grounds this in Severian's remark that entering the corridors leaves the universe and in Small Tzadkiel's words that the water flows through time toward Yesod and Briah.
disputes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Brooks run through time as well as between universes

Yet at least three of these brooks lead to Briah. The Green Man races beside one, and you say he travels only within Briah. Small Tzadkiel flutters beside a second, which is Madregot, and Severian chooses a third. The fact that Wolfe bothers to show us Severian choosing *not* to take the Green Man's brook seems significant to me.

I think they may be more synonymous than you realise. First of all, note that "to enter the corridors of time is to leave the universe." CotA, XXXIV.

And secondly, congruent or not, the brooks run between universes, but they also run through time:

"Madregot flows from the glory of Yesod" - she pointed upstream - "to the destruction of Briah, down that way." She pointed again. "Follow the water, and you'll be at a time nearer the coming of your star." UotNS, XL

2008-07-17 Roy C. Lackey replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the Green Man came from the Ushas future and that the books show only two possible futures, Ragnarok and Ushas, so the Corridors and brooks need not be the same. Responds to Tony Ellis's claim that the Corridors of Time and the brooks are synonymous, citing his quotation from The Citadel of the Autarch, and says there is no evidence of multiple futures after the white hole arrives.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Green Man came from the Ushas future

We just aren't going to agree on this. The books show only two possible futures, Ragnarok and Ushas, with one excluding the other. Whether the Corridors and brooks are the same or not, the Green Man came from the Ushas future, which is necessarily in the universe of Briah. There is no evidence that there were multiple futures or realities after the arrival of the white hole, which was the original point of debate.

-Roy
