Horn as Silk

Whorl list · 3 messages · 1999-11-16 to 1999-11-24

1999whorlThe Book of the Long SunKevin J. MaroneyAlice TurnerJames Jordan
The thread: Readers debated the inhumi's secret in Wolfe's Horn as Silk trilogy. Alice Turner disputed John Roberson's claim that Pas's voice shows up in Horn's narration, arguing it is a blend with Silk's and that the secret is more complex and still withheld. Kevin J. Maroney supported a list idea that the inhumi gain sentience only by feeding on sentient prey, so humans could defeat them by protecting one another, though he thought human divisiveness blocks that and Wolfe likely intends something subtler. Jim Jordan proposed the inhumi absorb human moral qualities, so their fate depends on human salvation, citing a Horn quotation and Quetzal's change near Silk. The thread ended without consensus, with the secret left open.
  • Alice Turner: Horn's narration blends Silk's voice, not Pas's; the inhumi secret is complex and still withheld
  • Kevin J. Maroney: inhumi gain sentience by feeding on sentient prey, so mutual protection could defeat them, but human divisiveness blocks it
  • Jim Jordan: inhumi take on human moral qualities, so their salvation depends on humans and Quetzal changed through Silk

Themes: Beasts and the animal in us, Free will and choice, Power, rulers and politics

1999-11-16 Alice Turner #

Disputes John Roberson's suggestion that Pas's voice appears in Horn's narration, arguing Horn's voice is a blend with Silk's rather than a Pas addition, and notes the third book's title points back to the Whorl. Reviews earlier guesses about the inhumi's secret, such as taking on prey's traits through blood, and argues the secret is more complex and still withheld.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Pas's voice appears in Horn's narration; Inhumi gain prey's traits by drinking blood; The inhumi's secret is fatal if known

Hi John Roberson, and welcome,

Thanks for pitching right in. My take on your two points:

I. I don't detect a hint of Pas (as I see Pas, or as we all saw him as Typhon) in the "voice" of Horn. Of course we first met Silk filtered through Horn, but this narration is recognizably similar to that amalgam. Horn tried hard to do Silk justice and now that they really *are* an amalgam it's shiftier but not nearly as strange as a Pas addition would make it. So I think not, though it's an interesting idea. Also, the very title of the third book indicates to me that we really will be going back to the Whorl (hooray, we'll see what happens with those super-tall asses!)

2. There have been a lot of guesses about "the secret of the inhumi," and the front-runner for a while was that it was simply that they took on (or could take on) aspects of the prey along with the blood; when they encountered a sentient race for the first time, it revolutionized them. Another conjecture has to do with their obviously being able to survive a long time without sustenance in a mummified state. But later thinking is that it's more complicated than that--it's a secret that if known could destroy them--and that Wolfe just hasn't given it to us even in clue form yet.

Arguments invited.

-alga

1999-11-16 Kevin J. Maroney #

Supports a later list observation that the inhumi gain sentience only by feeding on sentient prey, so humans could beat them by protecting one another during conjunction. Says human divisiveness prevents full mutual protection, letting some fall and the inhumi grow stronger, while agreeing Wolfe likely has something subtler in mind; responds to alga's remark about a secret that could destroy the inhumi.
supports · myth-and-structure · theories: Inhumi gain sentience by feeding on sentient prey

I like the later observation (I'm pretty sure it was made here) to the effect that: If the inhumi only gain sentience from feeding on sentient prey, the humans could defeat them by methodically protecting each other from the inhumi during conjunction. But human divisiveness keeps them from protecting each other completely, and thus some humans fall, making the inhumi stronger.

But I agree that Wolfe almost certainly has something more subtle and clever in mind.

1999-11-24 James Jordan #

Proposes that the inhumi take on the moral qualities of humans, so that if humans become good, the inhumi would become good too, and suggests Quetzal's change of heart comes from being near Silk. Grounds the idea in a Horn quotation about caring for others and the inhumi as underpeople whose salvation depends on human salvation.
proposes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: Inhumi take on the moral qualities of humans; Quetzal turns good because of Silk

It's Rat Time, Slimina!

To begin with, courtesy of Kieran:

Horn: "If only we cared about each other sufficiently. If only all of us loved all the others enough, they would go back to [mindless beasts]. We would still think them horrible creatures, and they would still be dangerous, as the crocodiles in this lower river water are. But they would be no worse."
Evensong: "That is the secret, what you said?"
Horn: "No, of course not." [p.375]

So, let's invert that. The inhumi take on our characteristics. We are not very nice people: enslaving others, raping wimmin, etc. What if we become good, "Green Man" types, like the forerunners of Blue? What would the inhumi become then? Good guys! Hey, is that why old Quetzal becomes a good guy, because of hanging around and being near Silk?
Inhumi are animals, "underpeople" of Cordo Smith, but of a different sort. Their salvation depends on and proceeds from human salvation. My guess. It think the clews are there, but I may be taking them wrongly.

Slavering Nutria of the Dripping Jowl
