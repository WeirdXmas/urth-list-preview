The thread: Andrew Mason opened by asking whether Kypris's advice to Orchid to find new love after Orpine's death pays off, and later asked which character is the second god in Kypris's reply to Silk. Lee Berman argued the advice is Kypris speaking to herself, prefiguring a Silk-Hyacinth romance, grounded in Kypris being a program whose lost beloved Pas was deleted. Andrew Mason agreed the advice may reflect Kypris's own loss and raised the Outsider as a possible presence in her mind. Gerry Quinn argued that Scylla treats people as things while wiser beings treat each other as persons, and later suggested the Outsider resonates with Kypris's position. António Marques questioned whether the false gods ever mention the Outsider and what either reading gains the narrative. The thread drifted from the textual question into side speculation on Mint and Joan of Arc, and ended without resolution.
  • Andrew Mason: asks whether Kypris's advice to Orchid pays off and suspects Kypris is speaking of herself
  • Lee Berman: Kypris's advice is self-directed, prefiguring a Silk-Hyacinth love story rooted in Pas's deletion
  • Gerry Quinn: Scylla treats people as things; wiser beings treat each other as persons; the Outsider resonates with Kypris
  • António Marques: doubts the Outsider is mentioned by the false gods and questions what either reading adds

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Love and loss, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2010-11-30 Andrew Mason #

Notes how Wolfe interweaves a band tune heard at Blood's house with Oosik, commander of the Third Brigade, and asks whether Kypris's advice to Orchid to find a new love after Orpine's death ever pays off. Cites the party scene with Silk and the later revelation that Oosik attended, and the Kypris-Orchid exchange, as the textual basis for the question.
questions · close-textual-reading

More from my reading of BOTLS.

First just a titbit, showing how nicely Wolfe interweaves things; while Silk is at Blood's house, he hears a band playing 'Brave Guards of the Third Brigade'. Much later, we discover that Oosik. commanding officer of the Third Brigade, was at that party; the piece was presumably being played in in his honour.

But my actual question is this. Kypris tells Orchid that now the person she loved (Orpine) is dead, she must find someone else to love. Does anything ever come of this? I can't recall it doing so, but it sounds as if it's meant to prefigure something.

2010-12-12 Lee Berman replying to Andrew Mason #

Argues that Kypris's advice to Orchid to find someone new to love is really advice to herself, prefiguring a Silk-Hyacinth love story since Kypris is now part of Hyacynth and Silk carries a part of Pas. Grounds this in Kypris being a computer program whose lost beloved Pas was deleted from Mainframe, and asks how such programs view flesh-and-blood people.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · close-textual-reading · theories: Kypris's advice to Orchid is self-directed; Silk-Hyacinth love story is prefigured; Silk carries part of Pas

I am not nearly as familiar with Long Sun as BotNS but I'll give it a shot. In my opinion, Kypris' advice to Orchid is actually advice to herself. Her beloved Pas has been deleted from Mainframe and now she must find another object to love.

As Kypris has become a part of Hyacynth, I believe she is refering to a character who has a part of Pas within him, namely, Silk. It is the Silk-Hyacinth love story she is prefiguring.

"Something" implying an object is a funny reference. Kypris, from our perspective is not a person, she is a thing, a computer program. How do computer programs view flesh and blood people?

2010-12-13 Gerry Quinn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Scylla regards people as things, while more mature gods and wise beings, human or chem, treat each other as persons, which Gerry Quinn sees as the general pattern in Wolfe's works. Responds to Lee Berman's question about how computer programs like Kypris view flesh-and-blood people, offering a brief view without citing specific passages.
disputes · theories: Scylla regards people as things while other gods do not

From: "Lee Berman" <[email]>

As things, not people. At least, Scylla does. But Scylla, despite being a goddess, is immature.

It seems clear however that other gods do not, and wise persons be they human or chem or something else view each other as persons. I think that is generally the case in Wolfe's works.

- Gerry Quinn

2010-12-21 Gerry Quinn replying to António Marques #

Suggests that the woman speaking in the Orchid passage may invoke the Outsider because his association with the broken and disparaged resonates with Kypris while Echidna and the gang hunt her. Responds to António Marques's question about whether the fake gods mention the Outsider, noting he appears in the Chrasmological Writings but is rarely raised elsewhere.
answers · close-textual-reading · theories: Orchid's remark alludes to the Outsider for Kypris

From: "Ant?nio Marques" <[email]>

He seems to be in the Chrasmological Writings, but I haven't heard of any incidences of them mentioning him apart from this. But we don't get all that much face time with them anyway, or reasons why the Outsider might be discussed.

As I noted in another post, Kypris is being hunted by Echidna and the gang. It may be that the Outsider's association with the broken and the disparaged resonates with her at this time.
