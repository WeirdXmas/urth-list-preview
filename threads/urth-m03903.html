The thread: Participants traced a recurring cave or underworld motif across Wolfe's fiction, starting from Transentient's observation about characters groping through darkness. Joshua Solomon and Matthew Knight cited caves in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, Sandwalker, Peace and The Wizard Knight, and Andy Robertson read them as underworld and realm-of-the-dead symbols tied to burial habits. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes broadened the discussion to repeated images and embedded non-Christian cosmologies, while Stuart Hamm asked a historical question about Korean War tunnel fighting. The thread ended in a loose drift toward Plato's cave, recalled from memory without citation, with no firm conclusion reached.
  • Transentient: caves and lightless wandering recur across Wolfe's work
  • Joshua Solomon: the cave in A Story is a doppelganger of Aunt Jeanine's basement room
  • Matthew Knight: caves recur in Sandwalker, Peace and The Wizard Knight, and shapeshifting women are unreliable allies
  • Andy Robertson: caves stand for the underworld and the realm of the dead, a near-universal pattern
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: recurring images and embedded non-Christian cosmologies within an ambiguous Christian frame
  • Stuart Hamm: asks whether Wolfe's Korean War experience shaped the battle scenes

Themes: Ghosts, dreams and visions, Suffering and sacrifice, Myth and folklore sources

2007-06-06 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Matthew Knight #

Argues that Wolfe's stories repeat recurring images and themes, such as a communion feast that is sacramental or blasphemous and paired blue-green worlds, and proposes that non-Christian cosmologies are embedded within an ambiguous Christian cosmology. Responds to Matthew Knight's note on cave scenes and recurring motifs, offering examples from the Book of the New Sun, including the Liege of Leaves feast, and listing other recurring images.
proposes · theology-and-religion · myth-and-structure · theories: Non-Christian cosmologies are embedded within a larger Christian cosmology; The Liege of Leaves feast is blasphemy that becomes sacrament with Severian present

Hello Matthew!

Yes, there are quite a few running images and themes in GW's work, aside from the obvious genes, wolves, roses, and rosemary, which are almost more signatures than thematics. (How cool to have a name you can do that with! I can't figure out how to work a danl or a danehyoakes into my attempts at fiction...)

Another that appears frequently is a communion feast, which can be either sacramental or blasphemous, or, sometimes, both. (I think, for example, that the feast of the Liege of Leaves is generally a blasphemy but becomes a sacrament on the occasion of Severian's presence.)

Another that gets some discussion at times >kaff, kaff< is the recurrent image of blue-green world pairs: Ste.Anne/St.Croix, Urth/Lune, Blue/Green. Probably others, but those are the most significant.

One I push at that nobody else seems to see or agree with: the embedding of non-Christian cosmologies into a larger Christian cosmology, which itself is frequently ambiguous.

Cheers,

--Dan'l

2007-06-06 Andy Robertson replying to Matthew Knight #

Argues that caves in Wolfe's work stand for the underworld and the realm of the dead, tracing this to ancient burial habits and a near-universal mythic pattern. Supports Matthew Knight's observation by citing the mummified priest Sandwalker meets and the human remains in Peace, while admitting uncertainty about the pattern's significance and welcoming Matt to the list.
supports · myth-and-structure · theories: Caves in Wolfe represent the underworld and house of the dead

Yes, caves = underworld = world/house of the dead, from the old habit of burying everyone in the tribe together in a mound or communal grave. This is an ancient and nearly universal mythical pattern. Most of these caves in Wolfe have the dead in them (the priest Sandwalker meets is a corpse-mummy, the remains in PEACE are human IIRC). Or if not they are underworlds of various sorts. This is a common pattern in Wolfe's writing but I am not sure of its significance - it's a common pattern in nearly all writing.
