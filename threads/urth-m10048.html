TSH: Doris and Lupine

Urth list · 22 messages · 2010-04-15 to 2010-04-17

2010urthThe Book of the New SunJosh GellerGwern BranwenCraig BrewerDavid StockhoffJames WynnDan'l Danehy-OakesDave TallmanRoy C. Lackey
The thread: The thread asked whether the letter-date problems and strange events in The Sorcerer's House are Wolfe errors, unreliable-narrator clues, or supernatural hints, and how Doris relates to Lupine. Dave Tallman argued Doris and Lupine are one person once called Mary King, a view James Wynn first backed and then reversed, while Gwern Branwen and James Wynn pushed back that Doris behaves as a real corporeal person. Roy C. Lackey and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes disputed whether reordering letters #21 and #25 resolves the timeline, and the discussion drifted into Delany's Dhalgren and whether the Kid is insane. Craig Brewer's late post proposed a con by Bax against George, and the thread ended without agreement.
  • Dave Tallman: Doris and Lupine are one person, formerly Mary King, who split off her soul to become a werewolf
  • James Wynn: Doris is a corporeal person distinct from Lupine, and he reversed his own soul-swap idea mid-thread
  • Roy C. Lackey: Lupine took Doris's appearance, and moving letters #21 and #25 does not fix the timeline
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: The letter-order note is a red herring from Wolfe's editorial persona and the letters work in functional order
  • Gwern Branwen: Doris is not Lupine, citing age, Lupine's youth, and the drive scene
  • Craig Brewer: Nothing fantastic happens; the events are a con by Bax against George with Millie as victim

Themes: Unreliable narration, Doubles, twins and clones, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2010-04-15 Roy C. Lackey #

Asks whether a letter-date inconsistency in The Sorcerer's House is a Wolfe error or an unreliable-narrator clue, and what a roadside episode with a woman impersonating Doris says about Doris and Lupine. Raises the question of how Lupine obtained Doris's keys, grounding both points in quoted passages about Bax's letters and the car.
questions · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Bax's account is left-handedly unreliable; Lupine can take another human's guise

Letter #18 bugs me, for two reasons. First, it is addressed to Millie, and on p-132, right after the break, Bax wrote: "Joe came for the car. I know I wrote to George about that." Well, no, he didn't. The first mention of Joe coming out to look at the car was in the previous letter (#17, p-118), which was also addressed to Millie.

Is this a mistake by Wolfe, or is it a left-handed comment about the reliability of Bax's account of events?

Second, when Bax got back to Doris's car, a woman who apparently looked like Doris got in as the passenger, after he had offered to return her keys. He drove and suggested that they stop somewhere to eat. She said no. He started to say that he would take her back to her apartment, when they saw another woman standing by the road and waving. The waving woman proved to be Doris, and when Bax stopped for her the passenger vanished, leaving behind a funky smell.

A werewolf is defined in the book as a human who takes on the shape of a wolf. I wasn't aware that a werewolf could take on the guise of another human. (Nevermind being in two places at the same time.) What, if anything, does this episode say about Doris and Lupine? How did Lupine get Doris's keys?

-Roy

2010-04-16 Dave Tallman replying to James Wynn #

Proposes that Doris and Lupine in The Sorcerer's House are one person, once known as Mary King, who split off her soul to become a werewolf. Builds the case by walking through the first vanishing-passenger scene step by step, linking Mary King's vanishing on p. 200 to it, and citing Doris's home-economics major and her dealings with Martha Murrey.
proposes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Doris and Lupine are the same person; Doris was once Mary King; Lupine split off her soul to become a werewolf; Doris killed Star Paxton to lure Bax

Let's see if this all fits together. We have two vanishing passenger incidents involving Doris. One is pp. 132-133, and Bax definitely says this is Lupine. ("She was in here with you." "Yes. She was."). The other is Mary King on p. 200. Doris sees her vanish (or says she does).

The theory would be that Doris and Lupine are the same person, that she was once known as Mary King and split off her soul as part of the process of becoming a werewolf.

Breaking down the first appearance of Lupine:
1) Walking with Doris down to the river, "a hand slipped into mine." 2) Doris said "I think I'll turn around and go back" from some distance behind him. This is her sending her soul back away (so she can take Lupine form).
3) Her physical body remains with Bax, and the car keys remain in her possession.
4) Ghost-husband Ted appears, disapproving of this move. She dismisses him. 5) Winkle shows up to protect Bax ("A fox barked") and Lupine races away in wolf form.
6) Lupine talks to Bax by the water about heads, arms, etc. She gives Bax the car keys.
7) The ghost passenger looking like Doris gets into the car with Bax. She cannot drive and doesn't eat. She has the odor of Lupine, or of carrion. 8) They catch up with physical Lupine, who has run ahead and is now back in Doris form. The soul rejoins her, vanishing from the car.

Another interesting fact about Doris is that she was a home-economics major (p. 127). Such a major is very rare these days, but was more common in the past. This allows us to place her in Mary King's days.

Doris knew Martha Murrey and might easily have found out that Bax was dining at her home. She probably killed the neighbor Star Paxton (first victim of the Hound of Horror) at the time she did so that Bax would see the body.

This helps resolve the question of the identity of the "wonderful girl" in the challenge note on p. 261. We speculated it could be Doris or Lupine. Now it seems there is no reason it couldn't be both.

2010-04-16 Roy C. Lackey replying to James Wynn #

Argues Lupine took Doris's appearance, citing Bax's account of the musty odor and the keys a girl gave him, and speculates Mary King's ghost may be Lupine's soul. Answers James Wynn's question by quoting Bax's odor and key passages in The Sorcerer's House, and notes Bax asked about Mary King before picking up Shell.
answers · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Lupine took on Doris's semblance; Mary King's ghost might be Lupine's soul

{Hmmm...Why do you assume a bad smell means a werewolf? I thought it was vampires that had a bad smell.}

Just before Martha's neighbor, Star Paxton, was killed by a werewolf, Bax heard an animal, went outside the house and noticed "an odor--a faint, musty stench." (p-82)

After Doris complained of the odor in her car, Bax wrote, "I sniffed. If I had used my nose earlier, I would not have been deceived." (p-133) Doris asked how he came to have her keys. He said, "A girl I met down at the river's edge gave them to me." He went on to say, "She brought the smell that you complained about." (ibid.)

There is no doubt about it. According to Bax, Lupine took on the semblance of Doris and left behind a funky smell. Lupine also banished the apparition of the big man Bax had seen in the woods (p-134). The big man, we are told, looked like Ted (ibid.).

2010-04-16 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that the note about letters being out of sequence in The Sorcerer's House is a red herring from Wolfe's editorial persona, and that the letters are in a good functional order. Responds to James Wynn's claim that letters #21 and #25 being out of order does not explain things, saying it would take a smoking gun showing an event could not have happened in that order to convince him, drawing on his rereading of Delany's Dhalgren.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · author-and-biography · theories: Letter sequence in The Sorcerer's House is a red herring

To be honest, I believe that the comment that the letters "may" be out of sequence is a red herring, part of Wolfe's editorial "persona."

As someone who's spent years reading and rereading Delany's _Dhalgren_, a book where things are seriously out of order, I have to say that I just don't see the signs of it here. It would take a serious smoking gun, something that _could not_ have happened in that order, to really convince me.

Mind you, I'm not saying that the letters are all in "perfect" order -- or indeed that any such "perfect" order is possible. When you're dealing with letters from multiple people to each other there is always questions that arise of synchronization. But I suggest that the letters are in a good _functional_ order, one that, as best as possible, captures the order of events.

2010-04-16 Gwern Branwen replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Argues against the theory that Doris is Lupine, listing inconsistencies in age, Lupine's youthfulness, Doris's attitude toward Bax, and the plot logic of the drive scene. Responds to Roy C. Lackey's letter-dating puzzle by using the same kind of textual checking, and cites the endgame and letters as evidence against the identification.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Doris is Lupine

I don't like the Doris=Lupine approach. It doesn't gel with the endgame of TSH.

If the ghost is Dorpine's soul, then how do we reconcile this with Lupine selling her soul to a distant powerful wizard?

Doris is in her 30s or 40s at least, the home ec degree aside. Is this consistent with a callow teenager like Ieayan* falling in love with her and her with him? Lupine is clearly presented as youthful; I don't believe any accounts of werewolfs include rejuvenation or extraordinary longevity**.

How do we reconcile Lupin not especially liking and loathing Bax by the end with Doris's like of him? Even in her last letter, she's more wistful than hateful.

Why would Ambrosius or Goldwurm (whichever we think Ted was this phase of the moon) either buy Doris's soul or tell her how to do it?

Why is it Bax who suggests that on the drive where they are attacked by a werewolf, he stay outside in the risky area? If Dorpine is scheming his murder, she should suggest that. For that matter, Doris doesn't respond as Dorpine would, it seems to me. ('Curses! Foiled again!')

There may be some oddities in the drive scenes which are explained by Dorpine, but the more general considerations are difficulties.

* forgotten the right spelling
** Japanese _kitsune_ on the other hand are canonically nigh-immortal and can choose to appear as beautiful young women

2010-04-16 Roy C. Lackey replying to James Wynn #

Argues that moving letters #21 and #25 out of chronological order does not fully explain the timeline problems in Bax's letters in The Sorcerer's House. Responds to James Wynn by citing letter #21's references to Skotos, Mary King and George's jail, and letter #24's reference to #21's contents, showing the ordering conflict persists.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Letter reordering alone cannot resolve the TSH timeline

I can tell you right now that letters #21 and #25 just being out of chronological order doesn't quite explain things adequately. The reason is; in #21 Bax asked Shell about both Skotos and Mary King, and also talked about having sex with two women. He also mentioned in #21 that the incident with George in the lawyer's office had occurred the night before the day he was then writing. After leaving the lawyer's office that night is when he and Doris made the strange drive to the lake and back. In #24, Shell commented on the two-women sex angle and told what he had learned about Skotos. In #25, Bax told the psychic about picking up Mary King on the way home from the restaurant at the lake.

Letters #21, 22 and 23 all make mention of events of the night of the meeting with the lawyer and/or the drive to or from the lake restaurant, as do letters #25 and 26. Since letter #24 references the contents of letter #21, and letter #21 was written after George went to jail . . . well, what can I say? Somethin' ain't right.

-Roy

2010-04-17 Craig Brewer replying to brunians #

Argues that nothing fantastic happens in The Sorcerer's House and that the events are a con by Bax against his brother George, with Millie as the victim. Grounds this in Bax's earlier jailing for fraud, Millie's visits to a psychic, her jealousy of Doris in her first letter, and the compiler's note about when the letters arrived.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Nothing fantastic happens in The Sorcerer's House; Bax is conning George and Millie; Facefox is a ploy to exploit Millie's belief

I just finished my first VERY fast read, so pardon any incorrect details.

But I have to say that I'm taken with Thomas Bitterman's suggestion that nothing "fantastic" actually happened. I'm not sure how (or even if) one could prove it, but a number of things lead to the notion that what we have is just a complicated scam by one brother to scam another brother. We know that Bax was jailed for "fraud" of some sort, so it makes sense that he understands something about perhaps how to defraud real estate agencies or something of that nature, so there's at least the reasonable possibility that his "good fortune" with acquiring a house, etc., has quite other explanations. He knows about Millie's weaknesses (both in terms of her feelings for George and Bax and the fact that she already visits a psychic...who even tells Millie that she cons other patrons...but not Millie, of course). The compiler's note suggests that he got the letters from Millie after Bax disappears, so, perhaps this is all coming out after Millie realizes that she's been conned, etc. Millie is even jealous of Doris in her first letter, and the facefox could just be another way for Bax to play that angle up while also playing on Millie's belief in the supernatural. And I don't recall, but Bax never mentions anything supernatural in his letters to Shotgun, does he?

I'm starting again, so I'll have a better notion later. But after reading Millie's first letter to Bax and seeing her naivete, I started to wonder. Bitterman's post just made sense after that.
