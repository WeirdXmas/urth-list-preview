Immortals and Archaic Rome

Urth list · 8 messages · 2003-06-16 to 2003-06-19

2003urthChe MonroMatthew MalthouseJosh GellerJames WynnChris PAndrew BollenJames Jordan
The thread: Readers asked whether Latro, the amnesiac narrator of Wolfe's Latro books, could really be one of Xerxes' Immortals, given Herodotus's statement that they were all Persians, and whether a Roman unit serving with them made sense. James Jordan argued 'Persian' might mean Persian subjects and that adoption was common, while Matthew Malthouse rejected this, insisting ancient historians clearly separated Persians from conquered peoples. Andrew Bollen read the Roman presence as a deliberate departure from Herodotus, and Chris proposed the Romans were exiles rather than mercenaries, with Josh Geller supplying the Persian-infantry rationale. The thread drifted into lighter speculation about Egypt and the Eleusis temple, ending without resolution.
  • Latro Wynns: questions whether an amnesiac Roman could be an Immortal given Herodotus 7:83
  • James Jordan: 'all Persians' may mean Persian subjects; adoption was common
  • Matthew Malthouse: ancient historians distinguished Persians from conquered peoples, so the adoption reading fails
  • Andrew Bollen: the Roman unit is a deliberate departure from Herodotus, with Romans shown superior to hoplites
  • Josh Geller: Persians lacked infantry and would have valued Roman foot soldiers
  • Chris: Lucius's languages suggest exile at the Persian court rather than mercenary service

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Colonization and the native, War and soldiers

2003-06-16 James Wynn #

Questions whether Latro, the amnesiac narrator of Wolfe's Latro books, could really have been one of Xerxes' Immortals, citing Herodotus 7:83 which says they were all Persians. Adds that Latro's tactical skill and apparent youth are hard to reconcile, and places Rome's wars in the 5th century BC as context for why he left home.
questions · source-and-allusion · chronology-and-plot-logic

Chris says:

Crush recants:
Hmmm. Herodotus 7:83 says that the Immortals were "all Persians". So either Latro was not an Immortal, or Wolfe is engaging in literary license in presuming that Latro somehow gained a special honor. There *were* 10,000 of them so I guess it is not inconceivable that a Latin slipped in.

Does identifying Latro as the killer of Leonidas reveal anything? Her. 7:218 seems to suggest Leonidas was assaulted by the Immortals commanded by Hydarnes. But Her. 7:223 says he was assaulted by the "barbarians under Xerxes". So it could go either way.

But Latro is clearly a seasoned tactician. I seem to recall someone saying he looked to be in his early twenties. This has always been difficult for me to rectify. When could he have had the opportunity to study war analytically? I can't remember where to find Latro's dream of him going into battle with his fellow soldiers -- perhaps some a light could be shed from there. I remember it was from there that I got the idea he was some kind of officer.

It is true that Latium is not numbered among the armies of Xerxes. This is not surprising. In 478 BC, Rome's republic was about 30 years old -- Rome was only about 100 years beyond an age of legend. 150 years later, Rome was not even considered worth the trouble for Alexander the Great or his heirs to conquer. At the time of the Persian War, Rome was in precarious war with its neighbors Veii and the Aequi. It is interesting to me that Latro left his home to become a warrior when Rome was in nearly constant war from the late 6th century to the late 5th.

-- Crush

2003-06-17 James Jordan #

Argues that Herodotus's 'all Persians' for the Immortals (7:83) may mean subjects of Persia rather than a race, so Latro could have been an adopted Persian. Answers Crush's claim that Latro cannot be an Immortal by citing the varied racial makeup of Persia and common adoption in the ancient world.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Latro could have been an adopted Persian

Or maybe that "Persian" does not refer to race per se, but to those considered part of Persia, which was pretty multifarious racially. Latro could have been an adopted "Persian," since adoption of various kinds was quite common in the ancient world.

2003-06-18 Josh Geller #

Argues that the Persian army lacked infantry comparable to the Greeks and would have valued Roman infantry, offering a historical rationale for the Immortals including non-Persian troops. Responds to Andrew Bollen's quoted point about Herodotus and the Immortal Guard, supporting the idea that the Persian ranks drew on varied peoples, with the claim grounded in the Persians' hiring of Greek infantry.
supports · source-and-allusion

The Persians generally lacked infantry to match the Greeks. Before, during and after the war they went to some efforts to hire Greek infantry. They would have definitely found a use for a cohort or two of Roman infantry.

J.

2003-06-18 Matthew Malthouse #

Disputes James Jordan's suggestion that Latro could have been an adopted Persian, arguing that ancient historians clearly distinguished Persians from conquered peoples such as Medes, Parthians and Assyrians. Relies on the general ancient-historical distinction between origin and subject status, and on Herodotus's account of the Immortals, with a joking caveat about whether Herodotus invented it.
disputes · source-and-allusion

The ancient historians had a pretty clear idea of what were Persians and what were other nations conquered by the Persian Empire. Just as they made the distinction between Romans by origin, Roman citizens and subjects of Roman Empire. I don't know but I suspect that Medes, Parthians, Assyrians etc in the Immortal Guard would have been worth of comment.

Of course I'm assuming that Herodotus didn't just make it up. ;-)

Matthew

2003-06-18 Andrew Bollen #

Argues that Latro's Roman unit serving with the Persian Immortals at Thermopylae and Plataea is a deliberate departure from Herodotus, with the Romans presented as superior to the hoplite phalanx. Cites Mist chapters 37 and 24 alongside Herodotus 8.210-224, 9.59-9.65 and 9.31, noting the Immortals' absence from the Thermopylae account and the Romans' omission from the army catalogue.
informs · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Latro's Romans are a deliberate anachronism; Latro has a story with Demeter's goddess

Matthew says:

Anyway, the relevant passage is Ch 37 of Mist for Thermopylae:

Latro addresses "the hundred" (I guess he is acting as centurion, if that term existed then): "While the Immortals are gone, we could have no higher honor than to be the protectors of the Universal King ...".

Her 8.210-224 gives the background. The Medes go in on the first day, failing & making it plain to the King "that although he had plenty of troops, he did not have many men." The Immortals are then sent in, but they do no better - fighting in a restricted area, with spears shorter than the Greeks'. Then Ephialtes the traitor reveals a path thru the mountains, allowing the Persians to attack from a more favorable position. The engagement takes place outside the narrows; Persian conscripts whipped on by their officers die like flies (as in Latro's account); but in the end appear to overwhelm the Spartans by weight of numbers. The Spartans fight with swords, their spears broken, their spears broken, and Leonidas falls but with no deatils given. The Immortals are not mentioned.

So there's a blank space here concerning the Immortals, and the precise details of Leonidas' death, which GW has painted in. I think his premiss is that the light infantry Medes and bowmen, not just the conscripts but probably the Immortals also, have shown themselves inferior to the hoplite & the phalanx, and Latro's heavy-armed, disciplined Romans are recognized as being a better "force-on-force" solution. This of course makes sense historically, since the Roman tactical organization which GW describes (the two pila, the short stabbing sword etc) was to prove superior to the hoplite/phalanx combination, but maybe anachronistic?

For Plataea, the passage is Ch 24 of Mist:

Latro sees the red cloaks of the Spartiates in their falling-back manouevre. He sees Mardonius on his white stallion in the midst of the Immortals - so presumably the Roman unit is somewhere close by, I imagine for the reason I give above. The lightly-armed Medes and bowmen charge ahead, totally disrupting Latro's formation, and smash themselves up on the reformed Spartan shield wall. The Persian forces are completely disorganized during this charge, and are routed by the counter-attack.

"We broke ... Even when they made a new shield-wall we were only a mob behind it. The Medes took the spears in their hands and broke them, died. The arrows were no good ..."

This all squares reasonably well with Her 9.59-9.63. The "shield-wall" is the wall of the Persians' wickerwork shields mentioned in 9.61. The Lacedaemonians attack this in 9.62, knocking it down, and the battle moves to around the temple of Demeter. The Persians grapple their spears & break them, but are defeated due to their lack of body armor.

For whatever reason, it's clear that Latro's Romans have been given a place of honor, with the Persians, against the Spartans. Her 9.31 gives the disposition of his army, mentioning "only the most important ones by name - the ones which were particularly prominent and famous ...". The Romans *should* be included in this list, but of course they're not - hey, it's a novel.

Her 9.65 is interesting. "I find it surprising that although the battle took place by the gove of Demeter, not a single Persian either entered the precinct or died there ... I think the goddess kept them away because they ahd burnt her temple at Eleusis." Somewhere in that, I suppose, is the story of what happened between Latro, "bearer of the wolf's tooth", and the Goddess, which I hope we'll someday get to hear.

2003-06-19 Chris P #

Argues that Lucius's fluency in Greek, Persian and Phoenician suggests he lived long in a culturally diverse setting, making his Roman contingent exiles at the Persian court rather than mercenaries. Builds on the quoted post about Persian infantry, adding that the Persians' numerical advantage and supply problems make distant mercenary hiring seem odd.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Lucius and the Romans were exiles at the Persian court

Hmm. This, combined with some other posts, made me wonder about languages. Our Lucius was fairly fluent in Greek (even if his memory problem leads to some strange interpretation). IIRC he also was able to talk to Persians and Phoenicians in languages other than Greek. Perhaps he was simply very learned, but I tend to think that he had lived for quite some time in a culturally diverse environment. If Lucius and his contingent had been living amidst the Persian court for a fair amount of time, this makes it much more likely that they'd be exiles rather than mercenaries. Certainly the Persian kings were fairly generous to other exiles.

And now that I think about it, given the numerical advantage the Persians had and the obvious problems with keeping them supplied, it would seem odd that they would scrounge so far afield for mercenaries anyway.
