The thread: Readers on the Urth list used this thread to introduce steampunk and recommend books, with Wolfe's work mostly a side reference. Nicholas Gevers, Jay Lake's Mainspring and the Extraordinary Engines anthology were promoted by Gevers and Kerry Benton, while Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, Matthew Knight, Jordan Dennis and Andy Robertson offered other picks. Chris Laybourn, a newcomer, asked what steampunk is, and Benton called it a homage to Jules Verne. James Crossley argued that true steampunk needs technology-induced anxiety and a dystopic mood, and Steven Hall and Danehy-Oakes debated whether SF speaks mainly of its own era. Lane Haygood, in separate posts, answered Laybourn's question about whether The Urth of the New Sun is required reading. The thread ended without consensus, drifting into genre definitions.
  • James Crossley: true steampunk requires technology-induced anxiety and a dystopic mood, not just Victorian-style extrapolation
  • Kerry Benton: steampunk is a homage to Jules Verne and recommends Jay Lake's Mainspring
  • Nicholas Gevers: recommends Mainspring and the Extraordinary Engines anthology to Wolfe fans for their Wolfean echoes
  • Steven Hall: steampunk blends alternate history with science fiction, and SF reveals more about its own time than its imagined future
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: SF commenting on its era is sometimes deliberate and sometimes inadvertent, and Moore's League of Extraordinary Gentlemen is ironic about Vi
  • Lane Haygood: The Urth of the New Sun is not strictly required but is the only way to make sense of the first four books

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Time loops and causality

2008-05-13 Nicholas Gevers #

Announces an upcoming steampunk anthology, Extraordinary Engines, with a list of contributors, and recommends Jay Lake's Mainspring and Brian Stableford's Werewolves of London trilogy to Wolfe fans. Notes that Lake admires Wolfe and that Wolfean echoes appear in Mainspring, but gives no analysis of Wolfe's texts.
informs

In addition to the VanderMeer Steampunk reprint
anthology already mentioned in the current discussion,
I myself have an original steampunk anthology coming
out from Solaris in October: EXTRAORDINARY ENGINES.
All new stories from Kage Baker, Keith Brooke, Jeffrey
Ford, James Morrow, Ian R. MacLeod, Robert Reed, Jay
Lake, Adam Roberts, Marly Youmans, Jeff VanderMeer,
Margo Lanagan, and James Lovegrove.

For Wolfe fans, I'd recommend Jay Lake's steampunk
novel MAINSPRING--he's a big Wolfe admirer, and there
are certainly Wolfean echoes in the book.

Another steampunk epic worth reading is Brian
Stableford's Werewolves of London trilogy--lots of
metaphysics.

--Nick Gevers.

2008-05-13 James Crossley replying to Kerry Benton #

Argues that true steampunk must include technology-induced anxiety and a dystopic mood, not just Victorian-style science extrapolation, and that stories with atomic zeppelins or steam robots lacking such tension belong to other categories. Responds to Kerry Benton's synopsis of steampunk as homage to Jules Verne, drawing the distinction from the parallel with cyberpunk and suggesting terms like gaslight romance and retrofuturism.
disputes

To be pedantic for a moment, that's not the whole story. True steampunk isn't just an extrapolation of Victorian-era science, but has to include an element of technology-induced anxiety. In other words, steampunk, like cyberpunk, from which it gets its name, shows the discomforts of rapid advancements in technology, and its mood is usually dystopic.

If a story features atomic zeppelins or steam-powered robots without attendant tension, it's something else. Suggested terms include "gaslight romance" and "retrofuturism."

James

2008-05-14 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Steven Hall #

Argues that Steven Hall's claim that science fiction is about its own present rather than the future holds only in some senses, distinguishing ambitious SF that comments deliberately on its era from unambitious SF that does so inadvertently. Cites Moore's League of Extraordinary Gentlemen as steampunk whose ironic view of Victoriana could not be held by a Victorian, and Wells's War of the Worlds and Doctor Moreau as early SF about its own world.
disputes · genre-and-craft

I quibble: this is only true for some values of the words "about," "science fiction," and even "is."

I.e., there is no doubt that in some important sense _The Space Merchants_ "is about" the advertising/marketing industry of the 1950s. But there is another sense in which one can meaningfully say it is about a future extrapolated from that time.

I would take a much more nuanced approach and say:

Science fiction of any ambition comments deliberately on the world of the present in which it is written, by setting up a contrafactual world in dialogue with that world.

Science fiction lacking in ambition comments inadvertently on the world of the present in which it is written, by setting up a world that, failing to differentiate significantly from that world, depicts its commonplaces as if they were inevitable.

Put the word "some" before that sentence and I will have far less trouble with it. But, some of the best steampunk (I think for example of Alan Moore's _League of Extraordinary Gentlemen_ -- not to be confused with the grossly inferior movie based very loosely upon it) can meet that description only by an inversion, because it uses our current view backwards of the Victorian era in an ironic pose that would literally not be possible to one living in that era. (Not that irony would not be possible to a Victorian, you understand; only that this particular form of irony, based on a historical view of Victoriana, would not be possible without that perspective.)

Of course, "the science fiction of the Victorian era" existed, in the work of Verne and others; it merely did not yet have a name, or an ironic view. That was introduced very specifically by Wells, whose early SF was very much "about" the world in which he wrote it (e.g., _The War of the Worlds_ as commentary on colonialism, or _The Island of Doctor Moreau_ as commentary on "vivisection").

Rather: in the present, we go back to an imagined (and usually explicitly fictional) past to imagine its possible futures.

> I quibble: this is only true for some values of the words "about," "science
> fiction," and even "is."
> 
> I.e., there is no doubt that in some important sense _The Space Merchants_
> "is about" the advertising/marketing industry of the 1950s. But there is
> another sense in which one can meaningfully say it is about a future
> extrapolated from that time.
> 
> I would take a much more nuanced approach and say:
> 
> Science fiction of any ambition comments deliberately on the world of
> the present in which it is written, by setting up a contrafactual world
> in dialogue with that world.
> 
> Science fiction lacking in ambition comments inadvertently on the world
> of the present in which it is written, by setting up a world that, failing
> to differentiate significantly from that world, depicts its commonplaces
> as if they were inevitable.
> 
>> Steampunk, then, is a combination of alternate history and science fiction.
>>  It is what we imagine the science fiction of the Victoria era might have
>> been.
> 
> Put the word "some" before that sentence and I will have far less
> trouble with it. But, some of the best steampunk (I think for example of
> Alan Moore's _League of Extraordinary Gentlemen_ -- not to be
> confused with the grossly inferior movie based very loosely upon it)
> can meet that description only by an inversion, because it uses our
> current view backwards of the Victorian era in an ironic pose that would
> literally not be possible to one living in that era. (Not that irony would not
> be possible to a Victorian, you understand; only that this particular form
> of irony, based on a historical view of Victoriana, would not be possible
> without that perspective.)
> 
> Of course, "the science fiction of the Victorian era" existed, in the work
> of Verne and others; it merely did not yet have a name, or an ironic
> view. That was introduced very specifically by Wells, whose early
> SF was very much "about" the world in which he wrote it (e.g., _The
> War of the Worlds_ as commentary on colonialism, or _The Island of
> Doctor Moreau_ as commentary on "vivisection").
> 
>> Or to put it another way: in the present, we go back to the past to
>> re-imagine the future.
> 
> Rather: in the present, we go back to an imagined (and usually
> explicitly fictional) past to imagine its possible futures.
