godlings

Whorl list · 3 messages · 2001-04-19 to 2001-04-19

2001whorlThe Book of the Long SunRobert BorskiKevin J. MaroneyMichael Straight
The thread: Robert Borski proposed that "godling" is a generic Whorl term for any unusually large being, organic or mechanical, so the giant who lifts Horn and Pig need not be a physics-defying miracle, citing Horn's remark, the slower Whorl gravity, and a talus reading. Kevin Maroney disputed this, arguing that in the Long Sun books godlings are constructed servants of Mainframe regardless of body type, so Pig's small size fits poorly, and he asked whether the text shows the giant is flesh. Michael Straight argued that Pig's godlingness, shown by the remark about his ears, implies biological kinship with the big gods, granting the low-gravity point for the undines but doubting a bipedal body could scale up without augmentation. The thread ends without resolution, with the disagreement over whether godling means a constructed servant or a biological being left standing.
  • Robert Borski: godling is a generic Whorl term for any unusually large being, with low gravity explaining the giant
  • Kevin J. Maroney: godling refers to constructed Mainframe servants regardless of body type, so Pig's size does not fit
  • Michael Straight: Pig's godlingness implies biological kinship with the big gods; low gravity may allow large godlings but a biped needs augmentation

Themes: Gods, angels and the Increate, Robots, androids and artificial life, The dying sun and the cosmos

2001-04-19 Robert Borski #

Argues that "godling" is a generic Whorl term for any unusually large being, organic or mechanical, so the giant who lifts Horn and Pig need not be physics-defying miracles. Grounds this in Horn's remark in OBW linking godlings to giant gods, the slower Whorl gravity, the slow-walking doll in Hide's dream, and a talus reading.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Godling is a generic term for any large being; The giant who lifts Horn is a talus; Pig is an organic godling built by Mainframe technomagic; Godlings were designed to host the gods of Mainframe

Rostrum writing:

<I thought the main objection to the godlings is that in Act 1 (New Sun) the author goes into detail about why anything that big would have to live in the sea or collapse under its own weight and then in Act 3 he presents us with such a creature without giving us any hint of how it is possible, or even a sense that we're supposed to see this as a physics-defying miracle (and Typhon/Mainframe's wonders seem to be otherwise technological, unlike the "miraculous" wonders of Severian or Silkhorn's astral travel).>

<(Maybe our hint is in Act 2 at the talus construction plant, but then what do you do with the obvious hints that organic Pig (he eats, his eyes are flesh) is a godling?)>

Would it help to suggest that the term "godling" might be a generic one used by the human Whorlese to connote anything of unusual size, whether it's organic or mechanical? It's never used by anyone in Mainframe if I remember correctly; and the first time it's mentioned by Horn (OBW, 157), it's linked with his supposition that the gods themselves might be giant-sized. ("It may be that our gods did not come among us except by enlightment and possession because they were too large to do so; even the godlings that they send among the people now are, for the most part, immense.")

I've already argued that the godlings may have originally been designed to host the gods of Mainframe, of course, giving them an alternate raison d'être. How better to preserve their prestige and awesome nature than by taking on larger-than-life forms? Surely, whenever he got to where he was going, Pas was not going to spend the rest of his life as a computer entity. And there's evidence in the LS series that the gods of Mainframe can ride not only people and animals, but chems.

Some of you also seem to believe the gravity aboard the Whorl is tantamount to Urth's, but somewhere or other (can't find the actual passage), it's mentioned that any potential departees with cardiac problems would be better advised to stay aboard the Whorl because the strain is less on hearts there. So a giant being of the size that picks up Horn would not be subject to the same forces that preclude such magnitude here. Even if the Whorl's gravity were earth-normal, however, there's the dinosaur parallel--are the godlings really that much bigger than the largest of these? Plus we're also told by Horn that "[The godlings] move slowly," (RTTW, 162) implying that even with reduced gravity, there are constraining forces at work. Hide's dream also contains a doll, "walking slow," which seems at least to me an obvious parallel with Horn's slow-moving godling--implying perhaps that the godling who picks up Horn is indeed a talus. Pig--he's something else--an organic giant created by the same technomagic of Mainframe, only using different components. But I'd argue that neither Pig nor the Brobdinagian who picks up Horn are science-defying miracles, even by our own limited 21st century standards. Just godlings--at least as the inhabitants of the Whorl define them.

Robert Borski

2001-04-19 Kevin J. Maroney #

Argues that "godling" in the Long Sun books refers to constructed servants of Mainframe regardless of body type, so Pig's small size does not fit the term's usual sense. Responds to Robert B.'s suggestion that godling is a generic Whorlese term for anything unusually large, by asking whether the text shows the giant godling is flesh and noting that sea-giant restrictions would then not apply.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: godling means constructed servant of Mainframe

That seems contrary to the idea of "small godlings". If the Narrator is indeed thinking of Pig a small godling, it seems odd to call him that when he's only slightly larger than a normal human. It seems more likely that the term "godling" refers to "constructed servants of Mainframe" regardless of their body type.

Is there textual evidence that the giant godling is a flesh creature? I don't remember seeing any such clues. If they aren't, then the restrictions which apply to the sea giants don't apply to them. (I've made this point before and don't remember anyone commenting on it; please forgive me if I missed a previous response.)

2001-04-19 Michael Straight #

Argues that Pig's godlingness, shown by the remark about his ears, implies biological kinship with the big gods, and that size limits on godlings in a low-gravity Whorl are open to question. Responds to Robert Borski's argument that godlings may be large because of low gravity, granting the point about the undines' size while doubting that a bipedal body could scale up without augmentation.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Pig is a godling with divine kinship; godlings carry the gods to Blue

But one of the main arguments for Pig's demideity ("godlingness"?) is that comment about his ears, suggesting some kind of biological kinship with the big guy.

I agree, but if the godlings' purpose is to carry the gods to Blue, that kind of negates your argument that they can be big becuase the Whorl's apparent gravity is low.

I can't do the math myself, but I've read discussions of why a bipedal human-type mammal can't be as big as a dinosaur. Maybe with drastically different biology and prosthetic bones and extra hearts to pump the blood around it would be possible. The muscles would probably have to be augmented too.

But the point that the godlings don't seem anywhere near as big as the undines is a good one.

-Rostrum
