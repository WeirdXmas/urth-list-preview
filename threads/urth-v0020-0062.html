Dualism & horror

Urth list · 6 messages · 1998-10-18 to 1998-10-19

1998urthMark MillmanAlex David GrocePeter CashTony EllisMichael Straight
The thread: Participants debated whether horror fiction is inherently dualist, with Alga's thesis that it is inherently dualistic as the starting point. Peter T. Cash and Michael Straight argued the genre is pagan or only weakly dualist, citing Salem's Lot and the way its good draws power from evil. Mark Millman and Sergeant Rock disputed whether Judaism is pagan or Christian and where supernatural evil comes from, with Millman conceding that Job makes Satan explicit. Alex David Groce argued that supernatural good exists in horror, citing Aickman and Dante, and defended a real supernatural evil grounded in Christian thought. Tony Ellis contended that in Lovecraft the normal world is itself the Dark, so the dualist reading fails. The thread ended without resolution, drifting into general genre and theology argument with no Wolfe text cited.
  • Peter T. Cash: horror is pagan rather than dualist; suburban light versus darkness is weak dualism
  • Michael Straight: horror isn't truly dualist; good in Salem's Lot draws power from evil
  • Mark Millman: supernatural evil in Judaism may come from pre-Jewish Semitic paganism; Christian demonology assimilates pagan sources
  • Alex David Groce: supernatural evil is a real agent; Judaism and Christianity share a similar view of evil; Lovecraft's dualism is weak
  • Tony Ellis: in Lovecraft the normal world is the Dark, so the normal-versus-horror dualism fails
  • Sergeant Rock: Judaism is pagan or contains Christian elements; a void Satan implies the same for God's hosts

Themes: Suffering and sacrifice, Gods, angels and the Increate, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

Responds to Alga's thesis that horror is inherently dualistic and to his Manichaeism remarks, and says the poster finds supernatural evil silly because it grounds evil in human acts.

Alga, your thesis that horror is inherently (will that do instead of "necessarily"?) dualistic is so thought-provoking that I hate to spoil things by finding fault with it. Never having read King and having not the slightest desire to do so, I'll have to withold judgment. I do believe you're helping me understand why I've always despised the horror genre. Since I do believe in the God of Abraham, this "supernatural evil" stuff seems a trifle silly to me; I just don't get it. I know that God permits evil in the world--but it's evil done by us humans--it is not supernatural. If Satan exists, he's void, in the sense of having no substance--I believe the common metaphor is say he is like a shadow. (Hmmm....shadows in Wolfe's works...not an uncommon theme. Hmmm.)

I never meant to say it doesn't. Just that it's a mess, and maybe a bit of self-parody contained within the larger work.

1998-10-19 Michael Straight #

Argues that horror fiction is not truly dualist, since the good in Stephen King's Salem's Lot seems to draw power from evil, and asks whether any horror novels show a supernatural Good losing to an Evil that wins. Responds to Alga's thesis that horror is inherently dualistic, using a scene where holy water gives the heroes strength to break into coffins, and contrasts this with the Good being superior and needing no such methods.
disputes · theories: Horror is not inherently dualist

I thought it amusing that in Stephen King's book _Salem's Lot_, the good almost seems to draw power parasitically from the evil. I seem to remember a scene where the good guys pour holy water on their limbs or tools or something and when they get to where the coffins are hiding find that they have the extraordinary strength necessary to break in.

My first thought was, "How come that doesn't work when building a church or a homeless shelter?"

My impression is that horror is not dualist because either there is no comparable Good to oppose the Evil or the Good is so far superior to the Evil that it doesn't need to stoop to the same methods to oppose it.

I haven't read much horror, however. Are there many horror novels that have both a supernatural Good and Evil but the Evil wins? (Not counting stories where the apparent "Evil" turns out to be morally superior to the apparent "Good".)

-Rostrum

1998-10-19 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Lovecraft's cosmic horror offers only a weak dualism of Cthulu against human ignorance, while horror fiction can still portray supernatural good. Responds to Tony Ellis by citing Robert Aickman's stories 'The Same Dog', 'Meeting Mr. Millar' and 'My Poor Friend', Dante's Inferno, and an essay in Castle of Days.
disputes · theology-and-religion · genre-and-craft · theories: Lovecraft's universe offers only weak dualism; Horror fiction can depict supernatural good

Continuing the dualism & horror thread (it's not off topic--one of the essays in Castle of Days talks about the issue, so there's our Wolfe link. I don't remember the essay in detail, but one part goes something like "Fantasy has the gods under God, while in horror Satan kills and is killed by Cthulu."

Tony Ellis wrote-

Yes, if anything, Lovecraft's world is a kind of opposite of non-dualist orthodoxy--the only Real Game in Town is the Utter Abyssmal Eldritch Things from the Deep. I agree with Tony that (A) the horrors are, as Lovecraft constantly emphasizes, MUCH older than humanity and (B) even if Innsmouth and Arkham weren't just upstarts, they are hardly a real force for good. The only defense against the horror is ignorance (I think that's a paraphrase of part of "The Call of Cthulu"). If the Dualism is Cthulu & Co. vs. human ignorance, that's a pretty weak Dualism.

Perhaps out of the simplistic version of it--I think there are some good horror writers who still make use of the concepts. Even a supremely ambiguous writer like Robert Aickman (my favorite) often suggests, in his quiet way, that something is evil--the dog in "The Same Dog," or Mr. Millar in "Meeting Mr. Millar" or Parliament in "My Poor Friend." In fact, part of Aickman's genius is the way he subtly equates certain social trends with a kind of invisible and incomprehensible Evil.

I wouldn't call the concept of supernatural good a feeble concept--maybe seldom well done in a horror novel, but at least outside that context it's been one of the most powerful ideas in history (and in literature). I'd even stretch the definiton and say Dante's Inferno is a brilliant work of horror that (in the end, even without the Purgatorio and Paradisio) is based on the concept of a supernatural good--"Divine justice moved my maker."

1998-10-19 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Judaism and Christianity share a similar view of evil, though not an identical one, and defends the idea that supernatural evil genuinely exists as an agent with power. Responds to Mark Millman's objection that Judaism is not pagan or Christian by drawing on orthodox Christian thought about Satan, sin as disordered love, and Aquinas on the mystery of evil.
disputes · theology-and-religion

Yes, Judaic works would be another category. Although I can't really think of a work from a Jewish perspective involving a supernatural evil right off hand... And I suspect that any I could think of would have an attitude to it not unlike some Christian works. Judaism may not have Christian elements, but I'd be fairly silly as a Christian to deny their are Jewish elements in Christianity... Either way, you have some similarity of the view of evil (not identity, at all, but similarity).

Yes, although evil is a perversion of good, it does EXIST -- Satan has a will of his own--in the end he's "without substance" in one sense, but I think he's an agent capable of action and indeed, in orthodox thought, of great power in the world. Sins, after all, arise out of an improper degree or object of love, but still exist (by drawing substance from the good). This is sloppy language of course, but then St. Thomas can sound sloppy talking about the Mystery of Evil (or Free Will, which is inextricably linked).

1998-10-19 Tony Ellis #

Argues that in Lovecraft's fiction the normal world is itself the Dark, so the dualistic reading of normal versus horror fails. Responds to Alga and Tony Ellis's claim about Innsmouth and Arkham by saying the horror predates humanity and that the good-evil framing is simplistic; the post cites no Wolfe text and is largely a general genre argument.
disputes

Apologies for prolonging a thread that steered due west of topic some time ago, but this is getting interesting:

I'm on Alex's side. I don't think you can say that the normal world "opposes" the Dark, in a dualistic sense, because in Lovecraft's fiction the normal world _is_ the Dark. What we think of as normality is invariably revealed to be a delusion, or merely the narrowest slice of a much bigger, scarier universe. As for "Innsmouth or Arkham before the horrors came", it's another basic tenet of the stories that the horror was there _first_, inhabiting this world long before humanity turned up. Any pretty suburban streets are just a part of a transient blip in reality, cosmically speaking.

Interesting choice of pejorative there. <g>

Well, the usual expression is "supernatural horror" rather than "supernatural evil". Most of the more highly-regarded horror writers long ago grew out of the simplistic good\evil trap.

You should! There's some great writing out there. (And an even huger amount of crap, naturally.)

Off the top of my head... none (but there must be a few). The rule seems to be that if you have a supernatural Good, it has to win. That's probably why the horror I prefer doesn't have such feeble concepts in it in the first place.
