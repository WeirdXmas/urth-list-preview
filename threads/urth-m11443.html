The thread: Readers debated whether Ouen's mother is Dorcas, whether Ouen's father the boatman is deceiving about her death, and whether the chapter titles and epigraphs of The Book of the New Sun come from Severian or from a translator. Lee Berman and David Stockhoff argued the boatman is a deceiver using age arithmetic, while Ryan Dunn, Antonio Pedro Marques and Andrew Mason pushed back on the age margin and the photograph evidence. On the titles question, Stockhoff argued translator, Ryan Dunn questioned this, and Jeff Wilson and Gerry Quinn offered partial support or doubt. The thread ended in drift: the epigraph debate spread into lost sources and future history without resolution.
  • Lee Berman: Dorcas is not Ouen's mother's second child; the boatman father is a deceiver and Cas disguises Dorcas's name
  • David Stockhoff: Ouen's age makes childbirth death implausible; chapter titles and epigraphs were added by the translator
  • Ryan Dunn: Dorcas identity rests only on a photograph; chapter titles hint at hidden facts and Severian may have written them
  • Antonio Pedro Marques: a two-year margin does not rule out childbirth death; poetry may come from lost sources
  • Andrew Mason: Casdoe may be Dorcas's niece; literary fragments survive but author names probably did not
  • Jeff Wilson: Severian's book may have chapter divisions from opening words; Kipling borrowings and the boatman as self-deceiver

Themes: Unreliable narration, Books, libraries and writing, Fathers, mothers and children

Argues that Dorcas had only one child, Ouen, and that her dream of tiny clothes is a pregnancy metaphor rather than evidence of a later child, while noting Ouen's account is unreliable. Responds to Jane Delawney's and Jeff Wilson's points by weighing Dorcas's own words, the Greek span measurement, and whether the Claw could shift her body to a time before her death.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Dorcas had only one child, Ouen; Dorcas's tiny clothes dream is a pregnancy metaphor; Claw shifts Dorcas's body to before her death

Borski takes this to imply that Dorcas had a pregnancy and birth after Ouen. I agree that it is a strange (though not impossible) turn of phrase to refer to one's self at birth as "young". I would express it as "she died when I was born".

I, personally, consider Severian's grandfather to be a great deceiver so I don't take a lot of stock in the exact wording of anything he says.

Dorcas has a dream in which she is shopping for, "tiny clothes", holding her hands half a span apart. "Doll's clothing, perhaps". A greek span is the distance from the thumb to the pinky of a hand with splayed fingers. Half that would be too small for any born child to wear. I'm more inclined to interpret this dream as a metaphor for shopping for baby clothes while she is pregnant. I don't remember a memory of her looking after a child but I could be mistaken.

Dorcas says: "I have to go back and find out who I was and where I lived and what happened to me...."..."My child may still be alive- an old man, perhaps, but still alive. I have to know."

She doesn't say "children" so I think her own sense is of having only one child.

I agree with this. On the other hand, Dorcas says, "I was dead, a shrunken corpse preserved in the brown water." She guesses that the Claw works by shifting time. If correct I suppose there is no reason it would have to work to shift her body to her moment of death. Her body could be shifted to a time/condition before her death, perhaps even before her pregnancy.

I don't think the text is silent on the subject of a second child but spread across a couple books and vague and perhaps conflicting.

Responds to Lee Berman, who quoted Borski's reading and disagreed with the wording, by offering an alternative explanation for the discrepancy and asking what else it might hide.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Dorcas killed herself during Ouen's first year; Ouen's father concealed Dorcas's suicide

I have noticed this as well, but in a society in which quantification is not precise, and a mother can die several days after giving birth, this may be exactly correct as far as Ouen knows.

However, if this were a Gothic mystery, we would assume the father was at least hiding something to protect his son---such as, she killed herself some time during Ouen's first year. Most people raised on such half-truths would not question such a small discrepancy.

What else could this hide?

2010-12-06 Jeff Wilson replying to David Stockhoff #

Proposes that the boatman's story of Dorcas's eyes opening as she is dropped in the sea shows anxiety that she was still alive, so he may be a self-deceiver who blames his neglect for her death. Responds to David Stockhoff's Gothic-mystery reading of Ouen's father, extending it with a 'Madeline Usher' comparison and a link to the Claw rewinding Dorcas to pre-parity.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Ouen's father is a self-deceiving liar who neglected Dorcas; Dorcas died of a markless illness while the boatman was away

The boatman's story about her eyes opening as she is dropped in suggests he has some anxiety that she was still alive somehow. This might mean that she died mysteriously with no marks, or that she happened to die while he was out from some lingering, markless cause and he is wracked with guilt that his neglect did her in and desperately hopes that she is just doing the Madeline Usher so he can be forgiven.

This would make him still a deceiver, but a more human self-deceiver above all. And if she simply never regained her full health after Ouen's difficult birth, the "in childbirth" lie keeps to the Book's theme of liars revealing the truth despite themselves, and would explain why the Claw would rewind her to pre-parity.
