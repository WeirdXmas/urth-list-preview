Themes: Memory and forgetting

1999-03-01 Peter Stephenson #

Argues that T. S. Eliot's Prufrock should not be read as a specific individual but as a general figure of modern impotence and apathy, and so has few structural resonances with Wolfe's Peace. Contrasts Weer in Peace, whose actions have wide effects on the novel's universe, with the passive Prufrock, and invites specific thoughts from others.
disputes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography

I would tend to caution against seeing Prufrock as anyone too specific --- he may well be a horny old man, but Eliot is saying `there's a lot of it about'. To me he stands for all (or much of) the impotence and apathy of the modern world, just as, for example, the `small house agent's clerk' in the Wasteland (`one of the poor on whom assurance sits Like a silk hat on a Bradford millionaire', not guaranteed to be an exact quotation) stands for its degeneration and pettiness.

For that reason, I don't think there are many general structural resonances between Prufrock and Wolfe, even Peace, since Wolfe's major characters often have wide-reaching, if subtle, effects on the universes of the novels --- Weer certainly does, however negative they may seem in retrospect; if Weer's tale is of being part of other people's stories (which is a very nice idea), then to a lot of them he's been a golem in the gears, which Prufrock was never capable of being. But I'm agog to hear any specific thoughts.

till human voices wake us,
corncrake
