Themes: Mirrors, pictures and art

2014-09-10 Marc Aramini #

Admits he cannot find a big-picture reading of Forlesen, the Wolfe story, beyond vague ideas about work and creativity, and lists its odd details: oil everywhere, mirrors that misreflect, a cop tethered to his car, moving road pillars. Compares its shifting construction to a Sturgeon story and says no objective bottom to the work seems to exist.
chats

sorry I was sending an attachment of a word document to finish working on the write up and in my autopilot state I sent it to the urth list instead of myself.

A big picture for Forlesen continues to elude me besides "work is hell" and an empty exploration of a creativity problem in which actors run through the motions of pointless working life on Earth. Oil everywhere, Forlesen finds a book detailing what he just went through with one word changed, glass and mirrors do not show what they should, the cop is hooked up to his cop car by tubes, the pillars holding up the road move, wild horses wind up in a dog food factory ... the whole set is constantly under construction like that one Theodore Sturgeon story where a guy accidentally sees the set up for tomorrow in progress. Forlesen thinks of his feet as shoes at first, as if he has put on the flesh as a costume.

This is the only work of fiction written before 1987 that I feel there might be no objective bottom to.

2014-09-11 Gerry Quinn replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that the novel Peace reflects Wolfe's guilt over his work in corporate middle management, and suggests the story's meaning lies in that experience. Responds to Marc Aramini's admitted failure to find a big picture for Forlesen by offering the office-life reading and a possible echo of Pohl's Tunnel Under the World.
proposes · author-and-biography · theories: Forlesen reflects Wolfe's guilt over middle management work

Bah, I spent enough time in middle management for that to be enough to explain the story (I still think _Peace_ embodies Wolfe's guilt for his part in creating the disgusting 'Pringles').

Aside from that, maybe echoes of Pohl's 'Tunnel Under The World'.

- Gerry Quinn
