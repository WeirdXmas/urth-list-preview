The thread: The thread opened with Tim Walters urging close readers to tackle John Crowley's Aegypt, but it drifted toward Edward Whittemore and Pynchon. Zvi Gilbert proposed discussing the Aegypt series and its historical figures, and Adam Thornton pointed to Frances Yates as background. Son of Witz recommended Whittemore's Sinai Tapestry, which David Stockhoff linked to Wolfe's alternate history. A dispute over whether Crowley's prose is silly or highbrow, with Stockhoff and Son of Witz disagreeing, ended unresolved, and many replies were chatty asides rather than close readings of Wolfe's text.
  • Tim Walters: Crowley's books are less confusing than Wolfe's but reward rereading
  • Zvi Gilbert: Crowley's Engine Summer and Little, Big are not like Wolfe; discuss Aegypt instead
  • Son of Witz: Whittemore's Sinai Tapestry shares puzzling, time-paradox qualities with Wolfe; Crowley's prose is straight-faced and highbrow
  • David Stockhoff: Crowley's passage sounds silly, and Whittemore's forged-Bible plot parallels Wolfe's conflation of miracle and deceit
  • Dave Lebling: Whittemore and Quinn resemble Pynchon more than Wolfe, Vance or Crowley
  • James Wynn: Short Sun books remain in print and are sold online

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Myth and folklore sources, Time loops and causality

2008-12-17 Zvi Gilbert #

Argues that John Crowley's Engine Summer and Little, Big are not confusing and are not like Wolfe's New Sun, and says a discussion of them would turn into praise. Proposes instead discussing Crowley's four-novel Aegypt series, whose characters such as John Dee and Giordano Bruno are drawn from history, and asks for an erudite reader to explain the historical sources.
proposes · source-and-allusion

I don't find either of those books particularly confusing. Wonderful, brilliant, and exquisite, yes; but they're not New Sun-like. I suspect a discussion of it for me would devolve into quoting favorite lines and swooning.

However, Crowley's long and complex four novel series Aegypt (The Solitudes, Love & Sleep, Daemonomania, Endless Things) is something that I would love to hash over with interested parties. As many of its characters and situations are drawn from history (John Dee, Giordano Bruno, and so forth), I would just like some particularly erudite person to lecture at me about the source material -- I suspect I would learn a lot that way.

--Zvi

2008-12-17 Adam Thornton replying to Zvi Gilbert #

Recommends Frances Yates as the key background reading for John Dee and Giordano Bruno, the historical figures in John Crowley's Aegypt series that Zvi Gilbert mentioned. Offers this from the poster's own graduate study of Renaissance esoteric material, in a light, joking tone without close engagement with Wolfe's text.
informs · source-and-allusion

I, er, might be able to help.

I did my Master's minor concentration in something my advisor and I agreed not to call "Renaissance Weird Shit."

It's all a bit fuzzy now, of course, but the main point is: read Frances Yates. She *did* look rather like the White Queen. I don't know if her sister looked like the Red Queen. Anyway, most of what you need to know about Dee and Bruno (at least for the purposes of reading Crowley) you can get out of Yates.

Adam

2008-12-17 Dave Lebling replying to Son of Witz #

Compares Edward Whittemore's Jerusalem Quartet and Quinn's Shanghai Circus to Pynchon, arguing that their episodic structure and effect-driven style resemble Pynchon more than Wolfe, Vance or Crowley. Notes that Wolfe tells plotted stories obscurely while being traditional on the surface, and that Whittemore's books were recently reissued in trade paperback.
proposes · genre-and-craft

I've read all of the Jerusalem Quartet books, and Quinn's Shanghai Circus, which has a similar style but is more-or-less unrelated. They actually remind me more of Pynchon than Wolfe or Vance or Crowley, though it's hard to articulate why. Probably because the whole quartet has an overarching theme and some consistent macguffins but is mostly episodic. I like them, but then I like Pynchon, and Flann O'Brian, and other strange more-or-less modernist types. Wolfe tells stories with plots, but tells them obscurely. Pynchon's books, and Whittemore's, have plots but they are at least in my opinion secondary to the desire to produce effects. Wolfe is in many ways a very traditional writer on the surface.

Whittemore was out-of-print for a long time, but recently (the last couple years) his books were all republished in rather nice trade paperback editions.

-- Dave Lebling, aka vizcacha

2008-12-17 Son of Witz replying to Zvi Gilbert #

Recommends Edward Whittemore's Sinai Tapestry to Urth readers, describing it as a puzzling, strange book whose symbols only made sense weeks after reading. Notes possible appeal to New Sun fans through its Jerusalem setting, repeated anchors in history, and time paradoxes, responding loosely to Zvi Gilbert's mention of Crowley's Aegypt.
proposes

anyone read Edward Whittemore's Sinai Tapestry.
that's a strange sort of puzzle. Odd book. One of those ones where I didn't like it until about a month later, when, not having thought about it since closing the pages, the symbols jumped out and started making sense. very curious work. I'm not sure if it's scifi or not. The cover would lead you to believe it, and many elements. I suppose New Sun fans might find a lot worth pondering. Jerusalem, repeating Anchors in history, time paradoxes. I just found out it's the first of the Jerusalem Quartet. I suppose I should read the others. very strange. the blurb on the cover compared it to LOTR, which left me scratching my head big time. ~witz

2008-12-18 David Stockhoff replying to Zvi Gilbert #

Argues that Edward Whittemore's Sinai Tapestry, with its forged oldest Bible, parallels Wolfe's alternate history in The Book of the New Sun, where miraculous truth and mundane deceit are conflated. Grounds this in a quoted description of the novel and points to its line about Mary, Fatima and Ruth being confused, which he sees recurring on Urth.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Urth's religious history is a forgery like Wallenstein's Bible

Speaking of the Jerusalem Quartet---I'm not very familiar with it though I have read reviews. A quick google came up with the following description:

Sinai Tapestry, originally published in 1977, is the first book of Edward Whittemore's Jerusalem Quartet: four novels that make the long, complex history of the Middle East comprehensible as no other books do, and that do so by creating an alternate version of history - part real and part imagined (and what pleasure, while reading, to speculate on what, in the novels, is real and what imagined!) - that begins, in this first book, by telling the story of how early in the nineteenth century, Skanderbeg Wallenstein, a fanatical Trappist monk from Albania, comes upon what is "with out question the oldest Bible in the world" and discovers that "It denied every religious truth ever held by anyone"

What would happen, then, he wonders - in ways not so different from the actual speculations of twentieth century Biblical scholars - "if the world suddenly suspected that Mohammed might well have lived six centuries before Christ" or that Christ had been a minor prophet in the age of Elijah" or "that the virtues of Mary and Fatima and Ruth had been confused in the minds of later chroniclers and freely interchanged among them?"

"Melchizedek must have his City of Peace," Wallenstein. concludes, just as men must have their Jerusalem" Believing that faith must be sustained in the world, Wallenstein also believes that if the cause for faith is absent, then it is his duty to provide it. "The decision he had made in his cell," Whittemore tells us, "was to forge the original Bible."

But this forgery - what has led to it, and what issues from it-becomes, in Sinai Tapestry, an imaginative conceit that informs the entire Quartet: It is Whittemore's way of asking us to consider the many ways in which illusions can give birth to realities, by which realities can be transformed by dreams, and - above all - through which the real and the imagined can conspire to create those events and legends that determine how we live, love, and die.

At a high level of abstraction, this sounds not very different from TBotNS. Wolfe also forges an alternate history but on a vaster scale, a history in which the miraculous truth and mundane deceit are conflated. Note the line about Mary and Fatima and Ruth; after chiliads, the same has occurred on Urth. And the last paragraph especially.
