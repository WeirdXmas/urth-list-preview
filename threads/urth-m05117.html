Lackey asked who the voices in Gid's hopper in An Evil Guest are, and whether readers should have recognized them from another source such as the Squid god, though he offered no answer. James Wynn proposed they are the Gray Neighbors from the Wrap Party, arguing Chase is a wizard, and Dave Tallman argued they are bound spirits modeled on Tempest characters acting on their own agendas. The thread drifted from the voices question toward Wolfe's pulp influences and ended without any settled reading of the voices.

2008-12-11 James Wynn replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Proposes that the voices in Gid's hopper are the Gray Neighbors from the Wrap Party at the book's start, answering Roy C. Lackey's question. Argues this shows Chase is a wizard, not merely a scientist, citing his possession of Cassie by an Aphrodite-daemon, and jokes about a juvenile series.
answers · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Gid's hopper voices are the Gray Neighbors; Chase is a wizard, not a scientist

I thought they were "Gray Neighbors" mentioned at the Wrap Party at the beginning of the book. The fact that they are there means that Chase is a wizard, not merely a scientist or high-tech fixer, which we knew when he had Cassie possessed by an Aphrodite-daemon.

If Wolfe had made a few very slight modifications to the book, he would have had a nice juvenalia series here.

J

2008-12-11 Dave Tallman replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Proposes that the voices in Gid's hopper are bound spirits modeled on The Tempest's Ariel, Caliban and the Shapes, acting on their own agendas when their master is absent. Grounds the reading in parallels between the voices' behavior and Tempest characters and in the night Cassie was alone in the car without hearing voices.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Gid's hopper voices are bound spirits modeled on The Tempest

The voices make me think of spirits bound in service to a wizard. If there's a specific literary reference, it may be to *The Tempest*: 1) The voice like the wind is like Ariel, the airy spirit. 2) The deep voice that tries to tempt Cassie to drink whiskey is like Caliban, who gets drunk along with Stephano and Trinculo. 3) The tinny voice that mocks the car's windows is like the Shapes that act with "mocks and mows."

Bound spirits often try to carry out their own agendas when their master is not closely controlling them. Chase thought it would be dangerous for Cassie to be in the car by herself. She tricked him so he had no opportunity to give orders to the spirits, so they tried to tempt her into trouble.

There was another time when Cassie was alone in the car and didn't hear voices -- the night she first met Chase. Either the spirits were not there then, or they were under orders not to speak. (They may have been under orders to cause Cassie to fall asleep. It seems odd that Chase was counting on her staying asleep so he could hop the car to Canada. It's doubtful she would have fallen asleep on her own after being called out in the middle of the night to wait in a car in a bad neighborhood).

2008-12-11 Matthew Keeley replying to James Wynn #

Argues that Gideon in An Evil Guest is a pulp hero like Batman or the Shadow, and that the book feels oddly nostalgic for a future setting. Asks whether Wolfe has said much in interviews about his childhood reading, since his books suggest influences such as Oz and the pulps. Responds to James Wynn's remark that the book could be juvenilia.
questions · author-and-biography · theories: Gideon is a pulp hero like Batman or the Shadow

As has been stated elsewhere, Gideon is (or pretends to be) the sort of pulp hero that kids of all ages love - he's dashing, he's mysterious, he has a cool car. He's like Batman or the Shadow. It's odd how nostalgic a book ostensibly set in the future is.

Are there any interviews where Wolfe talks much about his childhood reading? You can infer a lot from his books (Oz, the pulps, etc.), but I'd love to see some sort of list.

-Matt

2008-12-11 Gwern Branwen replying to Matthew Keeley #

Reports that in interviews collected in Shadows, Wolfe recalled reading pulp science fiction and mystery magazines bought at the pharmacy as a child, reading in hiding. Responds to James Wynn's question about Wolfe's childhood reading and the pulp flavour of Gideon in An Evil Guest, adding that Wolfe probably kept no list of what he read.
