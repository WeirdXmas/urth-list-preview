The thread: Posters with no subject line argued over whether the atomic bombs or a naval blockade and Soviet invasion of Manchuria forced Japan's 1945 surrender, and whether the bombings were justified. James Wynn argued the bombs were decisive, while Thomas Bitterman argued the blockade, bombing, and Soviet entry mattered more. Lee Berman called the bombings a crime and favored containment, and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes called the Soviet move an opportunistic late gesture. The exchange drifted into a historical dispute with no Wolfe text discussed, and Bitterman eventually admitted it had strayed far from the list's subject.
  • James Wynn: atomic bombs were decisive and a blockade could not have forced surrender
  • Thomas Bitterman: blockade, bombing and Soviet invasion of Manchuria drove the surrender, not the bombs
  • Lee Berman: the atomic bombings were a crime and containment would have been right
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Soviet invasion was a late, opportunistic move after Japan was already finished

2011-01-19 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Thomas Bitterman #

Argues that the Soviet invasion of Manchuria was a late, opportunistic move made after Hiroshima, when Japanese surrender was already certain and the region was overstretched. Responds to Thomas Bitterman's point about the invasion's size and timing, which he had supported with Wikipedia, and calls it a "us-too" action. This is an off-topic historical exchange with no reference to Wolfe's books.
disputes

The Soviet invasion of Manchuria was a bad joke - AFTER we had dropped the Hiroshima bomb, and Japanese capitulation was certain, they marched into a conquered area where the Japanese were grossly overstretched and the native population were going to hail them as liberators. A total "us-too" invasion.

2011-01-19 James Wynn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that a naval blockade and continued bombing could not have forced Japan's surrender in 1945, and that the atomic bombs were decisive. Rebuts Thomas Bitterman's position by citing the Cold War context, the lost Okinawa and island campaigns, the timing of surrender after Nagasaki, and comparisons with containment of North Korea and Iraq.
disputes

As in North Korea? Do you really think we could have pulled off such a blockade during the Cold War with China and the Soviet Union right off of Japan's coast? I think the idea that the bombing wasn't THE factor in the surrender is extremely wishful thinking. There was likely a Japanese ministers or two who thought privately that surrender would be a good idea. But the military and emperor called the shots. If the Japanese didn't surrender after the loss of Okinawa, it is improbable that they would have capitulated to the loss of some small islands (which the Soviets didn't take until after Hiroshima). And anyway, they didn't surrender after the loss of those islands. They surrendered after Nagasaki.

"War //is the remedy that our enemies have chosen, and I say let us give them all they want."
~ William T. Sherman

I appreciate that sentiment. However, knowing how containment has worked for North Koreans and Iraqis, I am unconvinced that would have been the humane choice. Compare Japan to North Korea. Compare SOUTH Korea to North Korea.

Actually, if you had been President in 1945 and sent 10s of 1000s of young men to die to liberate remote Pacific Islands and then said "We'll just spend the next 40 years watching Japan's borders." You probably would have been impeached. If you had chosen to invade Japan rather than end the war quickly with a couple bombs, you certainly would have been impeached. We elect presidents to make unpleasant choices: Take it in the face or fight back. If Americans had wanted half-measures, they'd have run the country from Congress.

2011-01-19 Lee Berman #

Argues that using atomic bombs on Japan in 1945 was a crime against humanity, and that a containment strategy would have been the right choice. Responds to James Wynn's devil's-advocate case for the bombings by weighing hindsight against the wartime mindset; no Wolfe text is discussed.
disputes

I certainly allow it, if I am permitted to take Angel's advocate role for a minute. It is a simplistic, cruel view of life to say that if an enemy Emperor orders the genocide of his people that we ought to be the perpetrators of it. Somehow that is the right thing to do?

If I were transported back in time and installed as US president I would order something along the lines of a N. Korea strategy. Containment. One cannot in good conscience commit a crime against humanity, kill hundreds of thousands of innocent people (including American POWs) in the "hopes" (and it was simply hope at the time) that it might avert a worse killing.

However, if I were actually from that primitive time period when declarations of victory and national pride were so much more important than human life than they are now I would probably think differently. If I thought as I do now I almost assuredly would never be elected president then.

I agree with this. But it is hindsight. A global blessing that couldn't have been foreseen (after WWII many were already looking to WWIII). If I had been preident in 1945 and enforced a containment strategy on Japan (and avoided assassination) I don't know if we would have had Pax Americana for the next 65 years. We'll never know. But as a 21st century resident I know could never nuke any nation. (meaning I couldn't be elected president now either, esp. if I spoke that publicly ;- )).
