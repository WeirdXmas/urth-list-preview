Proposes that the universe is shaped like a torus, with the black hole or White Fountain at its center, so that time and creation circle its surface and pass through the center repeatedly. Replies to Lee Berman's remarks on the ring of stars and Malrubius' words, offering the poster's own cosmology as a personal belief that makes the book resonant rather than citing textual evidence.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: The universe is a torus with the White Fountain at its center

I have always thought the shape of the universe was a torus. Hawking described a globe which had opposing poles that were the Big Bang and Big Crunch. It seems obvious to me that a better model is a torus, the center of which is this Black Hole/ White Fountain. Time and creation whirl around the surface and through the center again and again. (this is actually a cosmology I believe in, which may be why One of the reasons I find this book so resonant.)

Because it is outside our time-space and are capable of perceiving the breadth of temporality within that time bubble in a multi-linear way. Don't over think what is a simple metaphor. They escaped the wheel of time. They are outside of any time-space that concerns us.

2010-12-17 Lee Berman #

Argues that the cosmic cycle in The Book of the New Sun is a spiral in which the end of one universe meets its beginning, and that Yesod is both forward and backward in time. Builds on Malrubius' words and the Severian passage about the fuligin vortex, and suggests Wolfe deliberately writes these ideas in a self-contradictory way to show their vastness.

The parenthetical part is as Severian describes it. The beginning is at the same point as the end.

Normally when we think of sequences, there has to be a beginning of one element distinct from the end of the previous element. Otherwise it is just circular. The closest I am able to picture is a spiral shape where the end of one coil is right there at the beginning of the next. Maybe that's how Wolfe meant us to picture it. (Yesodis like the helix)

Consider also there is the issue of the location of Yesod. In last year's discussion we learned there are text passages suggesting it is forward in time from Briah and text passages suggesting it is backward in time. How can it be both?

I get the impression Wolfe is trying to describe some of these concepts in a confusing, self-contradictory manner to make the point that these things are too vast and n-dimensional for a human brain to comprehend. (I think Frank Herbert does the same thing to describe Paul Atreides' pan-temporal prescience in the early Dune books).
