Gene Wolfe is for Socialists

Urth list · 19 messages · 2010-12-16 to 2010-12-21

2010urthThe Book of the New SunLane HaygoodMatthew KnightMatthew KeeleyRyan DunnJeremy FrancisLee BermanAntónio Pedro MarquesPedro PereiraDavid StockhoffJames WynnGerry QuinnJeff Wilson
The thread: Readers debated whether a blog post claiming Gene Wolfe is a socialist writer, built around The Fifth Head of Cerberus and a China Miéville quotation, holds up. Pedro Pereira, Jeremy Francis, Matthew Keeley and Lane Haygood argued the blog overstated the case or that the book does not advocate socialism, while Matthew Knight and António Marques defended a socialist lens as valuable. Jeff Wilson read the novel as a dystopia where liberation technologies become new oppression, and Lee Berman read it as colonized natives subverting their colonizers. The thread drifted into Exultant biology and The Book of the New Sun, and ended without agreement, with Gerry Quinn and Pedro Pereira's later posts moving to personal tastes in Miéville and Kraken.
  • Pedro Pereira: the blog overstates Miéville's claim; readers project their own views onto Wolfe
  • Jeremy Francis: a socialist reading is wishful thinking because the colonizer-native identities are too vague
  • Matthew Knight: a socialist reading finds real value in the book, and good art can be read from many angles
  • Jeff Wilson: the novel is a dystopia where liberation technologies become tools of new oppression
  • Lane Haygood: the book explores socialist themes without advocating socialism
  • Lee Berman: the deeper message is colonized natives imitating, infiltrating and subverting colonizers

Themes: Colonization and the native, Power, rulers and politics, Slavery and service

2010-12-19 Pedro Pereira replying to Jeremy Francis #

Argues that the blog post titled Gene Wolfe is for Socialists overstates its case, since the quoted writer (Mielville) never calls Wolfe a socialist and only describes The Fifth Head of Cerberus as a meditation on colonialism, identity and oppression. Responds to Jeremy Francis's charge of wishful thinking by pointing to the blog's actual wording and noting that readers project their own views onto Wolfe.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolfe is a religious Republican with tragico-Catholic perspective; Fifth Head of Cerberus is about colonialism and oppression, not socialism

I don't see why is it wishful thinking on the part of the blog author (it's not even the author, this is a list by Mielville for the blog site). Mielville didn't claim Wolfe was Socialist or that his work was socialist bent. What he says is this:

Refering to the Fiht Head of Cerberus:
Wolfe is a religious Republican, but his tragico-Catholic perspective leads to a deeply unglamorized and unsanitized awareness of social reality. This book is a very sad and extremely dense, complex meditation on colonialism, identity and oppression.

Now, everyone interprets Wolfe's work according to his own views, and that is pretty clear in this Urth List too. Mielville may certainly see the work as emphasizing a speculation on colonialism, identy and oppression (since those are possibly the things that most ring a bell to him and touch him) but nowhere do I see any hint that Mielville sees this work as "Socialist", as you seem to imply.

Pedro

2010-12-19 Jeremy Francis replying to James Wynn #

Argues that a blog's claim that Gene Wolfe is a socialist writer is wishful thinking, since The Fifth Head of Cerberus is postcolonial but its colonizer-native identities are too vague to support a socialist purpose. Adds that if VRT is a despicable figure, as Borski reads him, the political reading becomes even more muddled, and credits the author with missing the identity difficulties.
disputes · author-and-biography · close-textual-reading · theories: Fifth Head of Cerberus is postcolonial but not socialist; VRT is a despicable character

Wishful thinking on behalf of the blog author, I think. Fifth Head is a good post-colonial text but, given how vague and variable (depending on your interpretation) the identity between the colonial powers and the natives is, attributing any socialist purpose is pretty difficult. Further, if you buy Borski's interpretation of the character VRT as being a fairly despicable sort (I do), the issue becomes even more confused. The writer might not pick up on the identity questions as being as difficult as they are, though, which doesn't necessarily fault him (God, if not for this list I wouldn't pick up half of what goes on in Wolfe's books).

Argues that a socialist reading of The Fifth Head of Cerberus can find real value in the book even though Wolfe's outlook differs sharply from the critic's. supports · genre-and-craft

I'm with Pedro on this one. I think the list's author is finding something of value in 5HOC when viewed through a socialist lens, despite recognizing vast differences between Wolfe's perspective and his own. Here's my own sweeping generalization that is likely logically unprovable: most good art can be appreciated from a variety of angles, insofar as the art reflects something true about life and the angles from which the art is viewed are able to discern some kind of truth, even if distorted.

2010-12-19 Jeff Wilson replying to Matthew Knight #

Argues that The Fifth Head of Cerberus is a dystopia about working people, where the narrator's gang of junior exploiters rises only to find themselves exploited, so piecemeal removal of rulers changes nothing and wider revolution is implied. Responds to Matthew Knight's socialist reading by reading the novel's technologies of liberation as tools of new oppression.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: Fifth Head of Cerberus is a socialist dystopia

It's not such a stretch to see how a general dystopia is also a dystopia for the working people. "5HC" is even a kind of "fiend confessional", where the narrator describes a world where people are openly bought, sold, and rented, how he and his gang of junior exploiters seek to steal and marry their way up the ladder of oppression, only to find that they are just as much exploited by their seniors, and piecemeal removal of the ruling progenitors ultimately changes nothing. (Implying wider-scale revolution is needed.)

From an adventure SF POV, "5HC" is a wonderfully subversive story where the speculative technologies of freedom or liberation from want (FTL, AI, and cloning, in once sense or another) are used instead to reach new places to rule, to make more efficient overseers over the working prisoners, and to make new races of exploiters and exploited for better returns, a good wake-up call to life's realities outside the neighborhoods of most US/UK/Commonwealth readers.

disputes

Argues that China Miéville's socialist reading of The Fifth Head of Cerberus is not the only way to value Wolfe, and says Miéville concedes that some writers he cites, such as Keith Roberts, are not socialist at all. Grounds this in Miéville's comments on Roberts' Pavane, which describe a feudal Catholic world as a fetter on social function, while Roberts was no leftist.
disputes · author-and-biography

These linked stories take place in a present day where Elizabeth I was assassinated and Spain took over Britain. This examines life in a world where a militant feudal Catholicism acts as a fetter on social and productive functions. Though Roberts was no lefty at all, and you could probably power France on the energy from his spinning grave at being included in this list.

2010-12-19 Lee Berman replying to James Wynn #

Argues that the deeper message of 5HoC is that colonized natives eventually imitate, infiltrate and subvert the colonizers' society. Responds to James Wynn's thread on socialism by linking this to immigrant and minority conflicts today and joking that the book suits anti-immigrant skinheads.
proposes · theories: 5HoC's deeper message is natives subverting colonizers

The best explanation I've seen is the suggestion that socialists might generally be against imperialism and colonization.

The funny thing is, the deeper message in 5HoC is that the eventual result of colonization is that the natives will imitate, infiltrate and ultimately subvert the society of the colonizers. An interesting consideration in light of minority and immigrant issues currently broiling in Europe, the USA (more in the past) and what Japan seems to be vigorously trying to avoid.

I could actually see the argument for 5HoC being a must-read for anti-immigrant, neo-nazi skinheads.
