The thread: Timothy Reilly asked why the Green Man tells Severian that Agia can be found 'above ground' in The Book of the New Sun, posting a bare question with no argument. Michael Andre-Driussi answered that the remark anticipates later scenes (the Saltus mine, the widow's attic, Agia's rescue behind enemy lines) and that the Green Man may be bluffing. Peter Stephenson countered that the Green Man is a generic fortune-teller offering platitudes, since an oracle who knows the future still lacks details of individual lives. The thread ends with two readings set side by side, with no sign of agreement.

  • Michael Andre-Driussi: the remark foreshadows later scenes, or the Green Man is bluffing
  • Peter Stephenson: the remark is a fortune-teller's platitude, not real foresight

Themes: Time loops and causality, Unreliable narration, Ghosts, dreams and visions

1999-05-26 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Proposes that the Green Man's remark that Agia can be found "above ground" refers to later scenes: the mine at Saltus, the widow's cabin attic, and Agia's rescue from behind enemy lines. Grounds this in the Green Man's supposed foresight, noting Severian's future is already known, and offers bluffing as an alternative.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Green Man's "above ground" refers to Agia's later scenes

Hello Timothy Reilly!

Since the Green Man can supposedly see into the future, and we have Severian's future in hand, the Green Man's phrase may relate to the mine at Saltus (which is the next place Severian will see her--"above ground" on the surface rather than "under ground" in the mine shaft), or, since this seems rather weak, it may refer to the widow's cabin in the mountains, where Agia comes down the stairs from the attic where she was hiding--definitely "above ground."

Still later, she comes down from the sky on a winged thing and plucks him up from behind enemy lines--come to think of it, that was "above ground," too.

Or maybe the Green Man is just bluffing.

=mantis=

1999-05-31 Peter Stephenson #

Argues that the Green Man's claim that Agia can be found "above ground" is a generic fortune-teller's platitude rather than real foresight, since he comes from the future but lacks details of individual lives. Grounds this in the paradox of an oracle who predicts the future yet must invent specifics, and in Severian's own first guess that the phrase simply meant she was alive.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: The Green Man's prophecies are generic platitudes made up from future knowledge

(late again, holiday trip to Ravenna, Urbino, life's hard. sigh.)

I haven't gone back to check, but reading the Book before my impression was very strong (in fact, I was quite certain) that the Green Man was simply giving out standard platitudes like any fortune teller in a fairground. I saw this as an example of Wolfe's brilliance with paradox --- he really does come from the future and so can `predict' it, but of course he doesn't know all the details about everyone's lives, so he has to make them up. Then Severian's first guess, `he simply meant that she was alive', is the right one; the entirely intentional oracular quality of the utterance has allowed him to reinterpret it some other way.

corncrake
