The thread: Participants debated whether eating the fruit of knowledge separated humans from beasts, with Nil Ultra defending the Fall view against Nutria's claim that humanity made itself bestial. Jim Jordan objected that this reading sounds more Mormon than Wolfe's conservative Catholic outlook and declined to argue theology. The thread then drifted to whether lexical analysis could show New Sun and Long Sun share an author, as Richard Horton proposed. Alice Turner and Daniel Fusch clashed over the Book of Revelation's authorship and literary merit, Turner citing canon disputes and Bloom's dismissal, Fusch defending its poetry. The thread ended in impasse, mostly off Wolfe.
  • Nil Ultra: eating the fruit of knowledge separated humans from beasts and innocence, which makes good and evil possible
  • Jim Jordan: the fruit-of-knowledge reading sounds Mormon, and the real question is what a conservative Catholic like Wolfe would work with
  • Richard Horton: lexical analysis could test whether New Sun and Long Sun share one author
  • Alice Turner: Revelation was never accepted as John's work and is rancid by Bloom's view
  • Daniel Fusch: Revelation is spectacularly poetic despite its misuse by evangelists and cults

Themes: Catholic faith and sacraments, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Books, libraries and writing

2000-04-18 Richard Horton #

Argues that lexical analysis used on Biblical authorship could test whether the New Sun and Long Sun books share a single author despite their profoundly different styles. Responds to a prior poster's doubts about scholarly authorship debates and notes that Revelation carries no attribution to John the Apostle.
proposes · source-and-allusion

Fair enough, though I'd be interested in seeing what lexical analysis of the sort used to address authorship questions of Biblical books would do with New Sun/Long Sun. On the face of it, the styles are profoundly different: no one can have failed to notice this. I wouldn't be surprised, however, if underlying similarities were detected.

I confess I don't really know enough about these techniques to say much more than : "I've read the articles".

We should note that (unlike for instance some of the Epistles now felt by many not to have been written by Paul) there is no attribution within Revelation to John the Apostle.

2000-04-18 Alice Turner #

Argues that the Book of Revelation was never accepted as the work of the apostle John, noting that bishops objected to it when the canon was formed. Responds to Richard Horton's remark on contemporary scholarship by citing textual analysis and Harold Bloom's view that the book is rancid; this is off-topic from Wolfe.
disputes

Rostrum rote:

The scholarship is not that "contemporary" regarding Revelation. It was suspect from the gitgo, with several more sober bishops strongly objecting to it when the 4th century canon was put together. It crawled in because of popular superstition that John the gospeleer wrote it, though more educated folk with a good "ear" even at that early time found this highly unlikely, and said so. There were lots of similarly apocalyptic texts around, this one being an anti-Rome rant with historical references to Babylon, Israel's prior primo peeve. Modern textual analysis makes the two completely incompatible. "John of Patmos" is the name given to the unknown author of what Harold Bloom (and his insignificant follower, me) call a "rancid" book. OTOH, as a heathen, I do think that it has pepped up the religion considerably! For TV evangelists, say, suicidal cults, and plenty of morons everywhere!

-alga

2000-04-19 Daniel Fusch #

Argues that the Book of Revelation is a spectacularly poetic work despite its misuse by evangelists and cults, singling out the four horsemen, the lamps on the crystal sea, and the closing lines about needing no sun. Responds to Alice Turner's remarks by offering a balancing view on the aesthetics of Revelation, citing Harold Bloom's dismissal of the book as a counterpoint.
disputes · close-textual-reading

"John of Patmos" is the name given to the unknown author of what Harold Bloom (and his insignificant follower, me) call a "rancid" book. OTOH, as a heathen, I do think that it has pepped up the religion considerably! For TV evangelists, say, suicidal cults, and plenty of morons everywhere!

Just to balance the discussion of the aesthetics of Revelation....

I think Revelation is a spectacularly poetic work, with some gems of great beauty. I've always found the sixth chapter (the four horsemen) particularly good. And then heaven with the lamps on the crystal sea. And the end--something along the lines of "they will need neither candles nor sun for God will be their light." Very beautiful. Associated with suicidal cults or not, I still think Revelation is one of those books that really deserves to be read.

Just my humble opinion,
Daniel
