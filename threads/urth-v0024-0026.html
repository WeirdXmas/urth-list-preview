Off Topic: Prufrock

1999-03-01 Tony Ellis #

Argues that Eliot's Prufrock poem is larger than its speaker as a sad seducer, and that the Inferno quotation shows Prufrock's passive stance toward life. Responds to Christopher R. Culver's reading of the poem as a bitter, horny old man, claiming literary criticism supports the broader view.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading

I can just about see why you might want to say that Prufrock the character was an "embittered and horny old man", but Prufrock the poem is something much bigger, and I really think you'll find the lit. crit. backs me up on this one.

Absolutely.
The quote from the Inferno - which can be roughly translated as "If I thought you could return to the world with what I am about to tell you, I would hold my tongue" - is also significant for the way it reflects Prufrock's own passive approach to life. He's only speaking out (or thinking aloud) because he has already decided that nothing can be achieved by doing so.
