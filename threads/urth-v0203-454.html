The thread: Readers debated whether the Whorl in The Book of the New Sun is a colonization ship that returns to Urth to repopulate it. Michael Andre-Driussi argued it targets a known habitable planet with compatible DNA and that Tzadkiel, not Typhon, is the ark, citing the Talos play, the sailors' wager, and guards' reports of ships during the deluge. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes supported the reroute-to-repopulate reading while conceding there is no smoking gun. Michael Straight took a different tack, arguing the Short Sun books deliberately use pulp planetary-romance conventions of Earth-like alien fauna. Matthew Malthouse backed the Tzadkiel-ark view, saying Typhon is a short-term tyrant rather than a planner of back-up plans. The thread stayed among these few voices and ended without resolution.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: the Whorl is a colonization ship and Tzadkiel, not Typhon, is repopulating Urth
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Tzadkiel or its masters reroute the Whorl to repopulate Urth, though the case lacks a smoking gun
  • Michael Straight: the Short Sun uses pulp planetary-romance conventions of Earth-like alien fauna
  • matthew.malthouse: Typhon's imperial ambitions make a deliberate Urth ark unlikely, so Tzadkiel is the seeder

Themes: Colonization and the native, Power, rulers and politics, Deep time and ruins

2002-03-26 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that the Whorl in The Book of the New Sun is a colonization ship targeting a known, habitable planet with compatible DNA, and that Tzadkiel, not Typhon, is the ark repopulating Urth. Responds to Rostrum's point about Earth-like life on other planets, contrasting it with Vernor Vinge's A Fire Upon the Deep and citing the Talos play, the sailors' wager in the Hall of Justice, and guards' news of ships dropping off people during the deluge.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: The Whorl is a colonization ship; Tzadkiel repopulates Urth as an ark

Right. We (well, me and someone else around here) were more distracted when hard sf guy Vernor Vinge had human kids crash on a random planet that just happened to have edible grains and protein . . . as a case of random chance, like a voyage to an uncharted planet, this seemed a bit far off the thinking at the time A FIRE UPON THE DEEP was written (the 90s). And it could have been fixed, it seemed, with a single sentence.

Back to Typhon. I don't think there is any sense of the Whorl being an exploration vessel: it is a colonization ship. It is not looking for a home, stopping at every oasis in the desert of space, it is going straight to the place, non-stop. The target star system is, therefore, presumed to be known in advance. Of all the uncolonized-yet-colonizable planets in the galaxy, surely a ruler will select a planet that has compatible DNA (especially if the colonization effort is one ship). This is part of the "habitability" package, after all, and has been since the 1960s: another factor, just as gravity, sunlight, oxy/nitrogen atmosphere, atmospheric pressure, water, length of local day, etc., but one that comes near the end of the stream.

As for Typhon loading an ark to repopulate Urth. No. This function is being carried out, either by design or by accident, by the Ship Tzadkiel. From the play by Talos; from the plan for the sailors in the Hall of Justice (iirc it was: if the sailors win, they can stop the New Sun, if they lose they can populate Ushas); from the way that guards bring news of ships dropping off people at the time of the deluge (echoing the play): it seems like Tzadkiel drops off Adam&Eve packets onto Urth in lean times, just as Tzadkiel seems to snatch people up in other times (all that trade in slaves).

=mantis=

2002-03-26 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the Whorl returns to Urth because Tzadkiel or its masters reroute it to repopulate the world, a more plausible motive than Typhon's concern for posterity. Responds to Mantis, citing the Talos play, the sailors' Hall of Justice plan, and guards' reports of ships during the deluge, while conceding the case lacks a smoking gun.
supports · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: The Whorl returns to repopulate Urth; Tzadkiel reroutes the Whorl deliberately or accidentally

... and you know what? This is the best argument I've seen for the WHORL returning to Urth: Tzadkiel (or Tzad's masters) rerouting it to repopulate. It still falls a smidgeon short of a smoking gun, but it provides a much more plausible reason, imho, than Typhon giving enough of a damn about posterity to want to repopulate Urth after the coming of the New Sun (which, I believe, he intended to prevent anyway ... though he's clever enough to play both ends against the middle this way).

--Blattid

2002-03-26 Michael Straight #

Argues that the Short Sun books deliberately adopt the old pulp planetary-romance convention of Earth-like alien fauna, with extra limbs marking alien-ness and similarity allowing colonists and blood-sucking monsters. Supports this by citing Star Trek, Larry Niven's seeded-planet justification, and the colonists needing to bring their own crops.
proposes · genre-and-craft · close-textual-reading · theories: Short Sun extra limbs denote alien-ness

Regarding the similarity of Urth and Blue/Green lifeforms and their ability to eat each other, this is an old standard convention in science fiction. Let's remember that the Short Sun series are, among many other things, Wolfe's take on the pulp planetary exploration novels complete with Vampires from Outer Space! In these novels, the fauna from other planets are just like ours, with extra limbs or two heads or blue fur. See also Star Trek.

Many otherwise "hard" science-fiction novels gloss over having Earth-like plants and animals on other planets. Some, like Larry Niven come up with some kind of justification for it (planets all seeded by earlier space empire), others just ignore the question. In general, if the characters don't bring up the unlikelihood of other planets having such similar plants and animals, you're just supposed to ignore it and get on with the story.

Given these conventions, I think Wolfe is using the extra limbs to denote alien-ness, while the similarity of life allows for low-tech colonists and monsters that suck their blood. (Note also that the colonists had to bring their own crops. They aren't able to subsist only on the native Bluvian crops.)

-Rostrum

2002-03-27 Matthew Malthouse #

Supports Michael Andre-Driussi's suggestion that the ship Tzadkiel, not Typhon, is seeding Urth with people, arguing Typhon's imperial ambitions make a deliberate Urth ark unlikely. Grounds this in Typhon's conversations with Severian on awakening and later, and calls Typhon a short-term tyrant rather than a planner of back-up plans.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Tzadkiel drops off Adam and Eve packets on Urth; Typhon has no plan to repopulate Urth

And in support: such a move would be contrary to Typhon's ambition to (re-)conquor his stellar empire. He doesn't care about Urth, the backwater, any more than as a springboard from which to re-enter the bigger game out amongst the stars.

But that does leave me pondering the old question "why did he do it?" Typhon's conversations with Severian first when awoken on his couch and later (in Severian's experience) as Severian is traveling the history of Urth definitely suggest a man right up there at the cutting edge of the tyranting business. Long tern strategems or back-up plans don't really seem to be Typhon's style.

Matthew
