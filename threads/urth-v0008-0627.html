Hethor

The thread: Alice Turner opened by asking where the name Hethor came from, since most Book of the New Sun names trace to sources and this one resisted searches, and she rejected the Egyptian Hathor. m.driussi offered a Chertsey saint, Hethor, from the Oxford Dictionary of Saints. Turner then argued Hethor is a false name and that Kimleesoong may be his real name, while C R Culver argued Jonas's speech is Korean and questioned whether the ship passed the Quasar. Culver later asked whether Hethor's babbling alluded to glossolalia, and driussi pointed to the Lexicon Urthus and noted Agia calls Hethor a non-true name, so the thread ended in partial agreement with the Korean and false-name questions still open.
  • Alice Turner: Hethor is a false name, not a saint's name; Kimleesoong may be the real name
  • m.driussi: offers the Chertsey saint source; says Agia states Hethor is not his true name and the Lexicon Urthus covers him
  • C R Culver: Jonas's speech suggests Korean; Hethor's babbling may allude to Christian glossolalia

Themes: Names and their meanings, Hidden identities and parentage, Catholic faith and sacraments

1998-03-14 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Shares an entry from the Oxford Dictionary of Saints about Beocca and Hethor, monks of Chertsey said to have been killed by Danes in 870, suggesting a historical source for the name Hethor. Quotes the entry's account of the chronicles, William of Malmesbury, and the uncertain number of monks, without adding an argument of its own.
informs · source-and-allusion · theories: Hethor named after Saint Hethor of Chertsey

Reply: Item #3560425 from [email]@INET03#

"BEOCCA AND HETHOR, monks and martyrs of Chertsey (Surrey), killed by the Danes in 870. The memory of these monks, supposed to be as many as ninety, was kept alive in fragmentary chronicles and by William of Malmesbury. Their deaths, like those of monks of Peterborough, Bardney, Ely, and Crowland, were believed to be the work of the same Danish army as that which killed Edmund of East Anglia. It seems unlikely that the number of the monks concerned is accurate. Feast: 10 April.
"G.P., p. 143; R.P.S.; C.S.P." (THE OXFORD DICTIONARY OF SAINTS, third edition).

;)

1998-03-15 C R Culver #

Argues that Jonas's feverish nasal, monosyllabic speech in the Antechamber suggests Korean, though Chinese is possible, and reconsiders whether Hethor's ship passed the Quasar rather than being the Quasar. Responds to Alga's claims by checking the text, admitting he could not find the passage, and musing whether Hethor's mirror skills came from working on the Ship of Tzadkiel.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Jonas speaks Korean; Hethor's ship passed the Quasar

He certainly speaks Korean. See the description of his feverish ramblings in the Antechamber. Severian notes that Jonas was talking in a nasal, monosyllabic language. Korean is such a language, almost every word ends in a nasal consonant or a vowel, and there are no two-syllable words as such. However, the language could also be Chinese. It's possible.

Well, I pulled out my battered citation-copy of _Shadow_, and it wasn't where I thought it would be! However, it comes to me now that, although Hethor mentioned it, the Quasar might not have been his ship. I believe he mentioned his ship passed the Quasar in the void, I'll sort it out.

1998-03-16 C R Culver #

Asks whether Hethor's babbling in Severian's dream is Wolfe's allusion to Christian glossolalia, noting that the babbling is not found elsewhere in The Book of the New Sun. Argues that documented glossolalia usually has a 1:1 vowel-consonant ratio and polysyllabic forms, which Hethor's speech lacks, and repeats the poster's view that the language is Korean.
questions · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Hethor's babbling is Christian glossolalia; Hethor's babbling is Korean

So Severian is hallucinating someone babbling? Hmm, is this Wolfe's way to throw some of the Christian phenomenon of glossolalia in there? I don't believe it shows up anywhere else in the BotNS.

The only problem with saying it is this is that documented glossolalia (almost always) has a vowel-consonant ratio of 1:1 and is polysyllabic, nasal consonants don't show up much either.

I still think it was Korean.

1998-03-16 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Points out that a reference book lacking an index and cross-references is poorly organized, then notes that much of the relevant material on Hethor appears in the Lexicon Urthus under his entry. Adds that Agia states plainly Hethor is not his true name and that Wolfean naming rules corroborate this, and observes that Hethor's clearest speech occurs inside a dream of Severian's.
supports · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Hethor is not his true name

Reply: Item #5234791 from [email]@INET03#

alga,

Re: OXFORD BOOK OF SAINTS, yes, I remembered your practice of judging a reference book by its index (or lack thereof) and muttered an oath against OBOS for not having an index and not being adequately cross-referenced--"Savas (Sabas)" has no pointer at "Sabas," for example.

But otherwise, a lot of this stuff is in the Lexicon, under "Hethor." (The main clue that "Hethor" is not his true name is given point blank by Agia; but yes, you are right that the rules of Wolfean naming provide corroboration--I hadn't thought of that before.)

P.S. Hethor's most lucid babbling happens within a dream of Severian's rather than in waking action.
