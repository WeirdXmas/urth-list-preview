The thread: Participants began with a wish for a complete R.A. Lafferty short fiction edition and debated estate sales and Lafferty's alcoholism, which Dave Lebling and others said was public knowledge. The thread turned to whether Wolfe would be remembered without The Book of the New Sun, and Marc Aramini, Dave Lebling and Daniel Petersen argued over Wolfe's discipline versus Lafferty's craft and voice. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Antonin Scriabin then pressed Aramini and Sergei Soloviev on whether Wolfe's female characters fall into a Goddess-Virgin-Whore template. The thread drifted into recommendations (Tim Powers, Apocalypses) and ended without consensus, with Daniel Petersen naming Wolfe's weakest passages.
  • Marc Aramini: Wolfe is more disciplined and consistent than Lafferty, a genius who cannot sustain long narratives
  • Daniel Petersen: Lafferty's craft and voice are better at their best than Wolfe's, though Wolfe is more disciplined
  • Dave Lebling: Wolfe would be remembered without The New Sun, citing Cerberus, Peace and the Doctor Island stories
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Wolfe's female characters fit a Goddess-Virgin-Whore cliche without independent lives
  • Antonin Scriabin: Female types are a craft issue, not a failure to understand women, and Cassie resists that template
  • Sergei Soloviev: Wolfe's women are adequate and avoid caricature, though psychologically external

Themes: Women, sex and gender, Books, libraries and writing, Catholic faith and sacraments

2012-04-16 Marc Aramini replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that R.A. Lafferty's vast unmarketable output makes him likely to be forgotten, while Gene Wolfe's New Sun series has protected Wolfe from similar obscurity. Replies to Gwern Branwen's remark about Lafferty's unavailable works by noting Wolfe's lost early novels and unpublished manuscripts, and that fame is random.
supports

Lafferty is in many ways the opposite of strange one hit mega-successes like "Confederacy of Dunces" - with Lafferty, so much material there but for all intents and purposes unmarketable.

Gene at least had one series that had true and lasting mainstream appeal, but I fear without that he might have wound up in much the same position of obscurity as Lafferty, and by no means is New Sun the sum total of what Gene had to say to the world. He had one or two early novels that never saw the light of day, but as far as I know those manuscripts no longer exist. Gene won't leave behind a bunch of unpublished stuff, unless he starts scribbling a whole lot in the next few years and it is not accepted.

Fame is such a random thing. Flannery O'Connor will never be forgotten - Lafferty might.

2012-04-18 Daniel Petersen replying to Sergei SOLOVIEV #

Argues that Wolfe's lows are rare and mostly a matter of drive and tone, citing the loss of narrative drive in the last Long Sun book and an alienating coldness in his voice. Responds to Sergei Soloviev's question about Wolfe's weaknesses, offering the dialogue of Castleview as a true low while noting Silk and Horn as sources of warmth.
answers · genre-and-craft

Don't get me wrong - I don't encounter 'lows' in Wolfe terribly often. Maybe 'mids' a bit more.

I think one 'low' or 'mid' would be when his narrative loses its drive - last book of Long Sun is surely a classic example (a series I totally love regardless of that - and the very ending picks up again). I haven't analysed how or why this happens.

Another low or mid would be the 'coldness' that many have remarked in Wolfe's viewpoint or voice or something - it's alienating somehow to the reader. E.g.:
http://examinedlife.typepad.com/johnbelle/2003/10/gene_wolfe.html.

To some degree this is just something to get used to - or was for me, and then I actually began to feel a lot of warmth in certain ways at certain times - most especially from the likes of Silk and Horn. Anyway, it's not really a low or a flaw - it's more a 'trade off' for other marvels and joys Wolfe gives. Not every writer can achieve every literary effect.

I just finished *Castleview* the other day and some of the dialogue in there is a true low in Wolfe to me. (Many highs are in there as well.) In fact, dialogue and the way people speak tend to be proving grounds in Wolfe's craft where he either shines in genius and skill or practically falls on his face.

-DOJP
