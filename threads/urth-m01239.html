The thread: The thread asked where cyberpunk's literary ancestry lies and whether The Long Sun belongs to the genre. Dan suggested Wells, Verne and Shelley as forerunners and linked Sterling's view of transcendence in SF to Dr. Crane revealing Loris's bio body, calling it a cyberpunk moment. James Wynn argued that The Long Sun is a cyberpunk novel with a narrator who does not understand his virtual world, and disputed Dan's claim that Wolfe defies genre. Hmpf MacSlow mostly digressed about the lack of sf courses at their German university and recommended Angel's Egg. The thread ends with two opposed readings and a side tangent, with no resolution recorded.
  • Dan: Cyberpunk traces back to Wells, Verne and Shelley, and The Long Sun has a cyberpunk moment in Crane's reveal
  • James Wynn: The Long Sun is cyberpunk, narrated by someone who misunderstands his virtual world; Wolfe does not defy the genre
  • Hmpf MacSlow: Lacks sf courses at their German university; recommends Angel's Egg as more Wolfean

Themes: Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Robots, androids and artificial life, Unreliable narration

2005-06-21 Dan #

Suggests Wells, Verne and Shelley as a starting point for tracing cyberpunk's literary ancestry, and recalls a class that traced such parentage. Quotes Bruce Sterling on transcendence in SF as a genre feature and links it to Dr. Crane revealing Loris's bio body in The Long Sun series, calling it a cyberpunk moment.
supports · genre-and-craft

Off hand I'd say Wells, Verne and Shelley would be a good start. I think that's what that class was attempting to do was reveal a similar parentage for cyberpunk by tracing through a collection of works (numerous short stories and a few novels).

Absolutely!

Oh, I was being facetious on the book title. That was a good quote worth repeating from
http://www.technologyreview.com/articles/05/02/issue/editor.asp?p=1:

{When the science fiction writer and journalist Bruce Sterling was asked why so many science fiction novels ended with their heroes transcending their circumstances, abilities, or bodies, he was dismissive. "It's just a riff," Sterling answered. "The element of transcendence is just a feature of the SF genre, like feedback in rock music. People who take that stuff seriously end up turning into trolls....H. P. Lovecraft was a big fan of that cosmic-type stuff. That may be okay for him, but from the outside what you see is this pasty-faced guy eating canned hash in the dim corner of a restaurant, hands trembly, and a gray film over his eyes." }

(Though as an aside, I'm not sure Lovecraft really took that cosmic stuff seriously, as the correspondence of his I've read seemed to treat it rather playfully.)

As for a literary device, it's a way to show the main character growing and changing.
One of the (few) stories I recall reading for that class was I Have No Mouth And I Must Scream by Harlan Ellison. Which was a good introduction to the idea of transcendence not necessarily being a good thing.

For some reason that article reminded me of The Long Sun Series when Dr. Crane reveals the true state of Loris' bio body. Truly one of the greatest cyberpunk moments ever.

How science and technology twists society and individuals and how individuals seize them to twist back.

IIRC, the cyberpunk class was actually a spinoff of a groundbreaking class from the previous semester that was taught by two instructors from two different departments. It dealt with science in science fiction, but also focused on cyberpunk and computer technology which inspired the cyberpunk class.

Alas, such a vast amount of water has passed beneath that bridge that it has long since washed away. I don't really recall many details from the classes other than there was a lot of reading, some heated classroom discussions and that we spent a lot of time trying to deconstruct the mass media pop culture hype that was already suffocating the genre. Both were great classes.

Dan

2005-06-21 James Wynn replying to Dan #

Argues that The Long Sun is a cyberpunk novel narrated by a protagonist who does not understand the virtual world he lives in, comparing it to The Matrix. Responds to Dan's claim that Wolfe defies genre, citing Quetzal and Crane as guides and an ending where the narrator rules the virtual world and learns he is a clone.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: The Long Sun is a cyberpunk novel; Long Sun protagonist is a genetically improved clone; Long Sun narrator dominates the virtual world

Hmmmm....

It occurs to me that The Long Sun is a cyberpunk novel told from the perspective of one totally uncomprehensive of the virtual world he lives in (imagine "The Matrix" where Neo only understands learns what the Matrix is 3/4ths of the way into the movie). Quetzal and Crane are his guides into that world. In the end, he dominates that world by becoming the ruling virtual personality in it, and discovers that he himself is a genetically improved clone.

~ Crush
