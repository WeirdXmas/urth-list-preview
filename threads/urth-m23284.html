The thread: Readers debated Neil Gaiman's praise of The Sorcerer's House and whether the novel's chapters follow the Greater Trumps of a tarot deck. Fred Kiesche and Gwern Branwen clashed over whether the book is deep or a plain evil-twin story, with Kiesche charitably explaining Gaiman's praise as aimed at newcomers. Darth Ed and David Stockhoff argued letters match individual trumps in shuffled order, while Gwern proposed the trumps might unlock an unscrambled letter order and a fit with the fish episode. Andreas Muegge questioned whether the obvious twin-identity clue is a deliberate lure. The thread drifted into Delany's Nova and Gaiman's other work, and it ended without consensus on any tarot ordering.
  • Fred Kiesche: Gaiman's praise is for newcomers, not evidence of deep reading
  • Gwern Branwen: the novel is a plain evil-twin murder story, but tarot structure may matter
  • Darth Ed: letters match individual trumps read in shuffled order, like a tarot reading
  • David Stockhoff: crossing letters shuffle sequence and identities, rejecting a fixed tarot order
  • Craig Brewer: Gaiman is a storyteller and channeler of genre, not a strong Wolfe critic
  • Andreas Muegge: the obvious twin clue may be a deliberate lure toward a hidden layer

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Unreliable narration, Myth and folklore sources

2012-05-03 Daniel Petersen replying to Fred Kiesche #

Expresses gratitude that Neil Gaiman praised Gene Wolfe and R. A. Lafferty, the poster's two favourite writers, in an interview. Recounts writing letters to Lafferty as a teenager and a final letter sent while Lafferty was in a care home with Alzheimer's.
chats

Aside from some of his own great stuff, I'll never stop liking and being grateful to Neil for bigging up my two faves: Wolfe and Lafferty. From the same interview:

*If you could meet any writer, dead or alive, who would it be? What would you want to know? Have you ever written to an author?*

As a teenager I wrote to R. A. Lafferty. And he responded, too, with letters that were like R. A. Lafferty short stories, filled with elliptical answers to straight questions and simple answers to complicated ones.

He was a sui generis writer, the oddest and most frustratingly delightful of American tall-tale tellers. Not a lot of people have read him, and even fewer like what he wrote, but those of us who like him like him all the way. We never met.

The last time I wrote to Lafferty, he had Alzheimer?s and was in a home in Oklahoma, shortly before his death, and I do not believe he read or understood the letter, but it made me feel like I was doing something right by writing it and sending it.

-DOJP

2012-05-04 Craig Brewer replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Argues that Neil Gaiman is strongest when working obliquely, through collaboration or varied formats, and that he is better seen as an old-fashioned storyteller and a channeler of genre material to wider audiences. Responds to Antonin Scriabin's remark that Gaiman's Sandman and Fragile Things outshine American Gods and Neverwhere, offering a general appraisal rather than any Wolfe-specific evidence.
disputes

I tend to agree. He always seems best when he's doing something oblique, like collaborating or writing for varied formats. I don't think that's a bad thing because parts of Sandman stand up with the best sf writing. But he's always described himself more as a storyteller in something of an old fashioned sense rather tha "a writer," and I think that's apt.

I also think of him as a channeler of other things to broader audiences. That's not only because he always promos people I like, but even stuff like Sandman brought some "literary" stuff to comics, and it brought non-comics readers to graphic novels, etc. He makes people who don't like genre stuff...like genre stuff, and that's a good thing. He also just seems to me like a guy who "gets it" in all the right ways, both in terms of knowing what's good (anything he edits is worth the price) and in knowing what he does best for the most part.

2012-05-04 Darth Ed replying to Gwern Branwen #

Urges list members to revisit a Tarot reading of The Sorcerer's House, noting that the book has 44 letters, twice the 22 Major Arcana cards, and that letters 22 and 23 share the title "Silver Bullets". Points to Gaiman's Goodreads review and earlier list threads by Dave Stockhoff and Dave Tallman, arguing that the proposed double-deck pairing of letters with trumps is worth further analysis.
proposes · puzzle-solving · myth-and-structure · theories: Sorcerer's House letters paired with Major Arcana trumps

I think so. I'd certainly be interested in the definitive Tarot reading of TSH. I'm not sure anyone has quite cracked it yet, other than perhaps Gaiman.

Gaiman has mentioned the similarities to the Tarot before in his review of TSH on GoodReads (http://www.goodreads.com/review/show/97189634), and it's been discussed on this mailing list by Dave Stockhoff, Dave Tallman, and others. Dave Tallman got the furthest, I think. Relevant links:

http://lists.urth.net/pipermail/urth-urth.net/2010-April/014621.html http://lists.urth.net/pipermail/urth-urth.net/2010-April/014624.html http://lists.urth.net/pipermail/urth-urth.net/2010-April/014631.html

But, alas, the topic is dropped just as the list starts to make some progress.

Background for those who missed the Tarot discussion the first time:

Wikipedia says: "The Major Arcana (greater secrets), or trump cards, consists of 22 cards without suits: The Fool, The Magician, The High Priestess, The Empress, The Emperor, The Hierophant, The Lovers, The Chariot, Strength, The Hermit, Wheel of Fortune, Justice, The Hanged Man, Death, Temperance, The Devil, The Tower, The Star, The Moon, The Sun, Judgement, and The World."

There are 44 letters/chapters in TSH, exactly twice the number of cards in the Major Arcana. Two of the letters (letters 22 and 23) have the same title ("Silver Bullets"). Presumably, we have a double deck of the Major Arcana with each letter can be associated with one of the cards of the Major Arcana in some fashion. Also, the repetition of "Silver Bullets" at exactly the midpoint of the book implies a thematic mirroring relationship to the letters on either side of the midpoint.

Hope this spurs some additional analysis,
Ed

2012-05-07 Darth Ed replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that the chapters of The Sorcerer's House do not need reordering to match a canonical Major Arcana sequence; instead each letter's principal characters and action match individual trump cards. Supports David Stockoff's matches of several letters to the Major Arcana, reasoning that the cards are read in shuffled order as in a Tarot reading and that readers, like a Tarot reader, interpret past and present to foretell the future.
supports · myth-and-structure · theories: Letters match Major Arcana by characters and action, not titles; Chapter order follows shuffled Tarot reading rather than canonical sequence

I concur. That's my feeling as well.

I don't think the letters/chapters are out of order per se with regards to some canonical ordering of the Major Arcana and that we need to reorder the chapters/letters to fit that canonical ordering. In a Tarot reading, the cards are shuffled and the ordering of the cards is significant to the person who is being read by the reader. And I don't think the titles are necessarily related to the Major Arcana, but rather the principle characters and/or action that takes place in each letter/chapter can be matched to each of the Major Arcana. In that respect, I'm keen on David Stockoff's interpretation which identified several such matches for a handful of the letters/chapters. He also incorporated the mirroring nicely into his interpretation. His identifications seemed quite reasonable to me, and I think further study is warranted and may come up with additional identifications between the letters/chapters and the Major Arcana. I'll certainly be looking for such connection on my next reading of TSH, but I suspect that's probably years from now, unfortunately.
