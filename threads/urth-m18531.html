Home Fires and calendar reform **SPOILERS**

Urth list · 9 messages · 2011-01-26 to 2011-02-02

2011urththalassocratJeff WilsonDave TallmanDarth Ed
The thread: Dave Tallman asked what calendar reform Wolfe's story Home Fires describes, since it uses days, weeks, hundred-day periods and years but no months, and he compared it with Asimov's uniform calendar. Jeff Wilson and Mr Thalassocrat offered logistical and thematic explanations, and Tallman built a reconstruction of a 400-day year with day 180 as winter. Darth Ed argued instead for a metric 10-day-week calendar anchored to a July Fourth-based dating, then conceded a travel-date error that undercut Tallman's timeline. The thread drifted into whether Christmas survives as a secular holiday in the future, with Tallman finally citing Chelle's company-party line as evidence, and it ended without a settled calendar.
  • Dave Tallman: calendar is a 400-day year unsynchronized with seasons, numbered from a restart
  • Darth Ed: dates point to a US-derived calendar, more likely a metric 10-day week
  • Jeff Wilson: hundred-day units are logistical bundles for relativistic travel and isolation
  • Mr Thalassocrat: the reform reflects Wolfe's theme of societies cutting off from nature
  • Darth Ed: conceded Skip and Chelle arrived on day 179, not 178 as assumed

Themes: Time loops and causality, The dying sun and the cosmos, Hidden identities and parentage

2011-01-26 Dave Tallman #

Asks what kind of calendar reform the story "Home Fires" describes, noting it uses days, weeks, hundred-days and years but no months. Compares it with Asimov's uniform calendar of 91-day periods, arguing four hundred-days would drift from the solar year, and speculates about business, political or secular motives.
questions · chronology-and-plot-logic · genre-and-craft

In "Home Fires" there is never a mention of months or month names. There are days, weeks, hundred-days, and years. What sort of calendar reform is this? Asimov proposed a uniform calendar abolishing the old months, with 91 days per period. Four of these gives 364 days, a reasonable fit with the solar year. But four hundred-days is 400, which seems unreasonable. Does this calendar just let the years fall where they may? It seems impractical for things like birthdays, holidays, and anniversaries (none of these things are mentioned within the story).

The solar year is slowly getting longer, but it takes centuries to add minutes. This story doesn't seem to be set that far in the future. One possibility is that uniformity for business purposes has trumped all other practical considerations. Possibly it was mandated by the power-mad politicians. Or it may be another separation of religion and state, like the contracts replacing marriage. Religious holidays would be observed based on seasons and solstices, not a particular day of the year.

Proposes that the hundred-day units in Home Fires are logistical bundles suited to relativistic time dilation and long isolation from Earth, comparing the story to The Forever War. Responds to Dave Tallman's calendar question without having read the story, citing a day's oxygen, meals and water as an example of such logistics.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Hundred-day units suit time dilation logistics

I haven't read it yet, but isn't it a story with lots of time spent isolated from earth, on top of the time dilation, similar to THE FOREVER WAR? Earth's calendar would become irrelevant while away, but provisioning would depend on the subjective time endured; hundred-man-day units is good for estimation and logistical bundling, where a day's consumables are 1500 minutes worth of oxygen, three hot meals, and 5 kilos of water.

Builds on the poster's earlier point about societies cutting themselves off from nature, and infers that ages should be inflated by about 13/12 to account for the year length.

2011-02-02 Darth Ed replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the opening of Home Fires dates Christmas to day 175, so day 1 falls on the Fourth of July, implying a US-derived calendar in the North American Union. Responds to Dave Tallman's claim of 400-day years by questioning that leap and proposing a metric 10-day-week calendar as more likely, grounding the dating in the waitress's phrase "this coming Saturday" and the snowy opening scene.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Day 175 is Christmas, so day 1 is July Fourth; Home Fires calendar derives from the Gregorian and 1776 system; Metric 10-day calendar rather than 400-day years

Hi, Dave. While I agree that the 100-day billing period for rent is suggestive, I think it's a bit of a stretch to make the leap to 400-day years. It just doesn't seem very pragmatic. And, if humanity were going to deviate from a solar-based calendar system, why choose 400? Why not 1000? 10 periods of 100 days each. Probably have 10 days in a "week" with 10 of those per 100-day period. I.e., the metric calendar system. That seems just as likely.

Anyway, I have another theory regarding the _Home Fires_ calendar, one that isn't wholly incompatible with yours, I think. As I was re-reading chapter 1, the opening scene got me thinking. It's snowing outside, and, when Skip wakes up, there's a gift of sorts waiting for him. Could this be Christmas???

Later that morning, the waitress says that Chelle will be returning "this coming Saturday" on day 180. Common usage of the phrase "this coming Saturday" leads me to believe that the book opens on day 175 +/- 1 day. (Most people would not use "this coming" to specify a day unless it was 4-6 days way.) So, if Christmas is day 175, then that would mean day 1 is the Fourth of July, which would make sense for a North American Union dominated by the former USA. The "NAU" name is probably just window dressing for the United States essentially occupying the other North American regions anyway. (David Foster Wallace does something similar in _Infinite Jest_.) Perhaps 1776 is also year 1 in this Gregorian-derived calendar system. All of this could be seen as evidence of secularization and extreme nationalism in this future society.

Where my theory possibly breaks down is that the rest of the world surely wouldn't adopt such a calendar system and it doesn't seem likely there wouldn't be a commonly accepted calendar system used by the whole world.

Later,
Darth Ed

2011-02-02 Dave Tallman replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the calendar in Home Fires runs on 400-day years, not the 365-day or metric year, and that the year numbering was restarted with the change. Answers the objection to a 400-day year by citing Johnson's 200-day job, quarterly business habits, and Skip's travel plans, which place his reunion with Chelle on day 180.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · puzzle-solving · theories: Home Fires uses a 400-day calendar year; Year numbering restarted with the calendar change; Skip's reunion with Chelle falls on day 180

There is more evidence that fits my theory. Johnson says he held a job for "200 days or so, about half a year." That looks like either the old 365 or my 400, not 1000. They still use week-day names, so likely the seven day week is still around. Business likes to work in quarters of a year, so I stand by 400.

Anyway, I have another theory regarding the _Home Fires_ calendar, one that

The only "gift" is the announcement that Chelle is coming home. If it's Christmas, it's also a work-day and nobody says "Merry Christmas" or anything. Based on the travel plans Skip makes, the date must be 178, so the Fourth of July doesn't work (a very clever idea, though). It's extremely likely that the year numbering was restarted with this calendar change.

Skip tells Sally he wants a suite reservation near the port for "the day after tomorrow" which must be for what he expects will be a reunion night with Chelle. He gets it for one night only, and that must be for day 180. His 34-hour bullet train trip, leaving no later than 20:00, is compatible with this. It would get him to the port no later than 06:00 on 180. So even if "this coming Saturday" is a little odd for the waitress to say, it has to be only two days away.
