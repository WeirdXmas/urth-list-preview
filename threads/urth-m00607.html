The thread: Dan Rabin opened by asking how the Osterlings' diet and raiding motives fit The Book of the New Sun, linking them to earlier blood-drinking talk. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that Osterling cannibalism, defined in the TK name list as eating people to become more human, is part of a wider Wolfe pattern of devouring someone to become them, read as perversions of the Eucharist. 2005-12-08 David Duffy replying to Dan Rabin #

Proposes that the Osterlings' drinking of blood serves a magical or spiritual purpose, like historical anthropophagy aimed at gaining the power of enemies, and links this to the Aelf's need for blood. Grounds the view in the idea that the Aelf are magical and that Faery is tied to death in Castleview and in the story under discussion.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Osterlings eat blood to gain magical power; Aelf need blood to become alive

Probably for the same reason as historical anthropophagy, to get spiritual/magical power. Traditionally, you want to eat the bravest of your enemies, IIRC. Coming back to the Aelf, they are magical and perform magic which humans don't, so even though they are lower, consuming their blood presumably has practical advantages. Ordinary wizards need the aid of "lower" elementals to do work, as Able does in the first half of the story. In _Castleview_ and here, Faery is conflated with death, so the Aelf want blood to become alive.

David Duffy.

2005-12-09 Don Doggett replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Agrees with Dan'l Danehy-Oakes that the Osterlings echo the inhumi and the Ascians of the New Sun books, and calls the devouring-to-become pattern a recurring obsession in Wolfe. Extends the pattern to The Wizard Knight, citing the Hierodules and Neighbors in the Overcyns, Severian in Able, Ymar in Arthur, and Erebus and Abaia in the Giants.
supports · myth-and-structure · theories: Osterlings are analogs of the inhumi and Ascians; Devouring to become another is a recurring Wolfe pattern

I agree. The Osterlings are analogs of the inhumi and,
farther back, of the Ascians in the NS books who seek
to devour the commonwealth. This is what fascinates me
about WK. I can see the Hierodules and the Neighbors
in the Overcyns. Severian in Able. Ymar in Arthur.
Erebus and Abaia in the Giants. I don't know if it's a
skeleton key, but GW certainly has his obsessions.

Don

2005-12-10 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to don doggett #

Argues that Wolfe repeatedly depicts characters rising, failing to rise, or falling into lesser states, citing Severian, Able, Typhon, the Gods of the Whorl, the inhumi, Kypris and the Osterlings. Builds on Don Doggett's comparison of the Osterlings to the inhumi and Ascians by naming the alzabo and its elixir as part of the pattern, tying it to who one serves and what one eats.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: Wolfe's characters follow a rising-and-falling pattern tied to service and diet

Not to mention the alzabo and those who use the elixer
made from its glands. This is an _extremely_ common
theme in Wolfe: rising to become something higher
(Severian, Able); seeking to rise but becoming a
perversion of the higher thing (Typhon, the Gods of
the Whorl); doing so but then being raised anyway
(some of the inhumi, Kypris); falling from your station
to something lower (the beastmen in NS, apparently
the Osterlings); etc. A lot of it has to do with (a) who you serve (b) why you serve them and (c) what you
eat.

And there's bits of it in other books, notably 5HC...

--Dan'l

2005-12-10 Chris P replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Typhon and Pas fall unevenly into the categories of perversion and being raised, and distinguishes passive becoming through osmosis from active imitation of a higher being. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's list of rising figures by applying the distinction to Severian, Silk, Able, Silkhorn and the inhumi.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon's rise is a perversion and also a raising; Pas is more benevolent than Typhon; Severian becomes by osmosis rather than imitation; Inhumi cannot become human by imitation alone

I am unsure whether Typhon falls cleanly into the category of a perversion or having been "raised anyway", but I think there is a distinct element of both in there. Pas, to all appearances, is far more benevolent than Typhon ever was; he tried to play a role and it ended up (at least partially) defining him.

And there is a distinction to be made between merely "becoming" by some sort of osmosis, and active imitation (a very Christian theme, the way Wolfe generally uses it). Severian displays little of the latter (except on certain other levels which haven't been mentioned) and a striking amount of the former. Silk and Able, on the other hand, are mostly the latter, and Silkhorn would perhaps be a mix of both.

The inhumi are particularly interesting in this framework because it seems they *cannot* become more human by imitation alone. And this may be entirely beside the point, but when I think of the Osterlings becoming "more human" I cannot help but think of what happened when the inhumi became "more human" - the influence thus exerted is not necessarily going to be a civilizing or morally uplifting one.
