5HC: woodhenge without abos

Urth list · 3 messages · 2002-05-07 to 2002-05-20

The thread: Michael Andre-Driussi opened by arguing that the ring of trees in The Fifth Head of Cerberus may be natural woodhenges rather than an aboriginal observatory, linking it to Le Guin and Zelazny. Adam Stephanides disputed that the Annese observatory proves the Annese existed, suggesting Marsch may have extrapolated 402 stumps from a few rotten trees, and concluded their existence is unknowable. Andre-Driussi returned with a timeline built on NAFAL transit and radio delays, placing the felling of the trees by colonists around year 127 and arguing the first settlers arrived about 85 years before the French discovery. The thread stayed a two-sided exchange with no clear resolution, as Andre-Driussi's dating argument answered the nitpick without settling whether the Annese existed.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: the tree ring may be natural woodhenges, and the trees were felled by later colonists around year 127
  • Adam Stephanides: the Annese observatory is unproven, since the stumps may be extrapolated from a few trees and the Annese existence is unknowable

Themes: Stories within stories, Colonization and the native, Deep time and ruins

2002-05-07 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that the ring of trees in The Fifth Head of Cerberus may be natural woodhenges rather than an aboriginal observatory, and suggests a cryptic reference to Le Guin's Eye of the Heron. Responds to a claim that there were no aboriginals by citing the trees' age relative to the French colonists and offering links to Le Guin and Zelazny's Dream Master.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: The tree circles in 5HC grew naturally as woodhenges; Eye of the Heron is a cryptic reference to 5HC

If there are no abos, really, and never were, then how to explain the astronomical observatory--the trees predate the French colonists by around 120 years.

Maybe they grew that way naturally: link to Le Guin's novel EYE OF THE HERON, which has a botanical detail about alien trees that detonate to spread their seeds, iirc . . . in any event, they naturally form circles of trees, or woodhenges, which makes the forest look rather like chainmail from above. (This had no bearing on EYE OF THE HERON that I could see at the time, but it could very well be a cryptic ref to 5HC, just as Le Guin's "Nine Lives" has the open ref to Zelazny's DREAM MASTER, and Wilhelm's WHERE LATE THE SWEET BIRDS SANG has the veiled ref to 5HC . . . )

2002-05-19 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that the Annese observatory in The Fifth Head of Cerberus does not prove the Annese existed, since Marsch may have extrapolated 402 stumps from a few rotten trees. Responds to Michael Andre-Driussi's claim that the tree circle predates the French by 120 years, citing the passage and Mantis's exploding-seed explanation, and concludes the Annese's existence is unknowable.
disputes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: The Annese observatory may be natural growth; Marsch extrapolated 402 stumps from a dozen trees; The Annese existence is unknowable by design

To pick a nit, 127 (Annese) years is the age of the trees when they were cut down, not when the French first arrived.

When I first read your post, I assumed that the observatory was proof positive that the Annese existed. But upon rereading the passage in 5HC, the "observatory" became frustratingly indefinite, like all the other traces of the Annese. Did Marsch actually see 402 stumps, evenly spaced in a circle? If so, that would be proof that the Annese existed. Mantis's suggestion of trees "exploding" their seeds might account for trees growing naturally in a circle, but not for their being evenly spaced, or for their number equalling the number of days in a year; and there should also be other "tree circles," but we don't here of any. (But if things are so clearcut, it's hard to see how anyone could maintain that the "observatory" is "possibly of natural growth" (208, Ace pb).) But an equally plausible reading is that Marsch only saw, say, a dozen stumps and rotten trees, and from this extrapolated a ring of 402, prompted by Victor's father's lore. In that case what Marsch sees could have come about naturally, Marsch's beliefs notwithstanding (I don't think Marsch is a fool, but he is predisposed to believe in the existence of the Annese).

I'm becoming more and more convinced that the existence of the Annese is unknowable, and was meant to be unknowable.

--Adam

2002-05-20 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that the trees of the Woodhenge observatory were cut down by colonists decades after the French discovery, not by the first starship crew, and proposes a timeline placing the felling around year 127. Works from the NAFAL transit and radio delays, computing 42 years plus 127 to show the first settlers could not arrive until about 85 years before French discovery. Responds to Adam Stephanides's nitpick about the trees' age by reasoning from dates given in Annese and Marsch's report.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · puzzle-solving · theories: Colonists, not the first starship crew, felled the Woodhenge trees

You are right! And the trees were not likely cut down by the first starship crew. It was done by the settlers (aka colonists), and the first wave of settlers could not have come any sooner than 42 years (radio time plus NAFAL transit time) after French discovery.

42-127 = -85, or 85 years before French discovery. Rather than circa 120 French discovery.

Would the colonists cut down all the trees in their first year on the ground, or, like the stripping of the outer shells of the pyramids to build Cairo (historical echo), would it happen much later?

Marsch is reporting in year 180: it is technically possible that the observatory was planted by the first starship crew in the year 0, and then was not harvested as lumber until the colony grew large enough to have such demand, around year 127. Then the stumps would have 53 years to rot and decay before Marsch sees them.

IIRC year 127 would be close to the year that Number Four was born (FL 135). But still after the War (FL 100).
