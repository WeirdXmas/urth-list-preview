TBOTNS as political document and the wait for new sun

Urth list · 7 messages · 2003-07-09 to 2003-07-10

2003urthThe Book of the New SunMatthew MalthouseMichael Andre-DriussiJosh Geller
The thread: The thread asked whether Severian's narrative in The Book of the New Sun is a political propaganda piece, with Michael Andre-Driussi arguing its timing (the expected New Sun and time dilation) exposes Severian's misjudgment. Josh Geller disputed that a single manuscript copy to the citadel library rules out propaganda, since restricted access and scriptorium copying could spread it, and then argued the Commonwealth has controlled printing with few books. Geller and another poster also clashed over whether Ultan's Library extends beyond the Citadel into the City, with the text cited as explicit. Matthew Malthouse countered that a single copy sent to the library and one cast into the void makes contemporary propaganda an implausible motive, suggesting a concern for history fits better. The exchange ran mostly between Geller and Andre-Driussi and did not reach agreement.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: the narrative is propaganda whose timing (expected New Sun, time dilation) exposes Severian's misjudgment
  • Josh Geller: propaganda still possible via restricted library and scriptorium copying; Commonwealth printing is state-controlled and society largely illiterate
  • matthew.malthouse: one copy to the library and one cast into the void make propaganda implausible; a concern for history fits better

Themes: Unreliable narration, Books, libraries and writing, Time loops and causality

2003-07-09 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Severian's narrative is a propaganda piece whose timing matters: Severian expected the New Sun to arrive within months of boarding the ship, so the wait and time dilation expose his misjudgment. Supports this by citing Canog's lost book, Severian's title choice, and the Conciliator's overheard talk of a new sun.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian will bring the new sun himself; Severian is Apu Punchau but not the Conciliator; Severian's book finishes Canog's storyline

I want to point out that John Clute first raised the interpretation of The Book as political. His essay is in STROKES, a collection of his essays and reviews.

This raises the question of how long anyone thinks the wait will be. Severian is probably the most "in the know" in the Commonwealth (aside from Father Inire, of course), and judging by his actions/reactions, he seems to have thought that the whole thing would happen in real-time, synchronized with his experience, so that he would return to Urth in less than a year and the Old Sun would have been replaced with a New Sun.

That is: from the moment he gets on the Ship, the Old Sun will last only a few more months -- certainly not a year.

From his point of view it took a bit longer than he thought, but still less than a year, whereas from Urth's point of view 40 years had gone by. Whoops!

The distinction is being made between Canog's book (he overheard the Conciliator talking to his friends about the death of the sun and the coming of a new one), which presumably became something of a bible for the church of the Conciliator, and Severian's book, which is a rip-roaring heroic adventure tale (with graphic sex and violence, monsters and magic, etc.).

I think Severian the Narrator chooses to call his tale by the same name because he understands that he will be bringing the new sun: he himself really will be "finishing" that tale, a somewhat garbled storyline begun 1000 years earlier (by himself, but he doesn't guess that part yet: he knows he will be Apu Punchau, but not the Conciliator). And nobody remembers Canog's book, anyway -- iirc, Talos says it is "long lost."

2003-07-10 Josh Geller #

Argues that the Commonwealth in The Book of the New Sun has mechanical printing but only under state control, with few books and no newspapers, because the society is mostly illiterate. Grounds this in the absence of a Printer's Guild and in the Library's control of presses, and suggests Ultan had copies made for the library branches; it responds to a reply that questioned whether the society lacks printing.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Commonwealth controls printing and limits literacy

Actually, I misspoke slightly. I think that they do have mechanical printing, but not much need for it because the society is mostly illiterate.

Exactly. But I think what I say still applies. There don't seem to me too many books around, and no newspapers. There is also no mention of a Printer's Guild (btw, secular guilds in the middle ages did have ceremonies analogous to the St. Katherine's Day feast). I think that if there are any printing presses around, the librarians control all or most of them, and in the same way that the use of the roads is forbidden to the general populace, the use of printing is forbidden to people not associated with the government, as the Library certainly is.

Perhaps upon receiving the book, Master Ultan had enough copies made to stock the branches of the library.

.

2003-07-10 Matthew Malthouse #

Argues that Severian's claim to have sent one manuscript copy to the citadel library and cast the other into the void makes contemporary political propaganda an implausible motive for the book. Responds to Michael Andre-Driussi's reply to Marc Aramini, which cited John Clute's essay on the political reading, by suggesting that a concern for history fits better.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's manuscript is written for history not propaganda

Severian states that he sent one copy of the manuscript to the library at the citadel and will cast the second - the copy we are reading - into the void.

Wouldn't a conscious effort at propaganda require a rather more active effort at publication and dissemination? Without that I can't see Severain having contemporary politic motives as being credible. A concern for history - one that rulers often have - looks more plausible.

Matthew
