Themes: Unreliable narration, Women, sex and gender

2001-08-02 John Bishop #

Argues that the narrator of There Are Doors is insane and living in our world, not in the alien society he describes, because the proposed sexual system is evolutionarily implausible and reads like a psychotic's belief. Grounds this in the death-after-intercourse premise requiring women to bear multiples or store semen, and briefly praises The Magician's Nephew in a Narnia comparison.
proposes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Narrator of There Are Doors is insane and in our world

Personally, of the Narnia books I prefer _The_Magician's_Nephew_, because it has that wonderful vision of a dying world, and because the Empress castigates the English, "You treat your slaves poorly", to which they reply "But they aren't slaves". That exchange could make a middle-class child of the time _think_ about the status of the working class.

But which ever book is your favorite, they're clearly in the short list of "possible best juvenile fantasy of the 1900s".

In _There_Are_Doors_, the death-after-intercourse requires that all women either bear twins or triplets or store semen for later pregnacies (as many insects do). Otherwise the species dies out. How you would evolve such a system is hard to figure out, and there's no implication that _this_ is how mammals work in this world.

The evolutionary wierdness of the idea, coupled with the non-wierdness of it as one psychotic's belief about sexuality, seemed to me to be a hint that the "hero" is _not_ in such a world--he's in our world, but he is insane.

-John Bishop
