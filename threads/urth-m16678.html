The thread: A thread begun by a newcomer's enthusiasm for Wolfe's prose in The Book of the New Sun quickly drifted from textual praise into recommendations for Neal Stephenson and talk of meeting other fans. Several posters (Stockhoff, Petersen, Scriabin, Danehy-Oakes) praised favorite passages such as Ultan's library, the Claw as rosebush thorn, and Talos's fox-mask face. Petersen argued that Wolfe's prose is initially unimpressive but works invisibly and grows more exquisite, and that ornate and spare styles divide readers. Most later posts were reading-order advice on Stephenson or social asides, so the thread ended in drift rather than resolution.
  • Daniel Petersen: Wolfe's prose is underrated and works invisibly, and ornate and spare styles divide readers
  • David Stockhoff: Wolfe's prose is beautiful, with the Citadel lotus passage as an example, while Stephenson's novels feel hollow
  • Antonin Scriabin: Ultan's library passage made them fall in love with Wolfe, and they recommend Stephenson's Baroque Cycle
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Recommends Anathem and Snow Crash as starting points and praises the Claw passage
  • Jerry Friedman: Tastes differ, and some friends find Wolfe's style verbose

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Love and loss, Names and their meanings

BNS has some of the most gorgeous lines I have ever read. There are many others, but I think most often of the description of lotuses on Gyoll in CotA immediately after Maxellindis's uncle's talk of what appear to be the legions of Erebus:

With that he fell silent, looking out over the nenuphars. We were well above that part of

Gyoll opposite the Citadel, but they were still packed more densely than wildflowers in

any meadow this side of paradise.

This kind of mood/content juxtaposition enraptures me.

2011-12-19 Daniel Petersen #

Shares a newcomer's enthusiastic Facebook comments on The Book of the New Sun, praising its languid, ivy-wrapped prose and comparing it favourably with Peake's Titus Groan. Relays the friend's remarks as evidence that first-time readers are being won over quickly by Wolfe's style.
informs · genre-and-craft

Thought people here might enjoy seeing that new readers are still discovering Wolfe for the very first time and being instantly enchanted. A friend of mine (an aspiring writer) who loves Tolkien and China Mieville finally, at my persistent insistence, obtained BotNS and posted this to me on Facebook just now about what he's read so far:

'I love the languid, ivy-wrapped prose that Wolfe writes in. I've been discovering that this is a style I find myself entranced by when I read it. I was not long ago working through Titus Groan and found myself ensconced by many of the same elements that I'm loving in Wolfe's writing. Mr. Wolfe seems much better at marrying plot and poesy than Mr. Peake, however.

I'm very excited about what waits around the corner. BotNS seems like the kind of novel I dream of writing.'

Some pretty apt comments, I thought.

-DOJP

2011-12-19 Antonin Scriabin replying to Daniel Petersen #

Agrees with Daniel Petersen that the prose of SotT is easy to overlook, and says Wolfe's writing in New Sun only struck them after reading Stephenson's Quicksilver and The Confusion. Grounds this in a comparison: after loving Quicksilver and Shadow and Claw, The Confusion felt weaker, so Wolfe's prose stood out, and they recommend that novel as an adventure.
supports · genre-and-craft

Daniel, I understand what you mean about the prose being so good it kind of slips under the radar, and "does its job". I read Neal Stephenson's * Quicksilver* and absolutely loved it, then read *Shadow and Claw*, which I loved even more. Then I went back to read Stephenson's *The Confusion*, and it felt like I had really, really stepped down a notch. I didn't realize how good the prose in *New Sun* was until I read something else; then it hit me how incredibly well Wolfe can write.

P.S. I feel like I should mention that *The Confusion*, once I got back into the swing of that story, ended up being one of my favorite "adventure" novels. I highly recommend it!

2011-12-19 Daniel Petersen replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Argues that Wolfe's prose in The Book of the New Sun is initially unimpressive but grows more exquisite later, and that his writing works invisibly, so readers notice it only later. Responds to Antonin Scriabin's praise of Ultan's library passage in The Shadow of the Torturer by generalizing from his own reading experience and adding that the Long Sun and Short Sun prose is equally fine.
supports · genre-and-craft

You know, it's funny. The first chapters of SotT did *not* terribly impress me as to writing style when I first tackled BotNS over a decade ago. There was nothing *wrong* with them at all - flawless grammar and syntax and what have you. The story itself and its themes began to sweep me up as the book really got going, but it wasn't until later in the book or series that the writing itself began to leap out at me as really exquisite prose.

Plus, sometimes I wonder if even the writing style itself is 'hidden in plain sight' from the reader along with so much else. The writing is *so* good (grammatical and graceful as Wolfe recommends to aspiring writers) that it just ends up doing its job so effectively that you don't always notice it until you're quite some way into a work.

Also, I'll here mention that I love the quality of prose in Long and Short Suns just as much as New Sun. It's just doing something totally different than Severian's baroque machinations.

-DOJP

2011-12-21 Antonin Scriabin replying to Daniel Petersen #

Describes the poster's effort to draw others into Gene Wolfe's work by displaying his full collection of novels and story collections by his front door, with passersby occasionally asking about the author. Replies to Daniel Petersen's earlier message and to David Stockhoff's note about his daughter reading Innocents Aboard, sharing a social anecdote rather than a critical point.
chats

I am trying to get people to ask about him by sticking the books on the shelf right near my front door. I have all of Wolfe's novels (multiple copies of some) and most of the short story collections. It takes up a lot of space! Occasionally I will have someone ask "So who is this Wolfe fella?" but no one has bit for real yet. Hopefully soon!
