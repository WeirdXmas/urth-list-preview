Fuligin?

Urth list · 20 messages · 2014-07-15 to 2014-07-17

2014urthThe Book of the New SunRyan BullerMo Holkar / UKGGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffMarc AraminiDavid DuffyJeff WilsonAntonin Scriabin
The thread: Readers debated whether the hydrargyrum/fuligin liquid in the sword hilt of The Book of the New Sun is simply mercury under an archaic or renamed label. David Stockhoff and Jeffery Wilson argued Wolfe would have written 'mercury' outright given the modern technology in the text, while Mo Holkar, Marc Aramini and Gerry Quinn held that it is mercury under an old or renamed name. The thread then shifted to whether a mercury-filled executioner's sword would help the swing; Gerry Quinn argued it gained no real striking force, and David Duffy ended by offering a historical sliding-weight sword as an example. The naming question was not resolved, and the mechanics question drifted into a sword-design side discussion.
  • David Stockhoff: Wolfe would have said 'mercury' outright, so the substance is likely something other than plain mercury
  • Jeffery Wilson: Modern words are used where the thing is recognizable, so the Urth word would be 'mercury'
  • Mo Holkar: Hydrargyrum is an archaic name for mercury, traced to its Greek roots
  • Marc Aramini: Fuligin may be an old name for mercury, since the Latin is mistranslated
  • Gerry Quinn: Fuligin may be a renamed mercury, and a mercury-filled sword would gain no real striking force
  • David Duffy: A historical sliding-weight sword shows such blades have been built

Themes: Names and their meanings, Robots, androids and artificial life, War and soldiers

2014-07-16 Jeff Wilson replying to Mo Holkar #

Questions why Wolfe's word hydrargyrum, used for the liquid in the broken sword hilt in The Shadow of the Torturer, would be a Latin term rather than a common word if the substance is simply mercury. Cites Wolfe's note that Latin marks inscriptions in a language Severian considers obsolete, and the passage where the hilt's hydrargyrum runs out in silver tears.
questions · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading

G.W. says, "Latin is once or twice employed to indicate that inscriptions and the like are in a language Severian appears to consider obsolete. What the actual language may have been, I cannot say."

In 3.37, it is writ, "Whatever had occurred, the mace was gone, and I held in my hands only the sword's hilt, from which protruded less than a cubit of shattered metal. The hydrargyrum that had labored so long in the darkness there ran from it now in silver tears."

If this substance is mercury, as known to the ancient Greeks and popularized in current science and left in dangerous quantities from the mid 20th century peak of its production and use, and suitable for Galilean vacuums, mercury switches, and driving hatters mad, it seems odd that there would be no current word for it encountered by Severian.

2014-07-16 Mo Holkar / UKG replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that the liquid called hydrargyrum in The Book of the New Sun is simply mercury, tracing the word's Greek roots (hydor and argyros) to show it is an archaic name rather than a different substance. Responds to David Stockhoff's suggestion that the 'spinning liquid silver' might be something more exotic, noting the text's descriptions all fit mercury.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading

Hydrargyrum is the Latinized Greek name for mercury, hence its chemical symbol Hg. The 'gyr' is just part of 'argyros', Greek for silver.

My understanding is that everything described about this liquid in the text is satisfied by mercury, so there seems no reason to consider it to be anything different.

It's not the same (it seems to me) as where he calls a riding beast by the name of a prehistoric animal and so we must understand it's not that animal and nor is it a horse. Hydrargyrum is not an ancient substance predecessor to mercury; it's an archaic name for precisely contemporary mercury.

Mo

2014-07-16 David Stockhoff replying to Gerry Quinn #

Argues that the mercury-filled hollow blade of Severian's executioner's sword is impractical but may be theatrical, with mercury shifting toward the hilt to make lifting easier and steadying the downstroke. Responds to Gerry Quinn's quoted reply to Marc Aramini, which had doubted any mechanical benefit, by citing Severian's use of the sword in combat and the psychological role of the executioner.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Fuligin mercury acts as a crude gyroscope

It's probably impractical. On the other hand, its "gimcrackery" emphasizes the theatrical nature of the the role of executioner. It might even be intended as much to frighten thieves and the gullible (as it did once) as to aid in the swing.

Besides, when you first lift the sword from the horizontal, the mercury would rush toward the hilt, bringing its center of gravity closer to the body. I think the real benefit may be simply to make lifting it easier. It could also assist in accuracy on the downswing, a notorious problem for executioners. Think of it as a crude gyroscope.

Because Severian doesn't seem to be bothered by its effects when using the sword in combat (for which is certainly was not intended), they may be slight. But it could speed the blade just enough to make a stroke look decisive, which as Severian suggests plays to the psychological aspects of the job, i.e., the problem of the executioner not being up to the job.

It's actually amazing that no one has tried to make one, at least that I have heard of. I wonder what technical challenges would be involved.

the job.

have heard of. I wonder what technical challenges would be involved.
