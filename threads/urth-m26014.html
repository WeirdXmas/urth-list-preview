The thread: The thread began as a joke about Wolfe's apparent paradoxes and quickly turned into a dispute about whether modus ponens and formal logic can be trusted, after Frederick Hudson Norwood challenged skeptics to explain how a true A implying B can have a false B. Norwood defended modus ponens as unprovable bedrock, while Jeffery Wilson pressed regress arguments drawn from Lewis Carroll and Marc Aramini argued a conditional's truth depends only on its premise being false. António Pedro Marques argued that ordinary language does not obey formal logic and was accused of post-modernism, and the exchange ended in personal bickering about rigor and the argument's direction, with little engagement with Wolfe's texts.
  • Frederick Hudson Norwood: modus ponens is unprovable bedrock and a false premise makes a theorem inapplicable, not disproved
  • Jeffery Wilson: modus ponens faces Carroll-style regress and the reflexive law A is A is simpler
  • Marc Aramini: a conditional is true only by linguistic definition and modus ponens cannot be misapplied
  • António Pedro Marques: human language does not follow the constraints of formal logic
  • Lee: the cherubim phrase shows language can express what logic cannot
  • Brendon Fuhs: the self-referential puzzle shows the thread's logic changes the meaning of true

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Mirrors, pictures and art, Names and their meanings

2014-10-15 Brendon Fuhs replying to Norwood, Frederick Hudson #

Offers a playful logic puzzle: in a universe where anything implied by a truth counts as false, the claim that such a universe is inconsistent turns out to be consistent, and the poster declares it Wolfe-related because of Wolfe's apparent paradoxes. Responds to Frederick Hudson Norwood's challenge about how A can be true while B, implied by A, is false, and his remark that he will go read Wolfe. The post is a joke-like reply that uses self-referential logic rather than analysing any passage in the books.
chats

Here is my attempt.

Suppose we are in a universe in which any statement implied by a true statement is considered false. Thus, if "A is True" and "A => B", then "B is False". But if we are in such a universe, and the fact that we are in such a universe implies the relationships we have described between A and B, then by the nature of implication in that universe, those relationships can't possibly be true. As it happens, we don't live in such a universe. Therefore the statement "if A is True and A => B, then B is False" is perfectly consistent! ;)

I declare this to be Gene Wolfe related because Wolfe likes to use apparent paradoxes and deliberately slippery logic in his worldbuilding.

2014-10-15 Jeff Wilson replying to Norwood, Frederick Hudson #

Argues that modus ponens is itself subject to an infinite regress, citing the Carroll-style tortoise and Achilles dialogue, and suggests that if abstract logical truths count as absolute, the simpler law A is A would serve. Responds to Norwood's challenge that one cannot be certain of anything, using a logic argument rather than discussing Wolfe's books.
disputes

That is known as modus ponens (the method that affirms), but that's short for modus ponendo ponens (the method that affirms by affirming), and as the longer Latin name implies, its truth is complicated by an infinite regress, as dramatized by another famed fantasy author:

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_the_Tortoise_Said_to_Achilles

If you consider a true statement about abstracted symbols to be an absolute truth, why not save steps and just say "A is A"? That remains valid regardless of the values of A or B or the validity of modus ponens.

2014-10-16 Norwood, Frederick Hudson replying to Jeffery Wilson clueland.com #

Argues in verse and prose that Lewis Carroll's Achilles and the tortoise paradox has been resolved by modern mathematics, invoking Godel's theorem on unprovable truths. Replies to Jeffery Wilson's challenge about whether A implying B can hold while B is false, citing Hamilton's Logic for Mathematicians, and Brendon Fuhs answers that this changes the meaning of 'true'. The post does not discuss Wolfe's fiction.
chats

Jeffery Wilson: I do say A is A. That's called the reflexive property of equality. And, yes, I've read the Lewis Carroll bit. Even wrote a poem about it.

Said the mathematician Kurt Godel,
There are obstacles we'll never hurdle,
There's a theorem that's true
But no proof can you do,
'Till Achilles outraces the turtle.

Modern mathematics has dealt with the paradox. See, for example, Logic for Mathematicians by Hamilton.

Brendon Fuhs: Sure, you can say any consequence of a true statement is false, but I think you've just changed the meaning of the word "true", not invented a new logic.

Rick Norwood

2014-10-17 Marc Aramini replying to Norwood, Frederick Hudson #

Argues that the statement 'if A then B' is true only by linguistic definition, so a conditional's truth depends on its premise being wrong rather than on any deeper logic. Illustrates with 'If I have no head I will die', claiming that technological advance makes the premise false and so the conditional meaningless. Responds to Frederick Hudson Norwood's quoted passage on whether modus ponens can be proved or doubted, which is only loosely tied to Wolfe's books.
disputes

These are linguistic truths built into the definitions themselves ... the statement if A then B is either true linguistically
or it isn't, in which case the premise was wrong. It doesn't mean much else.
ie "If I have no head I will die." technology advances, and now I can live without a head - the original premise then becomes false. Doesn't mean anything.
