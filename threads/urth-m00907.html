The thread: A thread that began with Jeffrey Lefstin finding the pro-gun and supply-side passages in Exodus from the Long Sun clumsily inserted, and wondering whether Wolfe meant them as satire or as his own views. It spread into a dispute over whether a post quoting Orwell's attack on the dead Chesterton and his remark about converts to Catholicism was insulting, with aqala defending the quote and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and James Wynn objecting. Wynn argued the political views seem jarring because a sympathetic character voices them against the liberal tone of SF, and Dan'l countered with a conservative SF tradition. Chris ended the discussion by reading Silk's stance as revolutionary political pragmatism about power rather than gun control; the thread trailed off without resolution.
  • Jeffrey Lefstin: the political passages in Exodus from the Long Sun feel clumsily inserted and may be Wolfe's own views
  • aqala: defends quoting Orwell on Chesterton and says offence is the reader's problem
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: an insult does not lose force through repetition; conservatism in SF has a long tradition but is not thereby right
  • James Wynn: the pro-gun and supply-side views are jarring because a sympathetic wise character holds them against SF's liberal tone
  • Chris: Silk's gun stance is revolutionary pragmatism about distributing power, so the passages fit the plot

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Catholic faith and sacraments, Books, libraries and writing

2005-01-11 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to aqala #

Disputes aqala's claim that denouncing religion has a long tradition and so is acceptable, arguing that an insult does not lose its force through repetition. Objects to aqala's remark that converts to Catholicism in their 40s lack intellect, calling it a general slight on anyone who embraces the faith after thought.
disputes

Yours.

Specifically:

"A man so lacking in thought and intellect he converted to Catholicism when in his 40's."

While stated as a remark on Chesterton, it is in fact a general remark on the intellegence of anyone who converts to
Catholicism, and especially in their 40s. (Which - by the way - does not take an apostrophe.)

To me and anyone else who voluntarily embraces Catholicism after a great deal of thought.

I refuse to insult you by assuming that you are too dim to figure it out yourself from teh clues given.

There is also a lengthy tradition of denouncing blacks as half-animals, jews as greedy Christ-killers, atheists as closed-minded bigots, etc. An insult repeated for
a long time does not become less insulting thereby.

--Dan'l

2005-01-11 James Wynn replying to Jeffrey Lefstin #

Argues that the pro-gun-ownership and supply-side economics passages in Exodus from the Long Sun seem jarring because Wolfe gives these views to a sympathetic, wise character, against the usual liberal tone of science fiction. Responds to Jeffrey Lefstin, who found those passages clumsily inserted and wondered whether they were satire or Wolfe's own views, by offering a reason they read as unexpected rather than awkward.
disputes · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe's conservative politics explain the jarring pro-gun passages

I didn't think they were clumsily inserted. I think they were jarring because the plurality of SciFi writers are politically liberal.

Thus, placing favorable views to gun-ownership and supply-side economics in the mouth of a very simpathic and (presented as) wise character might have caught you unaware if you did not previously know of Wolfe's conservative politics.

~ Crush

2005-01-11 Chris P replying to James Wynn #

Disputes James Wynn's claim that the gun-ownership and supply-side passages in Exodus from the Long Sun were clumsily inserted, arguing instead that they fit the plot. Reads Silk's stance on guns as political pragmatism in a revolutionary situation, where the issue is power distributed to individuals rather than gun control as such.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft

I am undecided on the supply-side economics bit, but I did not think that the gun-ownership bit was clumsily inserted either; it's quite to the point and a real concern in the particular political situation Silk found himself in. And ultimately it seemed to me like Silk's position on guns, at that point, was largely a matter of political pragmatism rather than universal principle. To me, the issue there wasn't "gun control" but rather the distribution of power to individuals in an unstable, revolutionary atmosphere.

I am not sure that Wolfe could even have realistically avoided the subject.
