Names on the Whorl

Urth list · 9 messages · 2010-09-07 to 2010-09-10

2010urthThe Book of the Long SunThe Book of the New SunthalassocratGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffJeff WilsonDave Tallman
The thread: The thread asked whether the names of characters and places on the Whorl follow a pattern, with Blood's name in The Book of the Long Sun as the main example. Dave Tallman argued that Maytera Rose coined 'Blood' from 'Bloody' after a difficult birth, and that the Whorl's varied town naming was deliberately built to keep people apart. Mr Thalassocrat questioned whether the name was more than a pet name. David Stockhoff read the officers Warren and Wight as plainly British surnames, while Jeff Wilson proposed they came from shaded underground places, and Stockhoff then agreed and extended that idea. The thread ended with competing etymologies rather than agreement.
  • Dave Tallman: Blood's name was coined by Rose at a bloody birth, and the varied naming was deliberate division
  • Mr Thalassocrat: questions whether 'Bloody' was only a pet name and why Viron alone uses its naming system
  • David Stockhoff: Warren and Wight are distinctive British names, later agreeing Typhon named things to erase memory of Urth
  • Jeff Wilson: Warren and Wight may come from wight-dwelling shaded regions of the Whorl, not English dictionary origins

Themes: Names and their meanings, Memory and forgetting, Colonization and the native

2010-09-07 Dave Tallman #

Argues that Blood's name in The Book of the Long Sun does not fit the naming pattern others proposed, since Maytera Rose coined it from an adjective after a difficult childbirth. Supports the view that mothers decide names, citing Nettle, whose mother named her out of dislike.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Mothers have the most say in naming

Blood is not a good example. We happen to know the exact circumstances of his naming, and Maytera Rose broke the regular convention by calling him "Bloody" after her difficult (perhaps C-section) childbirth. Since that was an adjective and not a noun it was soon converted to Blood by everyone except Rose herself.

It looks like the mother has the most say about the name. Another example is Nettle, named so by her mother out of dislike for her child.

2010-09-08 David Stockhoff replying to Mr Thalassocrat #

Argues that the IGJ officers Lieutenant Warren and Lieutenant Wight have plainly British names, glossing Warren as a surname for an enclosure or game preserve and Wight as Scottish, and concluding that such names are distinctive rather than generic trade names. Responds to David Stockhoff's question about whether the Anglo names derive from trades like Baker or Chandler, supporting the point with dictionary definitions of each name.
answers · source-and-allusion

Of course, no "Anglo" name has a simple history. But these are plainly British:*
*
---Warren (/woh-renn/), of old Germanic
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Germanic> origin, is a common English language <http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language> masculine <http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masculine> given name
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Given_name>,or a surname
<http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surname> meaning "enclosure". 1
/chiefly British/ /a/ *:* a place legally authorized for keeping small game (as hare or pheasant) /b/ *:* the privilege of hunting game in such a warren

---"Wight" is Scottish, either from pale/fair-haired or nimble/strong.

My thought was that these names could be found in any language, like a name derived from a particular trade. But they are pretty unique, whichever derivation you choose.

2010-09-08 Dave Tallman replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that Viron's 'Blood' name for the character Blood comes from Rose/Marble's own naming at his bloody birth, citing the C-section scene in Calde of the Long Sun, and that the Whorl's varied town naming systems were deliberately set up to keep people from uniting. Supports this with the Silk passage on natural barriers between towns in Exodus from the Long Sun and compares it to the Tower of Babel.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Rose/Marble gave Blood his name at birth; Whorl town cultures artificially established by the Mainframe

He grew up with the name Blood, and it's hard to see where else he couild have got it.

Rose/Marble always calls him this, even in formal situations like introducing him to the crowd when he came with a sacrifice. I think that's the real name she gave him. The description of the bloody C-section birth is on p. 111 of CotLS. When she saw the baby all bloody from the operation she gave him that name.

In EftLS, p. 380, Silk points out the natural barriers between towns and suggests they were put there to keep the people from uniting against the Mainframe. I think the different town cultures and naming quirks were artificially established for the same reason. It's a bit like the story of the Tower of Babel in Genesis.

2010-09-09 Jeff Wilson replying to David Stockhoff #

Proposes that the names Warren and Wight may come from a perpetually shaded region of the Whorl where wights live in tunnel warrens, rather than from their English dictionary origins. Responds to David Stockhoff's quoted etymologies by noting the ancient link between wight and underground dwellings and Tolkien's barrow wight.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Warren and Wight derive from a shaded Whorl region

"Wight" is also a person or a less-than-a-person, and it's roots' association with underground dwellings is ancient, though perhaps not as prominent as Tolkein's use of the 19th century "barrow wight".

I'm still trying to get started on the first Whorl book, but could Warren and Wight be from the neighborhood of one of those perpetually shaded places in the Whorl, where one might fear to enter a tunnel warren lest one encounter the wights who inhabit it?

Speculates that Typhon chose names for people and places on the Whorl to erase memory of Urth, naming them after objects in their environment so the inhabitants seem more native to the world. Responds to Jeff Wilson's suggestion that Warren and Wight may derive from underground dwellings and shaded places in the Whorl, agreeing and extending that idea.
supports · source-and-allusion · theories: Typhon chose Whorl names to erase memory of Urth

Yes! I knew there must be some reason Wolfe chose them, other than pointing to an Earthly past.

I wonder if Typhon's intent was to erase all memory of Urth in peoples' names. Naming themselves after objects in their environment, and their environment itself, must be a good way to do that.

It also makes the Whorlians seem more autochthonous.
