The thread: Starting from John Smith's claim that Horn's reflections in On Blue's Waters express Wolfe's own conservatism of slow, careful change, the thread quickly turned into a fierce US tax and government debate. Jordon Flato, Jerry Friedman, Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and James Wynn disputed the tax-cut rule's fiscal consequences, while brunians and Brochyus argued lower taxes and taxation as robbery. 2009-03-26 John Smith #

Argues that Horn's reflections in On Blue's Waters on ruling, delaying hasty action and laying new burdens on the people express Gene Wolfe's own political view, a conservatism favouring slow, careful change. Grounds this in quoting the passage from Chapter 7 about General Mint, advisors and the speed of action, and draws a parallel to present society.
proposes · author-and-biography · theories: Horn expresses Gene Wolfe's own conservative political view

Rereading "On Blue's Waters," I came upon some of Horn's reflections on being a ruler: "My advisors, who are all good, well-intentioned men, are forever suggesting that I get down to business, although they never phrase it quite so baldly. If action must be taken, they want it taken now, immediately...Immediate action is the soul of war, as I learned many years ago by observing General Mint. It is not the soul of peace...When a new burden must be laid upon the people, we should remove two, and look very carefully, first, at those we have chosen to remove. Those who sail fast do not sail for long, while what is written with great rapidity is rarely read--or worth reading." (OBW, Chap. 7)

Horn expresses--I think--Gene Wolfe's view, a view of true conservatism. Our present society is certainly not perfect, but change should be slow and careful, lest we create something worse.

Best wishes,

Jack

2009-03-26 James Wynn replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that a proposed fiscal resolution attributed to Horn is not exactly what was suggested, and that it leads to bankruptcy only under two conditions: governments cannot restrain spending, or good governance requires ever-growing shares of the economy. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's quoted remark calling it a formula for bankruptcy, explaining which premises that claim depends on.
disputes

I don't think this resolution is exactly what Horn suggested; however, it is only a formula for bankruptcy if one of the following is true:

A.) The leaders of a government cannot curb their inclination to spend more and more--or commit themselves to spending more and more--without regard to the money coming in (admittedly, a genuine impetus in democratic and autocratic governments)

B.) There is some kind of law of good governance that demands governments continually spend ever larger percentages of the nation's economy (which I don't consider true, and would inevitably lead to bankruptcy).

J.

Tax rates around the world vary considerably both in their statutory rates, and in their effective rates after all offsets are considered, preventing any straightforward comparisons of tax rates between countries. In some countries, for example, the United States, Canada and Switzerland, subnational governments also collect taxes, which further complicates the calculation of the tax rate.
