C. Sappan

The thread: Roy C. Lackey opened the thread arguing that the Jungle Garden in the story represents tropical Asia, citing a labelled Caesalpinia sappan tree, the OED entry for sapan, and the yellow-wood stilt hut. M.Driussi replied that the name refers to East Indian brazilwood used for red dye and suggested the hut is bamboo, adding etymology and the mixed origins of the setting. Lackey answered that the jungle may lie in Southeast Asia where the French held territory, since Robert was schooled in Paris, while granting the OED point was beside the point. The thread ended without agreement, with the sources of the setting left open.

  • m.driussi: the name refers to brazilwood dye, and the hut is more likely bamboo than sappan wood
  • Roy C. Lackey: the jungle may be Southeast Asia, linked to French holdings and Robert's Paris schooling

Themes: Names and their meanings, Colonization and the native, Memory and forgetting

1999-03-16 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that the Jungle Garden is meant to represent tropical Asia, not South America or Africa, because a fallen tree is labelled Caesalpinia sappan. Cites the OED entry for sapan, which places the plant in tropical Asia, and the yellow-wood stilt hut of chapter XX as further evidence.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Jungle Garden represents tropical Asia, not South America or Africa

Apparently the Jungle Garden is not meant to represent either S. America or Africa. One solid clue given is the name of the fallen tree Sev sees when the door of the Jungle Garden first swings open, near the end of chapter XIX. The sign on it reads: _Caesalpinia sappan_. The OED, under 'sapan', states that it is indigenous to tropical Asia and the Indian Archipelago. The roots of Caesalpinia Sappan, also called "yellow wood", are used to make yellow dye. Sev describes the hut as: "A house set on stilts of yellow wood. It's thatched with palm fronds.", near the end of chapter XX.

Roy

1999-03-17 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Explains that the name C. Sappan refers to brazilwood, an East Indian red wood used for red dye, and suggests the hut in the story is bamboo rather than sappan wood. Responds to Roy's remark by adding etymological context and listing the mixed origins of the wood, native, missionaries and animals in the setting.
informs · source-and-allusion

Roy,

Huh. I thought "C. Sappan" aka brazilwood aka "bresel wood" (when it was first imported to medieval Europe) is aka "the East Indian redwood" because its wood is a bright red in color and yields a red dye. (Yes, yes--the South American country was named after the Asian wood, not the other way around.)

FWIW I thought the hut was made of bamboo.

The wood is from East India; the autochthon is from Africa; the missionaries are from the Western World. But don't leave out the animals: a smilodon (sure, maybe some Asiatic tiger/African cat); an oreodont; a red-breasted, blue-backed parrot; and a reptile with evil teeth and a finned back.

=mantis=
