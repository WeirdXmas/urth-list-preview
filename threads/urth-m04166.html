The thread: Readers asked why the Latro books, especially Soldier of Sidon, drew so little substantive discussion, and whether they were too plain, dull, or hard to follow. 2007-06-27 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to don doggett #

Argues that the Latro books, especially Soldier of Sidon, are the hardest Wolfe novels to read and yield little hidden meaning because their surface is so plain. Grounds this in Latro's memory loss, which creates repetition and removes the usual Wolfean lacunae, and in the poster's greater familiarity with Egyptian than Greek history, which made Sidon easier to follow.
supports · genre-and-craft

Don,

Some personal thinks on "Soldier of Sidon."

1. I find the "Soldier" books the hardest to read of all Wolfe's work. I don't know why this should be. Part of it, I suspect, is that the artificiality of it is right on the surface; the conceit of Latro's memory loss, while poignant and meaningful, also creates a surface reason for Wolfe's usual lacunae, making it harder to look for meaning in them (Latro isn't suppressing unpleasant facts, he just doesn't know a lot of them), while at the same time almost forcing a degree of repetition that is totally un-Lupine. Because of this, the first two are my least reread Wolfe novels, and I chose to reread them prior to reading "Sidon," and so only got to "Sidon" this past month.

2. There was some discussion of "Sidon" after it came
out. I've saved it unread, and will get around to reading it soon. Maybe I'll respond to some of it.

3. In many ways, I found "Sidon" an easier read than its predecessors. Still quite difficult (something about Latro's style just does *not* fit well into my parser) but easier: possibly because I know Egyptian culture/history better
than Greek and had less trouble understanding what was
going on.

4. But I just don't find a great deal of meaning in these books. Partly, I'm sure, because I struggle just to read them, and because I've reread them so little; but partly because so much *is* on the surface. There seems to be very little
"hidden stuff" going on, other than whatever is going on with the gods of various lands bickering with each other. But once you take them as literally existing beings, well, that's what gods of that sort *do*.

2007-06-27 Daniel D Jones replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the Latro books are among the easiest of Wolfe's works to read because they are shallower and more plot-driven than the rest. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's point that the Soldier books are the hardest, agreeing with his point 4 about their shallowness while disagreeing about readability.
disputes

Interesting. I think the Soldier series are some of the easiest to read. In part, that's because I agree with point 4 - they also seem the shallowest of Wolfe's books. Some of the action is confusing, largely due to Latro's memory issues, but for the most part the books roll along with plenty of action and intrigue. Unlike much of Wolfe's stuff, they don't seem like they should have a gold stamp on the cover proclaiming "This Is Literature!" And I don't mean to imply that Wolfe is pretentious - I don't think that he is. But his stuff is deep, and the Soldier books, while they have plenty of depth to the story, seem more like they're "just" stories to be read primarily for pleasure.

2007-06-27 Matthew Groves replying to don doggett #

Says the Soldier of the Mist books are opaque to them, with a surface they enjoy but a hidden layer they cannot perceive, and complains that the gods Latro speaks with never name themselves in ways a modern reader could look up. Grounds this in Latro's amnesia-like mist, the unfamiliar place names and gods, and the foreword's warning that the words and punctuation cannot be fully trusted.
informs · genre-and-craft

The whole Soldier series is opaque to me: I enjoy the surface as I enjoy all Wolfe's writing, but I can tell there is something underneath that I can't quite perceive. Wolfe subjects the reader to something of Latro's own predicament by overlaying the events with the "mist" of his fictional translation -- e.g., the place names and gods are unfamiliar, and Wolfe suggests in the foreword that the punctuation and even the words themselves can't be completely trusted. Grrr! Throw a dog a bone, will ya? I can figure out the place names well enough, but if only the gods to whom Latro speaks would occasionally mention in passing the name by which they might be recognized or googled by a 21st-century reader whose public school education included not one single lesson in the Classics, it would make progress ever so much smoother for me.

2007-06-28 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to David Duffy #

Argues that the gods in Latro's books are expecting to lose their roles as rulers of humankind, while the God of the Israelites never appears to Latro. Places the Latro setting in the period after the return from Babylon, around Nehemiah's rebuilding of the Temple and before Antiochus and the Maccabees, answering David Duffy's question about where the God of the Israelites is.
proposes · theology-and-religion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Latro's gods expect to be ousted from rule

Lessee ... this would be during the period of the return from Babylon. Nehemiah would be exhorting the people to rebuild the Temple and reinstate the priestly class. Not *too* long after this comes the reign of Antiochus and the Maccabees.

As for "where is the God of the Israelites," He's not shown His face to Latro that I can see, but a few things some of the gods Latro meets -- no, I can *not* cite anything from memory! -- led me to think that they are expecting to be ousted from their jobs as rulers/guides of humankind soon (on their time scale).
