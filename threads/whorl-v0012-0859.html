Fan fic Fooey? Fooey.

The thread: Adam Stephanides and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued over whether the third-person sections of Wolfe's Short Sun and Return to the Whorl should be called fiction or fanfic. Stephanides held that these sections are partly invented and should be read as fiction, defending the fanfic label by its motives and comparing it to novels about historical figures. Danehy-Oakes held that the editors' invented material, especially the Remora conversation, is presented as factually equal to the Narrator's manuscript, which is misleading, and likened this to libel. Kevin J. Maroney joined in more playfully, arguing fanfic covers many kinds of material and joking about absurd New Sun pairings. The thread stayed in a debate over definitions and ended without resolution, drifting into jokes.
  • Adam Stephanides: third-person sections of Short Sun are fictional and should be read as fiction, defending the fanfic label by motive
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: editors' invented sections in Return to the Whorl pass fabrication off as factual, comparable to libel
  • Kevin J. Maroney: fanfic covers many kinds of material including real people; jokes about absurd New Sun pairings

Themes: Unreliable narration, Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

2001-06-19 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that the third-person sections of The Book of the Short Sun are fictional in part and should be read as fiction, though not literally fanfic, defending the term by its motives. Grounds this in fanfic about real people (boy bands, Pullman), the notion of slash, and comparison with novels about historical figures that invent conversations.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Third-person sections of Short Sun are a fictional protagonist based on Narr

It wasn't originally my term, and it wasn't a term I would have applied on my own. And I agree the sections aren't literally fanfic. But mantis used the term, iirc, to indicate

1) That the third-person sections are fictional, at least in part; and

2) That the motive of the fictionizers is to fill in emotionally satisfying bits absent from the original narrative.

The former of these is true, I would argue (see below) and the latter seems likely to be at least partially true for the conversation with Remora, and may well be true of the Whorl material.

As a matter of fact, on the website I cited earlier for Pullman fanfic, there is quite a lot of fanfic dealing with real people, namely music groups (the vast majority of it on boy bands: there are over five thousand Nsync fanfics).

"Slash" isn't necessarily pornographic or erotica. It posits a romantic or sexual relationship between two characters of the same sex, but need not depict sex.

If there were Lupine slash, I'd lay odds that the most popular relationship would be Horn/Krait.

Are those novels dealing with historical figures, which don't contradict the historical record but which invent situations and conversations not appearing in the record, outright lies? True, the editors did not present their third-person sections as fiction, and to that extent they are lying, at least by implication. But functionally, I would argue that the sections are fiction, and should be read as fiction, with a protagonist who is distinct from Narr, but based on the editors' view of Narr.

--Adam

2001-06-20 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the third-person sections of Return to the Whorl are fictionalized reconstructions whose editors' fabrications, especially the Remora conversation, pass off invented material as equal in factual standing to the Narrator's manuscript. Grounds this on a stipulated fiction-versus-fact distinction, comparing it to libel and fanfic, and on the claim that inserting invention alongside a genuine manuscript implicitly claims it facticity it does not deserve.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Return to the Whorl's third-person sections are fictional reconstructions by editors

Stipulated: within the completely fictional universe of discourse, the third-person narrative sections of RttW are fictional(ized) accounts of the activities of the Narrator Formerly Known as Horn.

Also stipulated: the claimed motives of fictional editors is to round out the incompleted narrative of the Narrator Latterly Known as Silk. Further stipulated: this is at least a part of their actual motivation.

I'm not sure what the correct term for this stuff is, but it's different from fanfic as known in the world of SF fandom. I suspect it might be actionable in a larger sense than copyright or trademark violation -- not clear if it would actually be considered libellous, but certianly it doesn't qualify as protected, either as reportage, as "satire or parody," or under any other protection I can think of. Okay, I'm not a libel lawyer, but there's a _reason_ for all those "no
resemblance to any person living or dead" disclaimers you read in the front of books, and there's no way any of those Nsync fans could claim that their fictional characters bore no resemblance to the real bandmembers. Oy, whatever.

Maybe now. It was originally. (Sorry; old fart speaking
here. I'm remembering the antediluvian 1970s and early
stuff like "The Rising.")

Duh. Should of thought of that one myself. Or maybe
Silk/Horn, though it would be hard to locate that
in the received chronology... Maybe Jolenta/Dorcas...

All fiction is lies ;*P

More seriously: if well-done, such a piece can be _true_ even though not _factual_. If presented as fact, it's a lie; if presented as a fictional reconstruction, it's not a lie.

And that gives the editors a kind of out: since they did go to the trouble of inserting -- no, not a disclaimer, just an explanation of what they did -- it's a non-lying fictional reconstruction.

Except, perhaps, for that interlude in the conversation with Remora: as others have noted, there is no plausible way for the editors to have done anything but make it up from whole cloth.

Functionally, I would disagree strongly. By interspersing their reconstructions with the Narr's manuscript, I feel that they've implicitly claimed for it a status of equivalent facticity, to which it is not entitled. Which drags me back to the Gospel According to Luke... but I'll let that one swing.

--Blattid
