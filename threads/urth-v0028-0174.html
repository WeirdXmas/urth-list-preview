Evidence of Christianity in Urth's past?

Urth list · 5 messages · 1999-10-06 to 1999-10-08

1999urthThe Book of the New SunMichael Andre-DriussiCraig ChristensenMichael Straight
The thread: Craig Christensen asked whether Christianity existed in Urth's past or was forgotten, citing Dorcas's memory of the rood. Michael Andre-Driussi argued it shaped Urth through the Conciliator's church, pointing to enamelled crucifixes, the pelerines' cross sigil, and names like Holy Katharine and Ctesiphon's Cross, and proposed Severian's narrative as a pagano-Christian text comparable to Dante. Michael Straight pressed him to clarify what Christian role the Conciliator's church actually had, then argued the Christian-seeming details may reflect the author's own affection for Christianity, as with Tolkien's Aragorn. The thread ends without resolution, with Straight's open question about how Severian's story relates to the Christian story left hanging.
  • Craig Christensen: asks whether Christianity was forgotten or never existed on Urth, citing the rood
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: Christianity shaped Urth's past via the Conciliator's church and names; Severian's story is pagano-Christian
  • Michael Straight: challenges the church claim, then says the Christian details reflect the author's taste rather than Urth's history

Themes: Catholic faith and sacraments, Myth and folklore sources, Names and their meanings

1999-10-06 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Christianity shaped Urth's past through the Conciliator's church, pointing to Dorcas's family's enamelled crucifixes and the pelerines' cross-form sigil as evidence. Responds to Craig Christensen's question about whether Christianity played any part in Urth's past, building on Greg Feeley's link between the pelerines and Dorcas's family.
supports · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Christianity shaped Urth's past and was forgotten; Pelerines bought crucifixes from Dorcas's family

It has been established that Dorcas's family made enamelled crucifixes. Likewise, that the pelerines are a band of Red Cross women (battlefield healers), and they use a cross form on their letters (the "narthex sigil of the order").

(Greg Feeley used these points to extrapolate that the pelerines bought their crucifixes from the family of Dorcas whenever they were in Nessus; this family connection/association then facilitates the meeting between Catherine and Ouen.)

So yes: a form of Christianity played a part in Urth's past, in the teaching and church of the Conciliator (at the very least); and was so long ago that it has been all but forgotten.

1999-10-06 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that the names Holy Katharine, St. Amand, Nilamon and Ctesiphon's Cross in The Book of the New Sun point to Christianity in Urth's past, offering three possible explanations. Proposes that Severian's narrative is a pagano-Christian text that approaches Christianity through pagan elements, comparing it with Dante's Comedy, in reply to Michael Andre-Driussi's question about the Conciliator's church.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Commonwealth culture remembers Christianity from Earth's past; Church of the Conciliator parallels Greek Orthodox Christianity; Severian's narrative is a pagano-Christian text

Rostrum,

(Where's Jim Jordan =now=?!) <g>

While I'm not about to produce an essay on the spot, I might be able to answer the question if it were a tad more specific.

Or, better: If you have something to say, go ahead and say it!

We are on the same page, I hope: Canog's THE BOOK OF THE NEW SUN.

A few details chosen at apparent random: Holy Katharine, St. Amand, Nilamon, Ctesiphon's Cross. It seems to me that there are only three ways for these to appear in the text: either the roots of the Commonwealth's culture go so deep as to remember Christianity (in the same way that the Apollo Mission photo is retained in the picture gallery); or, the Church of the Conciliator has recapitulated Christianity (mainly a Greek Orthodox form) in a sort of parallel evolution (so the Commonwealth's Katharine isn't Earth's Katharine, she just looks like a twin); or it is all just a translating error on the part of Gene Wolfe.

FWIW, I doubt that this approaches the answer you want to read, and as we have gone over this ground before, I have the sense of repeating myself repeating, still: I have a sense that Severian's Narrative is a pagano-Christian text, in that it approaches Christianity by way of pagan elements and traditions. It recapitulates Christianity along the way (just as an obscure Jewish sect was transformed by some Greeks and then some Romans). This is somewhat similar to Dante's Comedy, but also somewhat opposite: Dante gathered the suppressed pagan elements into a well established Christianity; Severian approaches the nebulous Christian through the established Pagan.

=mantis=

1999-10-08 Michael Straight #

Proposes that the Christian-seeming details in The Book of the New Sun may reflect the author's own liking for Christianity rather than a Christian past on Urth, comparing this to Tolkien's Aragorn. Asks whether Severian's story is meant to stand apart from the Christian story, be linked to it in the reader's mind, or be part of it.
proposes · author-and-biography · allegory-and-typology · theories: Christian resemblances reflect Wolfe's own taste not Urth's past

That last bit gets at the possibility I was wondering about. No one reading Tolkien thinks that Aragorn is Christ-like because there was Christianity in Middle Earth's past. The resemblances are attributed to Tolkien himself. Is it possible that the religious stuff in the New Sun looks like Christianity for the same reason the spaceships look like sailboats: because Wolfe likes Christianity and sailing?

I think one reason I ask is that I'm trying to puzzle out whether Severian's story is meant to be separate from the story of Christianity, to be related in the mind of the reader, or whether Wolfe intends the Christian story to be part of Severian's story (or vice versa).

-Rostrum
