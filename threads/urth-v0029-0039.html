2000-05-25 Dave Lebling #

Argues that the Autarchy and the Ascians offer two solutions to the same problem: humanity's inborn nature led Urth to the stars, war and destruction, and the black hole placement. Grounds this in the old Autarch's lecture to Severian in Citadel, which explains why the roads are closed, and links it to C. S. Lewis's view that space travel and immortality are sins.
proposes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Autarchy and Ascia share the same diagnosis of humanity; Black hole placed on the Sun as punishment for humanity's sin

The Autarchy and the Ascians are solutions to the same problem. That is, the people of Urth went to the stars, brought war and destruction, and were chastised by the placement of the black hole in the Sun. The cause is the inborn nature of humanity ("original sin," perhaps?). Both the Commonwealth and Ascia are attempting to keep mankind away from the stars until it is "perfected" or "cured" or "saved" (or whatever). Vodalus clearly rejects this point of view, proposing to recover Urth's empire, presumably with him at the head of it.

This is all pretty explicitly in the book, aside from whether the black hole was placed deliberately or it's just a funny coincidence. We are told all this by someone (the old Autarch, I _think_) lecturing Sev, in _Citadel_. It starts with an explanation of why the roads are closed.

I refer again to C. S. Lewis's position, explicated somewhat in _Out of the Silent Planet_ et seq., that space travel (or possibly only space colonization) and immortality are sins. This is a thread not far below the surface in much of _New Sun_ and even in other works by Wolfe.

-- Dave Lebling
(aka vizcacha)

2000-05-26 Michael Straight #

Argues that C. S. Lewis did not consider space travel or immortality sins in themselves, but bad ideas because they would let sin outgrow a lifetime and Earth. Responds to Dave Lebling's remark that this view runs through The New Sun, offering a finer distinction without citing any passage from Wolfe.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Lewis saw space travel as dangerous, not sinful

It's a fine point, but he didn't think space travel and immortality are themselves sins, rather they are a bad idea because they would allow sin to grow beyond it's current bounds of time (a lifetime) and space (Earth).

-Rostrum
