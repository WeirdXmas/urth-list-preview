The thread: Posters on the Urth list discussed Jeff VanderMeer's Ambergris books after JBarach noted that Gene Wolfe had blurbed VanderMeer's Shriek: An Afterword and wished someone tracked Wolfe's blurbs. Mo Holkar offered to start a wiki page for that purpose, and Mike Myers simply praised Shriek. The main debate was which of the three Ambergris novels is weakest: Craig Brewer picked Shriek, while Jordon Flato, Robert Thornton and James Crossley argued for Finch or, in Crossley's case, for Shriek as the most accomplished. The thread drifted entirely away from Wolfe's own fiction and ended without resolution, with opinions simply stated.
  • JBarach: reported Wolfe's blurb for Shriek and wished someone tracked Wolfe's blurbs
  • Mo Holkar / UKG: proposed a WolfeWiki page to list books Wolfe has blurbed
  • Craig Brewer: Shriek is the weakest Ambergris book; recommends City of Saints and Madmen
  • Jordon Flato: Shriek is the most compelling; Finch is the weakest, a Miéville-like book lacking soul
  • Robert Thornton: agrees Finch is weakest, finding Ambergris dreary in it
  • James Crossley: City of Saints and Madmen is flashiest, Shriek most accomplished, Finch a successful departure

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

2010-05-12 Craig Brewer replying to Mike Myers #

Argues that Jeff VanderMeer's novel Shriek is the weakest of his three Ambergris books, citing a fine premise that bogs down halfway and repetitive character study. Recommends City of Saints and Madmen to Wolfe and Crowley fans, responding to Mike Myers's post quoting Wolfe's blurb for Shriek.
disputes

I know this isn't the place for Vandermeer discussion...but for those interested, I'd have to say that _Shriek_ is the least successful of Vandermeer's three Ambergris novels. The premise is a blast (a missing brother has left behind a book, found by his sister, who decides to write an afterward to it explaining their lives...but the sister is now missing and the brother has found the book and is now commenting on her afterward by inserting notes as she tells it). But the execution becomes bogged down a bit halfway through once we basically know the whole story. And the character examination to me became a bit repetitive rather than illuminating at the same point.

The book that introduces the city of Ambergris, _City of Saints and Madmen_, though would I think be plenty interesting to Wolfe (and Crowley) fans for its variety of styles/mysteries/histories/wonders.

2010-05-14 James Crossley replying to Jordon Flato #

Argues that City of Saints and Madmen is the flashiest of Jeff VanderMeer's three Ambergris books and Shriek the most accomplished, while Finch is a successful but less likeable departure. Responds to Jordon Flato's preference for the first book and his dismissal of Finch, offering general impressions rather than close textual evidence.
disputes

City of Saints and Madmen is certainly the flashiest of the three, with its myriad styles. It leaves a lot to the imagination, in the best possible way, and is probably the most purely enjoyable book in the Ambergris cycle.

Shriek is also very rewarding, and I think the most accomplished of the set. The depth of characterization in the book is beyond almost anything else in genre. It?s quite different from its predecessor, which I find refreshing, although I can see where it wouldn?t push a reader?s buttons in the same way as CoS&M.

Finch is yet another departure, a successful one in my opinion, but again not necessarily a likeable one. In many ways, it wraps up the mysteries that the previous books set up, which I think lends to the deflating feeling that some have described.

James
