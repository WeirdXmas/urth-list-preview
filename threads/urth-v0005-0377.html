1997-11-03 John Bishop #

Argues that medieval readers lived with so much violence that Satan grilling sinners forever would not seem extreme, and that the eternity of the punishment was the shocking element. Supports this with an Economist claim that 13th-century English murder rates were ten times Detroit's and with the analogy of costly hand-made cloth and shoes, which makes a cast-off dress a major gift.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Medieval readers would not find Hell's fire shocking; Cast-off clothing was a major gift in the past

Others besides Wolfe have pointed out that medieval
and early modern daily experience was violent and gory; no one in 1100 would find the idea of Satan grilling
sinners over a fire a step too far from local practice. The stretch was that it didn't end! The _Economist_
magazine had an article which pointed out that murder
rates in 13th century England were ten times that of
today's Detroit.

Unless we remember this, we'll lose some of the meaning and connotations of old stories. As a parallel idea,
for us textiles are cheap because machine-made. In the past, every yard of cloth represented a vast investment of hand labor (even if it was cheap labor), and clothes were _expensive_. When you read stories from the past, think of clothes in general and shoes in particular as being like cars--necessary and costly, to be maintained and conserved, and an item of public display and status competition.

So when Princess A. gives servant B. her cast-off dress, that's not demeaning and cheap--it's a big gift, and
shows great favor.

-John
