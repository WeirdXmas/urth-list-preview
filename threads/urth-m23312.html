2012urthThe Book of the New SunJeff WilsonDavid StockhoffDaniel Petersen
The thread: Daniel Petersen opened the thread by sharing a simulation of a hollowed asteroid with a central light, as a model for the star crossers in Long Sun, while doubting it was built for interstellar travel. David Stockhoff proposed that the terrarium design shows Urth's spacefaring technology was advanced enough for Typhon to hollow out the Long Sun ship, possibly using pure-acceleration FTL. The discussion then drifted from engineering to Urth's place in the Solar Cycle: Petersen and Jeff Wilson argued Urth is a decayed backwater of a collapsed interstellar empire, with Typhon a petty tyrant amid ruins and Ozymandias imagery. Wilson also questioned how hierodules and the Yesodis fit a golden age that never existed. The thread ended without resolution, the hollow-asteroid question left behind.
  • Daniel Petersen: hollowed asteroid is a model for a star crosser's axial sun, though likely not built for interstellar travel
  • David Stockhoff: the terrarium design shows Urth was technologically advanced enough for Typhon to hollow the ship, perhaps with pure-acceleration FTL
  • Jeff Wilson: Urth is a mediocre remnant of a fallen civilization, with Typhon among Ozymandias-like ruins
  • Daniel Petersen: Urth is a forgotten backwater of a vast empire, Typhon a petty tyrant who ignores earlier tyrants' ruins

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Power, rulers and politics, Deep time and ruins

Proposes that the hollowed-asteroid 'terrarium' concept shows Urth's spacefaring technology was already advanced enough for Typhon to hollow out the Long Sun ship. Supports this with a link to an engineering design and speculates that Typhon's earlier ships, too small for large populations, may have used FTL by pure acceleration.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: Typhon hollowed the Long Sun ship using known process; Typhon's earlier ships used FTL by acceleration

http://www.projectrho.com/portfolio/port22.html

Incidentally, this "terrarium" concept does suggest something about Urth: that its spacefaring technology must already have been fairly advanced along these lines for Typhon to hollow out the Long Sun ship using just such a process as Robinson describes. His people knew what they were doing.

One might guess that his existing ships were unable to move large populations because they were too small; perhaps then they performed FTL travel by pure acceleration, being limited in size and therefore mass.

2012-05-17 Jeff Wilson replying to Daniel Petersen #

Argues that the hierodules and other Yesodis are mythic memories of a golden age that never existed, and questions how humanity could have reached that exalted state once and never again. Responds to Daniel Petersen's remark that the Urth stagnates after a mega-culture has collapsed, asking how the hierodules fit into that decline.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theology-and-religion · theories: Yesodis are mythic memories of a golden age that never was

I suppose the hierodules and other Yesodis sort of represent the memory of a fabled golden age that never was. They "know" humanity can do better and strive to better it a bit like an obsessive stage mother.

I'm leaning toward skepticality of the literal truth of their origin, though I'm not sure what the "real" origin would be. It makes sense for man's descendant to share his mythbuilding habits but if the origin as man's descendant is mythical, then I'm not sure why they would necessarily have the mythologizing impulse.

I'm also puzzled how humanity could have ascended to that exalted state of affairs the once and then never again approach it despite spiritual growth. Was the ascension and begatting of the pre-Yesodis achieved by the power of unalloyed depravity, to be equaled by the power of unalloyed virtue? Or was the original achievement one of the grace preserved by innocence, now lost but with mankind able to return to a state of grace in some future manvantara?

2012-05-17 Daniel Petersen replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that Urth and its neighbours in the Solar Cycle are a forgotten backwater of a vast interstellar empire, with Typhon as a petty tyrant rather than a true power. Responds to David Stockhoff's remark on Typhon's hollowed ship by citing the Solar Cycle's decay and its outcasts, then asks whether others see Urth as a tribal village.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Urth is a forgotten backwater in a galactic empire; Typhon is a petty overlord of a backwater; Solar Cycle narrators follow left-behind communities

His people knew what they were doing.

Yeah, that's the assumption back of the entire Solar Cycle, which is highly effective in giving a powerful sense of decay, and, even more so to me, the possibility that Wolfe's narrators are only following the exploits and dramas of basically 'left behind' 'backwater' communities (however vast and seemingly complex and important to themselves, with an Autarch and what have you). It actually has a strong plausibility to it.

It seems only too likely that a (inter)galactic space-faring multi-terrestrial empire (or mega-galactic interaction of cultures of some kind) of the magnitude suggested in the works would have 'pockets' of leftover cultural decay in the 'boondocks' of that massive crisscrossing of interstellar economy. And these pockets would have their overlords that would seem immensely powerful to the inhabitants, but are really lackeys and peons in the larger scheme, petty tyrannical landlords in the grip of an ultimately laughable megalomania - .e.g Typhon. (This would fit in very well with and hugely deepen Wolfe's whole focus on the 'outcasts' in the Solar Cycle.)

Has anybody else felt like Urth and co. are just a forgotten 'tribal village' tucked away in a vastly advanced cosmic mega-culture?

-DOJP
