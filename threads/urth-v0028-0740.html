Themes: Women, sex and gender

2000-01-27 Richard Horton #

Compares the portrayal of wives in Wolfe's "The Ziggurat" with Kingsley Amis's novel The Russian Girl, noting both depict wives as thoroughly vicious. Draws the comparison from reading the two works around the same time, and qualifies it by mentioning Amis's feelings about his second wife, Elizabeth Jane Howard.
proposes · author-and-biography · close-textual-reading

another writer often accused of misogyny is Kingsley Amis, and I read his novel _The Russian Girl_ at about the same time I read "The Ziggurat". It struck me that the portrayal of the wives in the two stories was striking: both are portrayed as quite thoroughly vicious.

(The Amis thing is complicated by reading about Amis' feelings about his second wife, writer Elizabeth Jane Howard, though.)

2000-01-28 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Amis, though not sexist in thinking women inferior, was misogynistic in actively disliking how women see and act, and that Wolfe does not share this attitude. Grounds this in Wolfe's apparently better relations with women and the view that men and women perceive things differently, ending with a John 8:32 quotation.
proposes · theories: Amis was misogynistic rather than sexist; Wolfe does not share Amis's misogyny

Ah, but it's always been my opinion (and I'm fairly sure I've read somewhere an essay by someone agreeing with it) that Amis, while falsely accused of "sexism," could be quite rightly accused of misogyny. Amis didn't think women were -inferior-, he just didn't seem to much like them (not in the sense of being homosexual, just in the sense of actively disliking the way he thought women saw the world and acted in it).

I don't think this is true of Wolfe (who seems to have gotten on with the women in his life somewhat better than Amis, as well), but both share the sometimes controversial perception that women and men, in general, have some very different ways of seeing and doing things--and not in a trite, "Mars and Venus," sense of this either.
