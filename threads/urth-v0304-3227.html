Re Moorcock

Urth list · 4 messages · 2003-04-18 to 2003-04-18

2003urthAllan LloydDan'l Danehy-OakesChris PAndy Robertson
The thread: The thread began as a debate about Heinlein's female and father-figure protagonists, prompted by a remark comparing Heinlein and Moorcock, and drifted into California and Letters from Hollywood. Danehy-Oakes disputed the wish-fulfilment view of Heinlein's protagonists, while Lloyd argued that his later novels repeatedly feature an idealized authorial self-image. Chris raised the recurrence of rape by protagonists in Wolfe's novels, stressing it is not gratuitous. Robertson contrasted Wolfe, who openly displays his values, with Moorcock, who hides them in plots, without offering textual evidence. The thread ended without agreement, largely off-topic from Wolfe.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Heinlein's female viewpoint characters contradict the wish-fulfilment self-projection view
  • Allan Lloyd: Heinlein's later novels feature an idealized authorial father-figure, psychologically revealing
  • Chris: rape by protagonists recurs in Wolfe's novels and is uncomfortable, though not gratuitous
  • Andy Robertson: Wolfe openly displays his values while Moorcock hides his in plots

Themes: Women, sex and gender, Fathers, mothers and children, Suffering and sacrifice

2003-04-18 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Heinlein's female point-of-view characters in later novels do not fit the received view that all his protagonists are wish-fulfilment self-projections. Responds to Blattid's remark about Heinlein and Moorcock by citing Job, Friday and To Sail Beyond the Sunset, and by mocking Blattid's comments on Moorcock's California.
disputes

You mean like the religious bigot in JOB, or the female narrators in FRIDAY and TO SAIL BEYOND THE SUNSET (and even chunks of "THE NUMBER OF THE BEAST--"? I'm just sayin', is all ...

It's the received view that all Heinlein's protagonists are wish-fulfillment projections of himself, and especially in his later years. If so, he wished to be a lot of different things -- which is quite possible, of course. Now, mind you, I think his female PoV characters are generally pretty badly realized, and especially Friday. That isn't the point; the point is that they _aren't_ what the received view says "all Heinlein characters" are and must be.

Much as, I suppose, it's psychologically revealing that Moorcock uses rape to redeem his female protagonists at least twice. No offense, man, but what's grease for the goose, you see.

Parts of it, yeah. Moorcock seems to have stuck pretty much to the coastal areas, between LA and San Fran. His take on San Francisco as a city that will eventually preserve itself entirely in lucite as a monument to itself is so dead-smack-on that I was howling with laughter (and then had to read it to my SF native wife, who thought it wasn't funny at all). There's a fair amount going on along Route 101, but California is a mighty big place -- about the size of Iraq, in fact. Driving from LA to San Fran is about like driving from London to Manchester, in terms of time.

--Blattid

2003-04-18 Chris P #

Argues that rape by protagonists or major characters recurs often enough in Wolfe's novels to be uncomfortable, while stressing it is not gratuitous or wish fulfillment. Responds to a remark comparing Moorcock's use of rape to redeem female protagonists, and speculates the short stories contain less of it.
disputes · genre-and-craft

This reminds me (in a way only loosely connected with what you've said) of something that I find a little disturbing in Wolfe's novels- the frequency of rape committed by his protagonists or major characters. Which is NOT, I must stress, to say that I think it's some kind of wish fulfillment or fetish on his part, it's not presented that way at all... it's not gratuitous, and when it does appear it tends to be a major thematic element, etc.

I guess what I'm saying is that the frequency with which the "Rape and Forgiveness" theme comes up makes me a little morbidly uncomfortable.

Of course I have not read any of his short stories yet, and I imagine it's nowhere near as prevalent there.

2003-04-18 Andy Robertson #

Argues that Wolfe, unlike Moorcock, openly displays his values in his fiction, while Moorcock hides his values in plots and would be undone by stating them. Replies to an unnamed earlier post that complained about frequent rape by Wolfe's protagonists, offering no textual evidence and only a general contrast between the two authors.
proposes · theories: Wolfe openly displays his values, Moorcock hides his

Moorcock is an intelligent but frightening, writer, who hides his values in most of his works, but spells out his plots.

Wolfe is an intelligent but frightening writer who does not hide anything of his values: just conceals details of plot.,

Moorcock hides his values because if he really **spoke** about what he believes, he would have to realise that it is pure poison.

That realisation would cramp his style.

Wolfe displays his values in the desperate hope of healing the world.

hartshorn

j manx
