1998-05-24 adam louis stephanides #

Proposes that the bowling balls in Wolfe's story "Cues" are devils or similar beings, and that the old man's arrangement resembles selling one's soul. Grounds this in the ball's evasiveness about its origins, its lie about having a sense of humor, and a slip about goblins, then asks why the balls see each other as a young world and why the pronoun shifts from it to he.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Bowling balls in Cues are devils

Quite aside from my own contributions, I enjoyed the discussion of "Suzanne Delage." It provided some variety from the discussions of a few major works, especially BOTNS (not that I don't enjoy those); and it allowed people to participate who don't have all the details of BOTNS memorized and don't have time to reread it.

In this spirit, I'd like to see what people think about "Cues." I certainly wouldn't rank it with BOTNS, or even "Suzanne Delage"; but it was selected for Wolfe's first collection (by Wolfe?) and I've always found it somewhat puzzling. My current take on it is that the "bowling balls" are devils, or something of that ilk. The not-so-young man's deal is very much like selling his soul. And the "bowling ball" is evasive about its origins, lies (about having a sense of humor) and makes a Freudian slip about "goblins" in the fourth paragraph. But this doesn't explain why the "bowling balls" perceive each other as "a fair young world, mottled by clouds and rich with life." And is there any significance to the fact that in the next-to-last paragraph (the same one in which that description occurs) Wolfe begins to refer to the "bowling balls" as "he" rather than "it"?

Anyway, that's as far as I've gotten. Anyone have any ideas?

--Adam

1998-05-26 Peter Westlake #

Wonders whether Wolfe's story "Cues" is just a shaggy dog story built around its pun or hides more, noting the galaxy-goblin spaceship, the ball's slip "I bed your pardon", and the Coalstack Nebula joke. Raises whether the balls truly have humour or are seen through the narrator's perceptions, and links the two kinds of cue in the story.
questions · close-textual-reading

I've wondered about "Cues" too: on the face of it, it could be a straightforward shaggy dog story, existing just to set up the pun; but there are hints of something more.

The galaxy-goblin spaceship isn't the only slip: right at the end the ball says "I bed your pardon". Unless it's a typo, of course. The "Coalstack Nebula" looks like a real joke by the ball, but I'm not sure why coalstacks (chimneys) would be funnier than coal sacks!

I wonder whether the balls really do have a sense of humour, or whether we are seeing them through the n-s-y man's perceptions? But the ball does seem to know how to answer the cue at the end. That's two kinds of cue in the story: if the balls were smaller, we might get a third in somehow.

Peter.

1998-05-31 adam louis stephanides #

Argues that the bowling ball's line "I bed your pardon" in the story Cues is probably a typo, while noting it may be evasive. Responds to Peter Westlake by pointing to the ending's joke and to how the ball's denial is described as evasive.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: "I bed your pardon" is a typo

I had assumed it's a typo, as it doesn't seem to make any sense as written. But I'm not 100% sure.

In addition to the joke at the end, the way the bowling ball's denial is described strongly implies that it is being evasive.

--Adam
