2006-04-07 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Juturna's knowledge of Severian is inconsistent across their meetings in The Book of the New Sun, since her throne-room meeting precedes the Cephissus encounter where she claims to have saved him from drowning in the Gyoll. Raises the question of when she could have known him earlier, suggesting it must be from a later encounter after the flood.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Juturna's meetings with Severian occur out of order

The meeting in the throne room with Juturna was the third for Sev. The second meeting was at the Cephissus river, when she tried to seduce him. At that time she mentioned having previously saved him from drowning among the clotted weeds in the Gyoll. Ergo, since from her perspective there in the throne room she had yet to save him from drowning in the Gyoll, for her the meeting at the Cephissus cannot have happened yet, either.

Yet she seems to know Sev. If that meeting in the throne room was not her first, when could she have known him earlier, if not from their coming encounter (coming from Sev's point of view) after the flood?

-Roy

2006-04-09 Tristan Davenport replying to nastler #

Argues that the pale, glossy nenuphars signify the dying sun, since the weakened sun fails to stimulate chlorophyll, contrasting them with the green plants of a healthy sun. Grounds this in references to plant paleness throughout the New Sun books and in the nenuphars' metaphorical role in Severian's near-drowning, and asks why Urth's plants are pale while lunar plants seem green.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Pale nenuphars reflect the dying of the sun

There are references throughout the NS books to the paleness of the plants; presumably the dying sun is not bright enough to stimulate chlorophyll production. This is contrasted with the rich green of plants under a healthy sun. So by the very fact of their glossy paleness, the nenuphars reflect the dying of the sun. Then I suppose there's the metaphorical meaning of their role in Sev's near-drowning. (Question: Why are the plants on Urth pale, while those on the moon apparently quite green?)

2006-04-09 Malcolm McGugan replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Asks what the nenuphars (water lilies) in the Gyoll represent and why they would try to drown the New Sun, questioning whether they are non-terrestrial since they are weeds with roots in dark waters opposed by the Undines. Responds to Roy C. Lackey's observation that Juturna's meeting with Severian in the throne room turns his words into prophecy, explaining the drowning-rescue loop as a puzzle in the time sequence.
questions · close-textual-reading · theories: Nenuphars are non-terrestrial weeds opposing the sun

Roy,

I don't think I have understand the problem with this.
he makes his "prophesy" that she will save him from
drowning in Gyoll. She searchs "backwards" through his
life and meets Sev at the sand bank where (among other
things) she mentions that it was (will be) her that
saves him from the weeds. When she reaches the moment
where the nenuphars attempt to drown the NS she saves
him.

Why are these plants the image of "the death of the
sun" anyway? "glossy, pale leaves and azure flower."
"black roots as fine and strong as hair, reaching down
into the dark waters". What forces are represented by
these plants? Why do they wish to drown the sun? Would
they not thrive in a sunnier Ushas? Are they
non-terrestrial? I think they must be. They are
"weeds" after all. They have roots reaching into dark
waters, but are opposed by the Undines. Who can clear
this up?

nastler

2006-04-09 Don Doggett replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Wolfe deliberately sacrifices literal physical consistency, such as the plant colours on Urth and the Moon, for poetic imagery, placing dream and story above physics. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's question about why Urth's plants are pale while the Moon's are green, and ties the tendency to Catholic undertones in Wolfe's major works.
answers · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe prefers poetic imagery over physical consistency

Perhaps, but Wolfe has also shown time and again that he his willing to forsake literal truth and internal consistency to make room for the right poetic imagery. The laws of dream and story far outweigh the laws of physics in his work, imho. Quite in keeping with the Catholic undertones of his major works, I think.

2006-04-09 Jeff Wilson replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the green color of the Moon Lune in The Urth of the New Sun may not come from surviving plants, suggesting algae farms, a ruler's decree, or some other source. Grounds this in the book's recurring theme of conflated identities across ages and points to Wolfe's habit of pairing blue planets with green companions in The Fifth Head of Cerberus and Soldier of Sidon.
proposes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Lune's green is algae farms or decreed paint

Perhaps, but there's no guarantee that there are any plants still on Lune at the time of the narrative. Conflation and multiplication of identities over ages is a very strong theme here as in other Wolfe.

The green color could be something else entirely, and presumed to be the forrests of old, if it ever was. Algae farms meant to feed the many billions of Earth could retain their green color from superficial, topical growth even after millenia of neglect. Or some ancient ruler could have proclaimed the moon be pained green in his honor.

The real answer is of course that the author really likes to write about blue planets with bright green companions, as he does in 5HC and SS.

2006-04-10 David Duffy replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the pale plants of Urth may look green from a distance, so the colour difference with the Moon need not be a rationalizing problem. Supports this by citing the green descriptions and the humid jungle at the waist of the world in The Book of the New Sun, which suggest insolation has not dropped much.
disputes · close-textual-reading

It seems perfectly possible to me that the present plant life can strike one as pale when seen up close, but their underlying green visible en masse from a distance. But only science fiction readers would worry about rationalizing such a cool effect ;) Anyway, there are plenty of descriptions of "green" greenery in BOTNS, plus heat and humidity in the "true jungle at the waist of the world", so insolation hasn't dropped that much.

David Duffy
