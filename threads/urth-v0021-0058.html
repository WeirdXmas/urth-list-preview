Themes: Stories within stories

1998-11-09 Charles Dye #

Reports failing to locate the phrases "soulless warrior" and "lucid yellow" in the text, suggesting they may come from someone else's stories or loosely refer to the Ascian armies and the New Sun. Cites a passage in Sword, chapter XXVIII, where a figure leaps and twists round the columns of the carapace, which the poster links to one of Number Five's dreams in Fifth Head.
answers · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion

mantis challenges:

I have not been able to locate either phrase. "Soulless warrior," if I really wanted to stretch a point, might refer to the Ascian armies, and "lucid yellow" to the forthcoming New Sun. But it feels more natural to me to assume that these are bits from someone else's stories.

Another peculiar snippet is revealed in Chapter XXVIII of "Sword":

"[Again] she leaped, and twisting round the columns of the carapace...."

Which reminds me rather strongly of one of Number Five's dreams in Fifth Head. Curiouser and curiouser.

1998-11-09 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Riffs on a previous claim that the brown book's noyade tale echoes Severian's fear of drowning in The Sword of the Lictor, suggesting chapter 30 may fit better and that the word's modern date hints the brown book tale is from the French Revolution. Builds on the poster's own OED dating of noyade (first cited 1822) and compares the brown book to the Vatic Fountain as an oracle, then challenges readers to find other hidden tidbits.
supports · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Brown book tale is from the French Revolution

Raster,

Re: "by noyade" and chapter 29 of THE SWORD OF THE LICTOR. Say more . . . seems to me that chapter 30 might be closer to "by noyade" (i.e., the fate of Severian's captors after he blows up their boat) but even then I had never thought to make the connection between that scene and the brown book.

Uh, oh-DUH. Looking over chapter 29 I see the word itself--Severian is wondering if they will dispatch him via noyade. THIS is what you are talking about! That =is= a curious touchstone . . . noyade being a technical term we might expect a torturer to know, but the editors/writers of the brown book? (Maybe they had a technical consultant?)

"Noyade" is also interesting by itself because it is more modern (Revolutionary France) than medieval when compared with a lot of the other torturing terms used in the Urth Cycle: OED gives "noyade" at circa 1794, first text citation 1822.

Egad, maybe the brown book tale is one from the French Revolution! (In the same twisted way that "The Tale of the Student and His Son" is from the American War Between the States; and how "The Tale of the Boy Called Frog" relates to the early English colonies in North America.)

But anyway, your point being that the brown book at that wind-in-the-leaves moment acts like an oracle (at least), in the same way that the Vatic Fountain works. So then, your challenge <g> is to find a few of the other tidbits, like:

"soulless warrior!"

and

"lucid yellow"

=mantis=
