The thread: Maa32 argued that the inhumi's moral reform could not be inherited, so their mindless offspring would revert to preying on humans and alternate with intelligent generations, citing the Long Sun inhumi and a human colony on Green. 2001-04-25 Marc Aramini #

Argues that the inhumi's moral reform cannot be inherited, so their mindless offspring would revert to feeding on humans and alternate with intelligent generations. Grounds this in the logic of the Long Sun inhumi and a colony of humans on Green, and rejects the golden-rule explanation for their behaviour.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Inhumi offspring revert to mindless animals each generation

If the inhumi all refuse to feast on human blood since they become "good" from good humans, then their offspring would return to mindless animals. There is nothing to prevent the mindless animals that are the children of good inhumi from sucking off of the people, especially with a colony of humans on Green. They would have no scruples, then their children would be intelligent. It would just be a threat to humans with alternating generations of inhumi. I don't like this golden rule idea.

2001-04-25 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that if inhumi become benevolent through the secret Horn describes, the threat would fall mostly to their mindless offspring, so the alternating-generations problem is reduced. Grants the premise for argument's sake, citing Horn's statement that the inhumi would be no more dangerous than crocodiles, and compares that risk to yearly alligator attacks.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Benevolent inhumi's mindless offspring would be the real danger

A debatable premiss, but consider the result as if it were a given.

Correct so far.

Also correct. But Horn does not say the secret will make the inhumi non-dangerous, only that it will make them no more dangerous than crocodiles.

Intelligent, yes, but (_ex hypothesi_) benevolent. So you would have the "alternating generations" situation you suggest, but the threat would be greatly reduced, as the dangerous inhumi would be the mindless animals.

(As a side note, how many people are _actually_ attacked by alligators and the like in a given year? I presume that proper precautions could make the threat from non-sentient inhumi no greater than that from crocodilians.)

--Blattid

2001-04-25 Kevin J. Maroney #

Replies to Blattid's question about how often humans are attacked by alligators and similar animals, arguing that crocodilian attacks are rare and that such animals are feared far beyond their real danger, much as wolves are. Responds to Blattid's side note on the threat from non-sentient inhumi, which connects the question to the Whorl's dangerous beasts in the Long Sun books.
informs

My understanding is that the number of human attacks from crocodilians is very low. They're much like wolves that way--feared as human predators all out of proportion to the actual danger they present. Which isn't to say that they can't sometimes be very dangerous, indeed.

2001-04-25 Michael Straight #

Asks how many people are actually attacked by inhumi, the non-sentient beasts of The Book of the Long Sun, in a given year and how many of those attacks cause permanent harm. Follows up Kevin J. Maroney's point that crocodile attacks on people are rare and that wolves are feared out of proportion to their danger, and jokingly excludes weddings from the count.
questions

So how many people are _actually_ attacked by inhumi in a given year (not counting weddings)? Of those, how many suffer any permanent harm?

-Rostrum
