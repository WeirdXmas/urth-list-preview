The thread: The thread began as a challenge to Gwern Branwen's claim that Gene Wolfe's 1987 political predictions were mostly wrong and ideologically slanted, with Branwen tallying errors and Allan Anderson suggesting the predictions were meant to provoke. Fans like António Pedro Marques, Fernando Gouvea and Ryan Dunn argued the predictions were half-joking, that fiction should be judged as fiction, and that Wolfe should not be read as a right-wing writer. James B. Jordan defended Wolfe's Catholic, libertarian conservatism, drawing rebuttals from Branwen and David Stockhoff, while Marc Aramini and Stockhoff debated age and self-awareness. The thread then drifted into a long, unresolved argument about whether arson could be an effective terror tactic, with no agreement reached and little engagement with Wolfe's texts.
  • Gwern Branwen: Wolfe's 1987 predictions were mostly wrong and share an ideological slant
  • Ryan Dunn: the predictions piece is a lark and Wolfe should not be read as a right-wing writer
  • David Stockhoff: the predictions succeeded more than half the time and the paranoid style merits study
  • James B. Jordan: Wolfe's Catholic, libertarian, traditional conservative politics are coherent and respectable
  • Marc Aramini: Wolfe's overt politics were early and his later work turns to spiritual concerns
  • António Pedro Marques: the predictions are not as wrong as critics claim and pressed for specific evidence

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Catholic faith and sacraments, War and soldiers

2012-07-27 Gwern Branwen #

Argues that Gene Wolfe's 1987 time capsule predictions for 2012 were mostly wrong, with only a handful correct, and that the errors share an ideological slant. Grounds this in a line-by-line tally of the predictions Wolfe made, noting which held up and which were right for the wrong reasons, such as attributing functional illiteracy to immigration.

What is striking about these is that they are all wrong. Of the dozens of discrete predictions here, there are perhaps 4 which are correct (no major nuclear war, Soviets more like Americans, widespread use of simulations, the rise of China), and a few more which are debatable (rise of genre fiction, SF/Fantasy in TV & movies), and the rest are blatantly incorrect or if correct, correct for the wrong reasons (eg. I haven't looked up whether 50m adults are functionally illiterate in English, but if there are that many, I bet much of that will be due not to the educational system - which continues as it usually has - but due to immigration and other such factors).

It's hard not to see what ideological trend characterizes Wolfe's wrong predictions.

(On the plus side, some of the others made some remarkably prescient predictions: Benford on shale oil and Wolverton on the Men's Right Movement, for example.)

2012-07-28 Fernando Q. Gouvea replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that a reader's concern over an author's political views is misplaced, holding that a story should be judged by whether it works as fiction rather than by its politics. Responds to Gwern Branwen's quoted critique of Gene Wolfe's 1987 time capsule predictions by citing Homer, Virgil, Dante and Milton as works enjoyed despite the poet's politics.
disputes

I'm wondering why you care so much. If I were going to worry about all the kooky ideas I meet in various writers, I'd be left with not very much to read. Do they work as stories? Then good. If not, then they fail as stories, not because of whatever political stance they take. It's hard to see how anyone could enjoy Homer, Virgil, Dante, and Milton if the issue was their politics. (Only Dante comes close to fitting mine in that quartet, and even then not very close.)

Fernando
