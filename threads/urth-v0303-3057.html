Hyacinth Not A Boy

Urth list · 4 messages · 2003-03-12 to 2003-03-13

2003urthThe Book of the Long SunCharles ReedJames WynnJosh Geller
The thread: Josh Geller reported that a WorldCon conversation with Gene Wolfe about the fan theory that Hyacinth is not a boy led Wolfe to quip that he was probably better off not on the list, and Geller treated Wolfe's answer as unambiguous. James Wynn disputed that, arguing Wolfe's other replies were equally cryptic and that every story has two authors, so even text-based readings alter the text. Geller countered that Wolfe's manner and tone made his answer clear, while Charles Reed defended text-evidence readers against charges of laziness, holding that characters' statements should be taken as true unless the text shows otherwise. The exchange ended without resolution, with the authority of Wolfe's remarks left disputed.
  • Josh Geller: Wolfe's answer that Hyacinth is not a boy was unambiguous, given his manner and tone
  • James Wynn: Wolfe's answers are cryptic, so calling them unambiguous is doubtful; text-first readings still shape the text
  • Charles Reed: Characters' statements should be taken as true unless the text shows otherwise; text-evidence readers are not lazy

Themes: Unreliable narration, Books, libraries and writing, Names and their meanings

2003-03-13 James Wynn #

Reports Wolfe's quip at WorldCon that it is probably good he is not on the list, and argues that this answer is not unambiguous, since Wolfe's other replies are equally cryptic. Responds to Josh Geller by citing Crush's argument that even a purely text-evidence reading changes the text, because every story has two authors and language is metaphorical.
disputes · author-and-biography · close-textual-reading · theories: Hyacinth is a male character

When I met Gene Wolfe at the last WorldCon, I mentioned this theory to him, and that there were people who supported it on this mailing list.

His response - which was not in any way ambiguous - was to say "It is probably a good thing that I am not on that mailing list.".

It is importamnt to keep in mind the fact that every story has at least two authors.

Crush responds:
That's an unambiguous answer? Well, maybe for Wolfe it is. Less so, I'd say, than "Urth is green," and you know how far that has gotten us. I've never spoken to Wolfe, but based on his answers to direct questions from this list, I recommend all beware of "unambiguous" answers from that Delphic oracle.

As I said recently, I no longer hold to this theory for purely thematic reasons. However, IF I were **what some consider to be** a purely "text evidence" reader...

(that is, to presume:
o All characters are telling the truth
o No characters are wrong in observations
o All things are as they are presented
o Ignore extra-textual plot-lines regardless of how closely they may seem to follow the story
unless the narrator says otherwise)

...then, I would still hold this theory.

Since every story, as you say, "has two authors", that is true even when the reader decides he will only read into the text the literal meaning of every word. Since no original author can write that way (language and story being, in part, metaphors), to read in such a way changes the text as well.

Keeping this law "in mind", does not deliver the reader from it, but it can fool a reader into thinking he has -- like a man who dreams he has awoken from a dream.

-- Crush

2003-03-13 Charles Reed #

Defends the 'text-evidence' readers against a charge of laziness, arguing that characters' statements should be taken as true unless the text itself shows otherwise. Responds to Crush's broad characterization of text-first readers, insisting that such readers still dig deep and question narrators without relying on outside myth sources.
disputes · close-textual-reading

Just a couple of thoughts about "text-evidence" readers:

A "text evidence" reader would hold no such view if the text indicated that the character were lying or speaking something false out of ignorance.

Same comment. If the text shows that a character is wrong in his observations, then that's what it shows. If there is no textual evidence for a character's observations being wrong, though, then the "text evidence" reader would accept such statements as true. It's a generally positive view of humanity, I suppose, to believe what a character says until proven (proven by the text) otherwise, but I find that such an outlook serves me well.

Now you're verging on slandering those of us who prefer to analyze the text on its own merits first and foremost. We're very much aware that many things are not as they are presented, and that we must dig deep and think hard -- about the text -- to figure out what's really going on behind the "false presentation." Just because we don't rush out to see how Wolfe's work aligns with Robert Graves or with some
Greek/Egyptian/Whatever myth, that doesn't mean that we're lazy readers, or that we don't think deeply about what's going on. It also doesn't mean that we're accepting of every bit of drivel that happens to come from the narrator's pen as God-ordained absolute truth. You're painting with a recklessly broad brush, Crush, and I hope that you're at least mostly kidding.

This is the only accurate "text-first" characterization that you've made.

Charles
