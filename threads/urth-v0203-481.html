2002-03-27 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Proposes that the number seventeen recurs deliberately in The Book of the New Sun, linking the Ascian Group of 17 and the seventeen stones at the Sanguinary Fields to Soviet politburo size and an unlucky Roman number. Grounds this in Graves's I, Claudius, Wolfe's reported comments, and Wolfe's own service in the 17th Infantry in Korea, as recorded in Letters Home.
proposes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Seventeen stones allude to the Group of 17; Group of 17 modeled on Soviet politburo

I believe that 17 was an unlucky number to the Romans, and they got it from the Etruscans. (Robert Graves, I, CLAUDIUS, probably.)

The Group of 17 (Ascian leadership in TBOTNS) is said by Wolfe to be based on the typical number of members in a Soviet politburo, iirc.

The "Seventeen Stones" called out at the Sanguinary Fields (TBOTNS) is very likely a coded allusion to the Group of 17.

At the more personal level, Gene Wolfe was in the first batallion of the 17th Infantry in Korea (LETTERS HOME, as I understand it).

2002-03-27 Jerry Friedman #

Points to a web page on Steven Brust's fondness for the number seventeen and offers a joke that seventeen is the most arbitrary number, from a logic lecturer's habit of using it in examples. Responds to Michael Straight's question about why Wolfe and Brust favour 17, adding an anecdote and a positive remark on Brust's science fiction.
informs

For what looks like the full story on Brust and 17, see
<http://world.std.com/~mam/Cracks-and-Shards/seventeen.html>.

In college, I took a course in mathematical logic from Paul Benacerraf. Whenever he did a numerical example in his lucid and interesting lectures, he would say something like, "Take an arbitrary number-- seventeen, for instance." Finally a student asked him why it was always 17, and he gave a parodic proof that 17 is the most arbitrary number. So it shows up in all kinds of strange places.

By the way, I like Steven Brust's sf a lot, even though it's not much like Wolfe's.

Jerry Friedman

2002-03-27 James Jordan #

Corrects an earlier citation and argues that the number seventeen in the Bible, including the 153 fishes of John 21 as a triangular number, reflects ancient numerology rather than anything Wolfe deliberately intended. Grounds this in counting Psalter divisions, the 7 plus 10 completeness pattern, and doubt that Wolfe drew on Scripture, responding to a posted link on the number's significances.
disputes · myth-and-structure · theology-and-religion · theories: Wolfe's seventeen is 7 plus 10 completeness

Correction to the above: The 17 nations are in Acts 2, not Romans 2. Otherwise, there are a number of occurances of 17 in the Bible, particularly in the Psalter. Books 3 and 4 of the Psalter each have 17 psalms, for instance. But as with the 17 nations, these occurances are not obvious unless you take the time to count or calculate. The 153 fishes of John 21 are the triangular of 17, which for those of you who don't know, is 17+16+15+14...+1. Lay out 17 dots, then below it 16, below that 15, etc., and you get a triangle. That's ancient math, not "modern math"! (666, btw, is the triangular of 36 [=6x6].) Cool, huh?
The number adds 7 and 10, both of which are numbers of completeness or totality in the bible. Added together, they form a symbol of complete completeness.
Who knows where Wolfe "got" it? He might have been thinking of 17 as 7 + 10, as above; or something else entirely. Unless he's a more avid Bible scholar than I think he is, I doubt if anything else from the Bible is in view. (Though I bet he'd really get off on those triangular numbers!)

Patera Nutria
