[urth] Why I don't like TBOTSS

Urth list · 6 messages · 2002-05-25 to 2002-05-28

2002urthThe Book of the Short SunSteve StricklandJerry FriedmanWilliam H. AnsleyAdam StephanidesAndy RobertsonAllan Lloyd
The thread: Allan Lloyd opened by arguing that the Short Sun books, especially The Book of the Short Sun, are contrived: they leave too many questions open, use withholding narrators, and offer speculation without textual support. William Ansley agreed the books are badly built, with mysteries piled on mysteries and an unnecessary ending, and conceded a reader must put in effort but said good reading should pay off. Steve Strickland defended the book as his favorite and said its design is less hard to grasp than critics claim, offering personal endorsement rather than evidence. Adam Stephanides sided with Lloyd, citing Pig's godhood, confirmed by Wolfe, as an arbitrary detail, and contrasting the Short Sun devices with earlier works where they enriched the text. Jerry Friedman said the book could have been like the New Sun but easier, and admitted it demands continual effort. The thread ended withou
  • Allan Lloyd: Short Sun withholds too much and its puzzles are contrived, with speculation lacking textual support
  • William Ansley: Short Sun is poorly constructed with mysteries piled up and an unnecessary ending; good reading should reward effort
  • Steve Strickland: The Book of the Short Sun is his favorite and its design is less hard to grasp than claimed
  • Adam Stephanides: Unexplained details and unreliable narrators serve only as puzzle devices, unlike the richer use in earlier Wolfe
  • Jerry Friedman: The book could have matched the New Sun but been easier to read; it demands continual effort

Themes: Unreliable narration, Mirrors, pictures and art, Stories within stories

2002-05-25 Allan Lloyd #

Argues that the final Short Sun book, The Book of the Short Sun, leaves too many questions unresolved and that Wolfe's recent fiction does not play fair with readers. Cites the narrators who withhold facts, such as the narrator who forgets to explain reasons or veers off into cabbage growing, as contrived puzzle-making; also faults speculative readings lacking textual support.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft

Adam,
Way back in the archives there is a post from me expressing = deep dissatisfaction with the Short Sun books, and because of this, I = too have not been taking much part in the discussion of the books.

My main point was that in the final book, I expected some sort of = resolution to some of the problems and questions that Wolfe had been = setting in the previous two books. Maybe this was unreasonable of me = when dealing with Gene, but I was frustrated with the fact that he just = kept raising more questions without giving enough clues in the text to = solve any of them. (I also find some discussions in this group lately to = be speculating more and more freely in trying to invent solutions that = may fit the facts, but have no textual support.)=20

It could just be me, but I am feeling that in his more recent = works, Wolfe has not been playing fair with his readers. I still think = that he and Crowley are the best writers in the science fiction field, = but I really don't think that his recent work has been up to his own = high standards.

Certain stylistic habits are getting harder to bear. If I read one = more narrator telling me that there are three reasons why he is doing = something, but just happens to get sidetracked before he tells me all of = them, or starts to expain a major incident in the plot, but veers off = with a description of ways of growing cabbages, I may have to be = restrained from tearing pages from books. Too many narrators are = children, or people who don't write very well, or are not very = intelligent, just as an excuse to obscure facts and with-hold = information that would normally be provided in the text. I find this a = contrived and unnatural way of creating puzzles. A problem that arises = through plotting, or genuine lack of knowledge on the part of a = character, seems to me to be a proper part of a story, but having the = narrator forget to tell us is becoming a contrivance.

Is it just me feeling particularly grumpy tonight, or does everyone = else feel that these are justified ways of provoking thought and = reflection on a story?

Allan

2002-05-26 William H. Ansley #

Argues that the Short Sun books are poorly constructed, with mysteries piled on mysteries and an unnecessary Urth of the Old Sun ending, and says the poster does not enjoy them. Responds to Andy Robertson's suggestion that the poster has not put enough effort into reading, conceding the point but saying good reading must bring satisfaction in return.
disputes

I agree with Allan. I didn't care for the Short Sun books and I still don't.

Andy Robertson, in his passage above, suggests I have not put enough effort into reading the books. This may be true. In fact, it is very likely.

But writing is a two way street; I expect to get some kind of return for the effort I put into reading something, some kind of satisfaction. I have gotten that from most of what Gene Wolfe has written, enough, at least, so that I feel that I was part of a fair exchange. (And in many cases, the return I have gotten from reading Wolfe's work was much more than fair.) But I don't feel that way with the Short Sun books. They seem a bit slip shod, between mysteries being piled on mysteries and the seemingly unnecessary shoe-horning of the Urth of the Old Sun into the final volume. It may be that I am totally missing the grand design. But if that's the case I doubt I will ever see it; I am not likely to re-read the series.

Of course, someone on this list could post a revelatory message that makes me see the light, but I haven't been convinced by any attempts so far.
--

William Ansley

2002-05-26 Steve Strickland #

Argues that The Book of the Short Sun is the poster's favorite Wolfe work and that its overall design is less hard to grasp than other posts claim. Responds to Andy Robertson's quoted remark that the books are hard to read and demand much effort, offering a personal endorsement rather than textual evidence.
disputes

TBOTSS is my favorite of all Wolfe's works. Though I can't claim to have solved every mystery the books have to offer, I don't think the "grand design" is nearly as difficult to grasp as some of the posts claim. I can't even begin to describe how rewarding these titles have been for me.

I cast my vote in favor of the SS.

Steve Strickland

Argues that the Short Sun books feel contrived because their unexplained details, unreliable narrators and withheld information serve only as puzzle devices, citing Pig's godhood, confirmed by Wolfe via Nick Gevers, as an arbitrary detail. Yes, this was another of the things that provoked me to articulate my dissatisfaction. Of course, people have done this with all Wolfe's works, but the Short Sun books seem to particularly invite it. I don't think think the problem is the techniques Wolfe uses. Unexplained details, narrators who omit crucial information, pulp-SF elements: Wolfe has used all these before, in many of his best works. In his earlier works they served to deepen and enrich; here, as you say, they come off as contrived and unnatural.
