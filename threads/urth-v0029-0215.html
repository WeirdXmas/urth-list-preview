And When They Appear

Urth list · 4 messages · 2000-09-20 to 2000-09-21

2000urthAlice TurnerAdam StephanidesPeter Westlake
The thread: The thread asked whether Wolfe's 'And When They Appear' is as grim and powerful as Adam Stephanides found it, and whether Sherby's ending is comforting. Adam argued it is grim, the child narration effective, and disputed readings by Tony Ellis and James Jordan, citing Charlie's molestation, the rock thrown at Smoky, and Eddi's statements that the real Yeshua is inside House. Peter Westlake pointed him to Ellis's point about Mouse under Sherby's pillow and to archived essays by Jordan. akt dissented, calling the story disjointed, with Smoky wasted and the Jesus figure gratuitous, and ranked it below The Death of Doctor Island. The exchange ended without resolution, with Adam and akt holding opposed judgments.
  • Adam Stephanides: story is grim and powerful; Sherby's ending is not comforting, citing Charlie's abuse and the rock thrown at Smoky
  • Adam Stephanides: disputes Tony Ellis's freezer point and James Jordan's claim that leaving House lets Sherby meet Yeshua
  • Peter Westlake: points to Tony Ellis's observation that Mouse lies under Sherby's pillow and to Jordan's archived essays
  • akt: story is disjointed and careless, gothic elements fail to cohere, and Smoky is wasted
  • akt: judges it weaker than The Death of Doctor Island, citing the gratuitous Jesus and the gnomic Christmas line

Themes: Suffering and sacrifice, Christ figures and messiahs, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2000-09-20 Adam Stephanides #

Calls Wolfe's 'And When They Appear' one of his grimmest stories, made powerful by matter-of-fact narration and a child narrator. Notes its deceptive Christmas-story opening that resembles Bradbury, compares it to 'The Death of Doctor Island' and invites others' thoughts.
proposes · genre-and-craft

I finally got around to reading STRANGE TRAVELLERS, though I'd read a number of the stories in it before. On a first reading, I'd say the major one I'd missed is "And When They Appear." This is one of Wolfe's grimmest stories (which is saying a lot), made more powerful by the matter-of-fact narration and by the deceptive "Christmas story" atmosphere of the first half. (It just struck me that this first half reads a lot like a Bradbury pastiche.) It's also one of the most effective uses of a child narrator by anyone that I've read. In some ways the story is like a reprise of "The Death of Doctor Island," with a boy protagonist in an enclosed setting controlled by an AI, but the new story stands on its own.

Anybody else have any thoughts on it? (Forgive me if I've been redundant; I don't recall anything being said about it during the STRANGE TRAVELLERS discussion, but I can't download that volume of the archives onto my computer.)

--Adam

2000-09-20 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that Sherby's situation at the end of "And When They Appear" is not comforting, citing his molestation by Charlie and the rock thrown at Smoky as the worst moment. Also disputes Tony Ellis's freezer point and James Jordan's claim that leaving House lets Sherby meet Yeshua, grounding these in passages where Eddi says Sherby cannot open the door and that the real Yeshua is inside House.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Sherby's refusal of House's advice saved his life

I'm not comforted, since he's already been molested (Charlie tells him "'We'll take off our clothes and have some fun, Chris. _Then_ we'll go to bed.'" (195, emphasis mine)) and there's no indication that he knows the blade is there (the owl-slicing is "the first time [the blade] had been used since Mouse had been made" (170) and Sherby doesn't see it). Besides, the most horrific moment is not the molestation, but Sherby's throwing a rock at Smoky.

Thanks for pointing this out; I'd forgotten about it. Now that I've read the story, I have a couple of comments.

On Tony Ellis's point about hiding in the freezer: yeah, that had niggled at me. We're told explicitly that Sherby can't open the door by himself (170); Eddi says that House will open the door when it's safe, but if House is destroyed, how can it (of course, the AI core might survive, but it might not, and apparently it doesn't)? And even Eddi (House) thinks there's a good chance of Sherby freezing to death before being rescued: the best he can say is "If you snuggle up with Smoky, you might stay alive until they leave." (188) It looks like Sherby's refusal to take House's advice saved his life, although it wasn't based on any rational calculation.

On Jim Jordan's point that by leaving House, Sherby will be able to meet Jesus (or Yeshua, as he's called in the story): but according to Eddi, the real Yeshua is in House too (185).

--Adam

2000-09-21 Alice Turner #

Argues that Wolfe's story And When They Appear is disjointed and careless, its gothic elements failing to cohere, and that Smoky is wasted. Responds to Adam's judgment that it is weaker than TDoDI, citing the gratuitous Jesus, the angry mob, Corporal Charlie's treatment of Sherby and the closing gnomic line about Christmas.
supports · close-textual-reading

From Adam:

I don't think it's a quarter as good as TDoDI. It's too disjointed; the gothic elements don't come together. Mouse and Owl, Smoky, the parents in the freezer, Corporal Charlie, the folklore figures, the gratuitous Jesus, the angry mob--why?. Even the gnomic sentence at the end: "'I don't like it,' he said [regarding Christmas], but as soon as he said it, he knew it was not true." This is, we are led to believe, is after Corporal Charlie has had his way with Sherby. Messy stories irritate me. From Bradbury too, who, in later work, epitomizes mess.

Me too, as it happens. But more annoying is that Smoky is never used properly in the story. He and Cpl. Charlie are the only "real" figures, and they're virtually thrown away.

Given what I feel to be the general carelessness throughout, I'd say yes.

-alga
