Auk's fate?

Urth list · 12 messages · 2007-03-29 to 2007-04-04

2007urthThe Book of the Long SunFredthalassocratCliff JudgeMatthew KingJosh GellerGwern BranwenChris PMatthew GrovesCharles ReedJames Jordan
The thread: Fred asked what became of Auk, a minor figure whom Horn invokes in Return to the Whorl. Transentient read the line as Horn kicking Jahlee the way Auk would kick a prostitute, while Matthew Groves recalled Auk's guilt over kicking Chenille and Charles Reed recalled Auk and Chenille surviving on Green. Thalassocrat speculated that Auk reprogrammed a lander and was sent to build a new Mainframe. The thread then drifted to the novella Memorare, where James B. Jordan and Gwern Branwen argued the god-playing dead man is a Wolfe self-portrait, and Matthew King called that closed circle too neat. It ended without resolution, and a later news link repeated an earlier post.
  • Charles Reed: recalls Auk and Chenille surviving on Green for years, without textual support
  • thalassocrat: Auk reprogrammed his lander under inhumi control and was sent to build a new Mainframe
  • Transentient: Horn's line means kicking Jahlee as Auk would kick a prostitute, a plain reading
  • Matthew Groves: Auk is a sympathetic foil to Silk, tormented by guilt, with his fate left unstated
  • James B. Jordan: the godplaying dead man in Memorare is Wolfe's self-portrait, a warning about tyranny
  • Gwern Branwen: the godplaying dead man is Wolfe himself, holding author-like power over characters

Themes: Suffering and sacrifice, Power, rulers and politics, Free will and choice

2007-03-31 Matthew Groves replying to Fred #

Recalls that in The Book of the Long Sun Auk confesses to Silk that he struck and kicked Chenille after she spurned his gift, and remains tormented by guilt even after Jahlee's death. Frames Auk as a sympathetic foil to Silk, noting that the text gives little information about his eventual fate.
informs · close-textual-reading

It's been a little while since I read the books, but in The Book of the Long Sun, (Horn and Nettle write that) Patera Silk shrives Auk, and the content of his shriving is, among other things, that he struck and kicked Chenille when she spurned his gift (I forget what the gift was). I seem to recall he had already been shriven for this by Silk's predecessor, Patera Pike, but his guilt is so great that he confesses again to Silk. Anyway, after Silk/Horn kills Jahlee, he also is unable to forgive himself. I suppose the gift Jahlee spurns is Silk/Horn's forgiveness. He invites her into his family in spite of the attack that nearly killed Sinew, and she tries to kill Horn's wife.

I like Auk too, especially in that pair of scenes surrounding Silk's caper at Blood's villa -- his shriving beforehand and his laughter at Silk's adventure afterward. He's a great foil for Silk. As for what happened to him, we're just not given that much information.

2007-04-02 thalassocrat replying to Fred #

Speculates that Auk, whose earlier kick killed a man, reprogrammed his lander to fly between Green and Blue under inhumi control, and that Pas sent him to build a new Mainframe. Responds to Fred's question about Horn's line "I kicked her like Auk" in Return to the Whorl by working through what the cellar meeting with Chenille achieved.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Auk reprogrammed his lander under inhumi control; Pas gave Auk insight to build a new Mainframe; Inhumi capturing Auk served a valuable purpose; SilkHorn's reconciliation with Chenille resolved the hostage crisis

On Fri, 30 Mar 2007 07:09:35 +1000 Fred <[email]>

Somewhere early on in Long Sun, we learn that Auk once kicked a man to death.

It is very frustrating. SilkHorn meets Chenille (and Auk? can't rememebr) in the cellar of Sinew's house on green, but we learn absolutely nothing about what they said to each other.

However I think it's pretty easy to guess what happened afterwards. This meeting happens shortly after SilkHorn and his little army of astral-travelling mercenaries have cleared the old city on green of inhumi. These inhumi were holding A & C's children hostage. SilkHorn would have reconciled Sinew's villagers with Chenille, and she & her colleagues would have gone back to the city to claim back her children, and to claim the city for humans, guarding it against the return of inhumi. This would have fulfilled the hope of the Neighbor when he set Horn the task of clearing the sewer, that by doing so the sewer might be cleared forever.

I think you can guess other things about Auk. It must have been Auk with his preternatural mechanical insight who reprogrammed his lander so that it would fly between Green & Blue under inhumi control.

Less certainly, I feel pretty sure that Pas gave Auk this insight and sent him from the Whorl just in order that Auk would build a new Mainframe for Pas & the others to dwell in & rule from. In a sense the inhumi performed a valuable service in taking Auk prisoner before he could do this.

2007-04-04 James Jordan replying to thalassocrat #

Argues that Wolfe casts himself as the god-playing dead man in the novella "Memorare", making the statue a self-portrait of potential tyranny rather than a figure like Nixon or Hitler. Grounds this in the prayers and title, which he reads as signalling salvation by grace, and asks whether the people sent back to earth stand for Wolfe's readers.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Wolfe makes himself the god-playing dead man; The people sent back are Wolfe's readers

I've been away so much that I may have missed it, but have we discussed "Memorare" here yet?

Spoiler space

I was struck by the fact that Wolfe makes himself the godplaying dead man. It would be easy to have the statue be that of "Richard Nixon" or "Hitler" or someone else, but Wolfe takes upon himself the role of god-playing tyrant. He seems to be saying that he's as potentially evil as anyone else. Salvation is only by grace, as the prayers and title indicate.
Which raised to me another question/dimension of the story: the persons sent back to earth by the Wolfegod. Are these the readers of the story? Is Wolfe suggesting that his own readers move back into the world to do good (he hopes), but that any anti-Wolfe would also be sending readers back into the world for evil purposes? Anyway, I thought this was excellent vintage-Wolfe novella, multidimensional and fully textured. Anybody want to talk about it?

2007-04-04 James Jordan replying to Matthew King #

Argues that the statue in "Memorare" resembles Wolfe himself, so the god-playing dead man may be a Wolfe self-portrait, though this is a minor device rather than essential. Reads the fake deadly heaven as an allegory for socialism, fascism and anti-liberty tyranny, linking Wolfe's theme of freedom to realism and to prayer and grace. Responds to James B. Jordan's earlier post on Wolfe as the godplaying dead man, and notes the narrator's remark that identifying the dead man will be hard.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · author-and-biography · theories: The statue's dead man is Wolfe himself; The fake heaven symbolizes socialism and fascism

I guess one would put the Wolfe identification into the category of a mcguffin (?), something we don't have to know to get the story. But the statue looks just like Wolfe, and Wolfe has appeared in other stories by describing himself. Also, the main character (I don't have the story before me) says that identifying the dead man will be difficult for him -- being a character, how can he identify the author? I have not yet reread the story to see if there be other clues. Setting aside the Wolfe identification, I think we can see that the "heaven" that turns out to be fake and deadly is a symbol for socialism, fascism, and all the other anti-liberty things that Wolfe is against. One is told that everything is fine, and one keeps believing everything is fine, but the reality is that things are not fine at all. It is all one man, one dictator, controlling it all, including controlling the mind. The comments in the story about America no longer being a free country fit with this theme. Freedom as Wolfe's large thematic interest is, in this story, linked with being realistic about life. Not living in a fantasy world (!). And it would seem that prayer and grace are ways of becoming realistic and being disabused of fantasy and fakery. Just some more observations.

2007-04-04 Gwern Branwen replying to James B. Jordan #

Proposes that the godplaying dead man in the story "Memorare" is really Wolfe himself, since the author holds god-like power over characters who cannot have a theodicy. Builds the case on the story's own framing: the dead man is a figure who will be read long after death and whose evil acts are fully purposed, as with any author.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · author-and-biography · theories: The godplaying dead man is Wolfe himself

To be facetious for a moment, doesn't it make perfect sense for the godplaying dead man to be Wolfe, the author of the story? After all, the story will be read long after he's dead; the author has the power of a god (sort of); and there is no acceptable theodicy for the inhabitants of a story - any evil done to them is fully purposed, thought through, and carried out by the author.
