Julian Jaynes' Bicameral Brain

The thread: Ye Olde Mothman asked whether Julian Jaynes's bicameral-mind theory had been applied to Soldier of the Mist, since Latro hallucinates gods who seem to be internal voices. 1998-10-28 Ye Olde Mothman #

Asks whether Julian Jaynes's bicameral-mind theory has been discussed in relation to Soldier of the Mist, since the protagonist hallucinates gods and lacks self-consciousness. Grounds the question in Jaynes's claim that pre-500 B.C. internal voices were attributed to gods, citing the Iliad's speaking gods heard only by heroes.
questions · genre-and-craft

Has Julian Jaynes' theory expressed in "The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind" ever been discused in relation to Wolfe's "Soldier of the Mist?" If I read it right, Jaynes beleives that self consciousness didn't develop until about 500 B.C. and that any internal voices, motivations, or initiative, prior to this thinking shift, would have been attributed to Gods -- that in the Iliad, when the gods speak they are only heard by the heroes they are speaking too -- this is what makes humans act. Sounds like the situation the character in Wolfe's book finds himself -- hallucinating gods, being at their mercy, no real concept of having a say in what's going on around him. Maybe not...

1998-10-29 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Wolfe's Soldier of the Mist may draw on Julian Jaynes's bicameral mind theory, but contends that Latro already possesses a developed self-consciousness. Supports this by pointing to Latro's desire to return home, which the gods exploit as a lever, implying a self must exist for the gods to manipulate.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Latro has developed self-consciousness despite bicameral theory

I don't think we've discussed this, but it sounds as if Wolfe might have had it in mind. On the other hand, I think Latro actually has a fairly developed self-consciousness (at least of the same peculiar kind as Severian, his mnemonic opposite, has)--it seems that there IS a line between the gods and the self to be found. For example, his desire to get home seems to be a tool the gods use to manipulate him--hardly possible if he had no self to dangel a carrot in front of.

1998-10-29 Michael Straight #

Agrees with Mothman's hypothesis that Latro's longing to go home appears as Hestia's repeated urgings, and notes Wolfe uses three obscure names for her alongside an invented one. Admits never having read Jaynes's book, then compares Jaynes's theory with C.S. Lewis's claim that ancient peoples did not separate physical from spiritual, as in worship and liturgy.
supports · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Latro's desire manifests as Hestia's visits; Wolfe varies Hestia's names deliberately; Jaynes's bicameral mind explains Latro's gods

Exactly. Following Mothman's hypothesis, one would expect Latro's desire to manifest as regular visits from Hestia urging him to search out his kindred and get home. Except that Wolfe would find three other more obscure names for her and use them interchangably with one he made up.

I've heard of Jaynes' theory before and find it intriguing, but never read the book to see how (she?/he?) defends it. Thanks for reminding me of the book, I'd like to check it out.

It reminds me of C.S. Lewis's argument that ancient peoples did not make the same distinctions we make between physical and spiritual; that there didn't need to be different words for spirit and wind because they were the same thing; that no one bothered to ask to what extent "seated at the right hand of God" was literal or figurative; that no one would think to ask, "Am I really participating in this liturgy, or am I just going through the motions? Am I really worshiping or just dancing?"

-Rostrum
