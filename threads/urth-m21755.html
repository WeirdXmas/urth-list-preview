Horn's ability

Urth list · 28 messages · 2011-10-26 to 2011-10-28

2011urthThe Book of the New SunJerry FriedmanLee BermanGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffJames WynnMarc AraminiDan'l Danehy-OakesAndrew MasonDaniel Petersen
The thread: Readers debated whether Horn's dream-like abilities are meant to remain mysterious and what the name Hethor signals, moving from the Neighbors' limbs and sword to etymology (Hector, Heather, Hathor), occult sources, and Gnostic angels. Lee Berman and Gerry Quinn repeatedly clashed over Father Inire, with Berman arguing he is the true ruler and the boatman, and Quinn insisting he is a single-identity cacogen. Positions were held by many posters with no real resolution; the thread ended in banter and continued arguing rather than agreement.
  • Lee Berman: Hethor may carry a demonic and saintly dual meaning, is likely named Heather, and Father Inire is the true ruler and possibly the boatman
  • Gerry Quinn: Inire is a cacogen who must stay where the story places him, and name-based theories about Hethor are weak
  • David Stockhoff: Hethor reads as a gnostic archon, possibly possessed, and is named Heather for gray-purple associations
  • James Wynn: Hethor was chosen for a Gnostic fallen-star angel and a Hector or Hathor link
  • Daniel Petersen: Wolfe leaves the quasi-dream nature of Horn's ability deliberately mysterious, aiming at enlightenment
  • Jerry Friedman: Inire has clear narrative functions and may act through agents like Rudesind

Themes: Names and their meanings, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

Here's the deal. The five books of Severian, and the four books of Silk (and perhaps the Book of the Short Sun although Wolfe veers into very Orthodox territory there, I think) are so ingrained with Gnosticism that it would be difficult to refute the assertion that these books are to Gnosticism what the Chronicles of Narnia are to Protestant orthodoxy. I'm not saying I'm comfortable with that argument, but it would be very difficult to refute it.

Admittedly, it's possible to make too much of it. However, I think it is a mistake to assume that the saint names were chose haphazardly for the characters. "star", that is a "fallen star", that is a "fallen angel" ). And it would be very like Wolfe to craft a character that refers to both of those etymologies...as well as others such as "Hector" or the English primrose variety known as the "Hethor Giant".

The word "inhuma" references a species of bird, Dionysus, vampires, and monstrosity--and all that is worked into the narrative. Wolfe is just very, very clever. And he invites us to be very clever too.
