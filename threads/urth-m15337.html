Proper Use of Subject Lines

Urth list · 33 messages · 2010-05-21 to 2010-05-24

2010urthThe Book of the New SunLane HaygoodJohn WatkinsJosh GellerCraig BrewerDavid StockhoffJames WynnFred KiescheJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-OakesRob Thornton
The thread: The thread began as a request that list subject lines accurately describe posts so readers can skip unwanted messages, with David Stockhoff supporting the idea and Jeff Wilson and James Wynn doubting that people would keep to it without enforcement. Jeff Wilson then proposed moderator-enforced discipline, which Dan'l Danehy-Oakes reframed as voluntary self-discipline; Wynn objected to self-appointed enforcers, and Lane Haygood and brunians debated whether voluntary enforcement is coherent. The exchange never reached Wolfe's fiction and drifted into meta-argument about list administration and definitions, ending without agreement and with the original subject line largely forgotten.
  • David Stockhoff: subject lines should accurately describe posts so readers can skip unwanted ones
  • Jeff Wilson: keeping subject lines accurate is unrealistic without moderator enforcement
  • James Wynn: retitling drifting threads only partly works, so ignoring them is better; objects to self-appointed enforcers
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: meant voluntary self-discipline by members, not universal policing
  • Lane Haygood: voluntary enforcement is coherent because penalties attach only to non-compliance
  • brunians: voluntary enforcement is a contradiction since voluntary means freely chosen

2010-05-21 James Wynn replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that changing subject lines as threads drift off topic only partly works, since many participants do not follow the change. Reports the poster's own experience of trying to rename threads and concludes that simply ignoring a drifted discussion mostly solves the problem.
informs

I have, in the past, attempted to change the subject lines of threads as they moved OT. Maybe half or less of the participants followed. If the particular email that prompted them to responded did not have a changed subject, they didn't change it. Probably, because they didn't care about it as much as me or Mr. Brewer. Or, in the respondents opinion, their response *did* have to do with the original subject line. Hopeless cause.

On the upside, it is generally easy to tell when a discussion's wagon train has moved on. If I've lost interest, I stop following it entirely. With that method --although it's not neat and orderly, the problem is mostly solved.

J.

2010-05-24 Lane Haygood replying to brunians #

Argues that voluntary enforcement is coherent because enforcement only attaches a penalty to non-compliance, so one remains free to disobey while bearing consequences. Replies to brunians' claim that voluntarily enforced acts are a contradiction, using the law, good manners and Kant's universal maxim as examples; the post does not discuss Wolfe's fiction.
disputes

Something is enforced if there is a penalty for failure to comply. You are still free (in terms of will and capacity) to not comply; you must merely suffer the consequences.

Let me put it another way: you are free to disobey the law, id est, to commit a crime. You must, however, "do the time" if you have "done the crime," as the cliche goes.

You may voluntarily comport yourself with dignity and class. You may of course also fail to do so and be a boor. That is your prerogative, and none shall gainsay you your boorishness, for while it may be against the mores of good taste, one may not enforce good manners.

One voluntarily enforces, therefore, a sense of good character in how one comports onesself if one self-legislates a moraltiy consistent with civility, pace Kant. You must act at all times as if the maxim of your actions could be willed as universal law. Therefore, though you may not be subject to compulsion to play nicely, it is eminently rational and proper for you to compel yourself to do so, should some base nature suggest otherwise.

yvt,
Lane

to commit a crime.  You must, however, "do the time" if you have "done
the crime," as the cliche goes.

You may voluntarily comport yourself with dignity and class.  You may
of course also fail to do so and be a boor.  That is your prerogative,
and none shall gainsay you your boorishness, for while it may be
against the mores of good taste, one may not enforce good manners.

One voluntarily enforces, therefore, a sense of good character in how
one comports onesself if one self-legislates a moraltiy consistent
with civility, pace Kant.  You must act at all times as if the maxim
of your actions could be willed as universal law.  Therefore, though
you may not be subject to compulsion to play nicely, it is eminently
rational and proper for you to compel yourself to do so, should some
base nature suggest otherwise.

> Something is enforced if there is a penalty for failure to comply.
> You are still free (in terms of will and capacity) to not comply; you
> must merely suffer the consequences.
>
> Let me put it another way:  you are free to disobey the law, id est,
> to commit a crime.  You must, however, "do the time" if you have "done
> the crime," as the cliche goes.
>
> You may voluntarily comport yourself with dignity and class.  You may
> of course also fail to do so and be a boor.  That is your prerogative,
> and none shall gainsay you your boorishness, for while it may be
> against the mores of good taste, one may not enforce good manners.
>
> One voluntarily enforces, therefore, a sense of good character in how
> one comports onesself if one self-legislates a moraltiy consistent
> with civility, pace Kant.  You must act at all times as if the maxim
> of your actions could be willed as universal law.  Therefore, though
> you may not be subject to compulsion to play nicely, it is eminently
> rational and proper for you to compel yourself to do so, should some
> base nature suggest otherwise.
>
> yvt,
> Lane
