1997-07-01 David Langford #

Supplies Eric Partridge and 1811 Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue definitions for Long Sun cant terms abram, kate, large, nanny and nipper, answering Mantis's request for help with words missing from the OED. Then asks whether the communicative pet Tick the catachrest echoes the crest cat Tick-Tock in James H. Schmitz's story Novice.

Here's some stuff from Eric Partridge's =A Dictionary of Historical Slang= (abridged 1972), with addenda from =1811 Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue: A Dictionary of Buckish Slang, University Wit, and Pickpocket Eloquence= or [1811] for short ... the latter was reprinted and extensively remaindered in the 1970s and 80s.

A malingerer: C.19-20 nautical; ob. 2, As adj., c.: mad, C.16-17, C.17-18 ... // abram, v. To feign sickness: ?ca 1840-90 ... // Abra(ha)m-cove or -man. A pseudo-madman seeking alms; a genuine lunatic allowed on certain days to leave Bethlehem Hospital ... ca 1550-1700. 2. Also, in late C.18-19, a mendicant pretending to be an old naval rating cast on the streets. // [1811] gives "naked" as primary meaning -- also allowed by Partridge -- and agrees on Abram Men, pretended mad men.

[1811] Money in general. To polish the king's iron with one's eyebrows; to look out of grated or prison windows ... Iron doublet; a prison.

A master or skeleton key: c. of late C.17-mid-19. cf JENNY; JIMMY: esp. BETTY. 2. Hence, a picklock: C18-mid-19. 3. (Also katy, Katy.) A wanton: (mainly Scots) coll: C.16-early 19. // [1811] agrees: A picklock (cant).

large, adj. gen. used as adv. Excessively: (low) coll: from ca 1850. Thus, dress large, i.e. showily; go large, i.e. noisily; play large, i.e. for high stakes; i.e. boastfully. cf. FINE AND LARGE.

nanny. A whore: late C.17-C-19: coll. Ex Nanny, the female name. Mostly in combination: see, e.g., NANNY-HOUSE. // nipper. A thief, esp. a cut-purse or a pickpocket: c.: ca. 1580-1830. // nipper, v. To catch; to arrest: ca. 1820-1850. // [1811] confirms: Nanny House. A brothel. // Nip. A cheat. Bung nipper; a cutpurse.

By the way, no one on this learned list will have missed the upfront joke that Tick the catachrest speaks in catachresis ... but is there an echo there, too, of another communicative pet in James H. Schmitz's well-loved sf story "Novice" (incorporated into =The Universe Against Her=, 1964): a "crest cat" called Tick-Tock?

Dave

1997-07-01 Derek Bell #

Proposes that the word "shaggy" in Wolfe's text may carry an Irish slang sense close to an obscenity, not just its meaning of matted hair. Asks whether this usage is common outside Ireland and whether Wolfe intended it, noting the word suits the contexts where it appears.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: "Shaggy" carries an Irish obscene slang sense in Wolfe

Strong language ahead!

One cant term that's intruiged me is "shaggy". Apart from meaning matted, in Ireland it's slang that's means something akin to "fuck". I'm not sure how common this meaning is outside Ireland or if Wolfe intended it in this manner - it's certainly an appropriate meaning for the places it's used.
