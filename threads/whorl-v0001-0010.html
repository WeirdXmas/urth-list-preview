1996whorlThe Book of the Long SunPeter CashJames Jordan
The thread: Peter Cash asked the list what happened on the airship in The Book of the Long Sun, specifically whether Mucor possessed Horn or she fell by herself, and whether Silk meant to jump, saying the scene is menacing but unclear. Cash then argued that Quetzal is ambiguous rather than simply good, linking him to Quetzalcoatl arriving as Cortez, and that Silk's calling was to save his people from sin, not to lead them to new worlds. Jim Jordan replied that Silk is a Christ-like figure whose pursuit of Hyacinth echoes the Good Shepherd parable, and that Hyacinth is not meant to be liked. The thread shows two readings of Silk's character and Quetzal, with no visible resolution; the airship question went unanswered in these posts.
  • Peter Cash: Quetzal is ambiguous, and Silk's calling was to save his people from sin
  • Peter Cash: asks for help understanding whether Mucor possessed Horn or she fell, and whether Silk meant to jump
  • Jim Jordan: Silk is a Christ-like figure whose pursuit of Hyacinth reflects the Good Shepherd parable
  • Jim Jordan: Hyacinth is not meant to be liked

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Suffering and sacrifice, Catholic faith and sacraments

is really too good to be true.

It may be that Silk understands all this, and loves her anyway (he makes some
remarks to this effect after discussing Hyacinth's escapades with the Trivigaunty

1996-12-06 Peter Cash #

Asks the list to explain what happened on top of the airship in The Book of the Long Sun, specifically whether Mucor possessed Horn and pushed her off or whether Horn fell on her own, and whether Silk meant to jump. Says the scene builds a sense of menace without making clear the cause or the event itself, and requests help understanding it.
questions · close-textual-reading

I really don't feel that I completely understand what happened on top of the airship. Why did Mucor possess Horn (that _is_ what happened, right?)? Did Mucor "push" Horn off, or did she have nothing to do with it? Was Silk going to jump?

Throughout the scene on top of the airship, I get a feeling of building menace. You _know_ something bad is going to happen, but it's not clear why--and when it happens, it's not clear what happened.

Can anybody help me understand this better?

Peter

1996-12-18 James Jordan #

Argues that Silk in The Book of the Long Sun is a Christ-like figure whose pursuit of Hyacinth reflects the parable of the Good Shepherd seeking the lost sheep, and that Hyacinth is not meant to be liked. Responds to Peter Cash's reading by citing Silk's burial and soul-journey, Wolfe's remark that Jesus fed people, and the offer of parental visits Silk may have accepted.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Silk is a Christ figure in the Long Sun books; Silk's pursuit of Hyacinth is the Good Shepherd parable

Point taken.

Again, good point.

That would not seem to work with "Fifth Head," which depends on a contrast between shape-shifters, true humans, and clones.
My own thought would be that IF there be any connection, it is just that in the Urth universe there are also a pair of planets corresponding to those in "Fifth Head," but which were not settled as in "Fifth Head." I imagine the next book in the series will settle this one for us.

snip

Probably more of Wolfe's ambiguity and myth-mixing. In the Severian Quintet, Severian is both a Christ (=Christian) figure and an Apollo figure.

Chapter 9. Hardcover pp. 321ff. He is covered by dirt (dead and buried), and his soul departs to visit his mothers and fathers (both physical and adopted). It is clear from the ensuing descriptions that Silk was covered for quite some time, and considered dead.

You may be right, but I don't think so at this point. I asked Wolfe about salvation once, and he commented that Jesus fed people, and that people need food and a place to live before they can do much else. Thus, Severian "saves" Urth by providing a new sun, and Silk "saves" his church by getting them out of the ship and to a planet. The Increate/Outsider wants this salvation as well, and may USE Pas and others for His ends (for, as Wolfe says elsewhere, they are His servants, too, though they don't know it). So I don't see the end as tragic, though it is only a partial salvation, as you note.

Yes. Wolfe wants us to see those who are saved as having been sinners first, and thus adds this feature to his Peter.

Hmmm. Good question.

It is probably both.
BTW, Silk REJECTS the offer of a visit from his parents (p. 348), right? And Silk knows both his sets of parents, as I pointed out above.

It is true that we are not shown his accepting the offer. But there was something that made me think he too her up on it. I don't have time to reread the remaining 50 pages of the book to find it.
Anyway, the offer itself carries the same symbolic-parodic weight. Perhaps it is a temptation: to join an anti-trinity?

I don't think we are supposed to like Hyacinth. People are corrupt, more than they realize; right? Christ's Bride often (usually?) acts like a whore, acts like she did before He saved and married her. But He loves her anyway. I think that is the point.
I think Silk's running after Hyacinth is the Good Shepherd leaving the 99 and seeking the one sheep who is lost. Remember that Wolfe likes the parables a lot, as we see in "The Detective of Dreams."

I enjoyed your interaction. Do you have any replies to mine?

Jim Jordan
