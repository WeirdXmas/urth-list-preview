The thread: The thread began as a debate over how old Urth is, given the planet's cooled interior and absence of volcanism. Jeffrey Lefstin argued from Severian's remarks and rough cooling timescales that Urth must be far older than the 'perhaps a million years' figure. Macronaut and Jeff Wilson countered that intelligent action could accelerate cooling, with Wilson tying it to the Great Beast Erebus draining Urth's energy. The thread then drifted into a separate debate over whether Cormac McCarthy's Blood Meridian is pointless violence or epic, between Don Doggett and maru dubshinki, and ended there without resolution on the geology question.
  • Jeffrey Lefstin: Urth is far older than a million years because its interior has cooled and volcanism has stopped
  • Macronaut: Intelligent beings could have accelerated cooling, so inactivity does not imply antiquity
  • Jeff Wilson: Erebus in a volcanic mountain drains Urth's energy, so inactivity is not natural cooling
  • don doggett: Blood Meridian's violence is deliberately pointless and its biblical rhetoric makes scalp hunters epic heroes
  • maru dubshinki: Blood Meridian's violence seems gratuitous; the judge is the only striking character

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Deep time and ruins, Books, libraries and writing

2006-07-30 Jeffrey Lefstin #

Argues that Urth must be far older than the "perhaps a million years" figure Jeff Wilson quoted, because the planet's interior has solidified and volcanic activity has stopped. Grounds this in Severian's repeated mentions of the lack of volcanism and a geologist's rough sense of cooling timescales, while conceding Wolfe is not a hard SF writer.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Urth is tens of millions of years old

If we were really trying to deduce the age of Urth from the text, it must be older than that. At several points Severian mentions the lack of volcanic activity on Urth and the solidification of the Urth's interior. I'm not a geologist, but a few million years, or even a few tens of millions of years, isn't going to cut it if we assume that volcanic activity has ceased due to natural cooling and decay.

But since Wolfe is not a hard SF writer I wouldn't make too much of it.

2006-07-30 Cliff Judge replying to Jeffrey Lefstin #

Praises the stories in Book of Days as Wolfe's best short fiction, especially "How I Lost the Second World War," and reads its transistor and board-game elements as a model of how the real world relates to fiction. Also suggests "Forlesen" may be Gnostic, casting the Demiurge as Mr Frick or the man in the black suit and Forlesen as Jesus or the Christian God.
proposes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Forlesen reads as a Gnostic myth of the Demiurge; Transistor represents how the real world relates to fiction

Okay I'm rapidly running out of easily-obtainable Wolfe books to read. I've recently started on _Castle of Days,_ and I am ready to proclaim the stories in _Gene Wolfe's Book of Days_ to be, IMO, Wolfe's best short fiction.

I'm particularly fond of "How I Lost the Second World War and Helped Turn Back the German Invasion," I think it is officially my very favorite Wolfe short story. It's just a perfect little piece of science fiction - it is alternate history, has a clever ending, a terribly fun exposition of a logical game, contains the real world inside of it, as a board game, and, I am convinced, that business with the transistor is meant to explain how the real world relates to fiction, though which side of the transistor represents which, I am not sure.

And "Forlesen," wow, I haven't caught up on all of the more enlightened discussions to take place on this list about that one yet, but to me it seems like he might actually be talking about Gnosticism in there. The day that Forlesen leads is a life in the imperfect universe created by the Demiurge, who is either Mr Frick or the man in the black suit who asks him which Explainer he wants. Hmm...perhaps Forlesen is Jesus or the actual Christian God.

Right let me go catch up on those past posts before I waste too much bandwidth speculating...

2006-07-31 Macronaut replying to Jeffrey Lefstin #

Argues that the cooling of Urth's interior and the lack of volcanic activity need not mean Urth is geologically ancient, because intelligent beings could have accelerated the cooling. Responds to Jeffrey Lefstin's point that the text's 'perhaps a million years' and the geology imply a much older Urth, using the cooling sun as a parallel for human action causing natural-seeming effects.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · puzzle-solving · theories: Human activity accelerated Urth's cooling

A good point but, as the cooling sun teaches, we
cannot discount action by intelligent beings as the
cause of apparently natural phenomena. Given
mankind's other advanced technology, it's more than
possible that men engaged in some large-scale activity
which, intentionally or not, resulted in the cooling
at an accelerated rate.

Macronaut

2006-07-31 Jeff Wilson replying to Jeffrey Lefstin #

Argues that Urth's apparent geological inactivity is not due to natural cooling, because the Great Beast Erebus lives in a volcanic mountain and drains energy from Urth as the black beasts drain the old sun. Supports this by citing the 19th-century refrigeration model from old books and by pointing to Wolfe's hard SF in a story where a motor race turns on transistor physics.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Erebus drains energy from Urth like black beasts drain the sun; Urth's cooling reflects 19th-century misteaching

We can't assume it's natural because one of the Great Beasts (Erebus) lives in/on/as a volcanic mountain itself. They sap energy from Urth the same way the black beans/worms sap the old sun. This, combined with the 19th century refrigeration model gleaned from old books that are the source of other similar misteachings, more than explain the apparent deviation from 1981 geology.

Gene Wolfe is a very hard SF writer when he wishes to be; in "How I Lost the Second World War and Helped Turn Back the German Invasion" , victory in a motor race hinges on the quantum mechanical workings of the transistor.

2006-08-03 Don Doggett replying to maru dubshinki #

Argues that the violence in Blood Meridian is pointless on purpose, since it reflects the bloody making of the American West, and that its biblical rhetoric lifts scalp hunters to the level of epic heroes like Homeric Achaeans and Argonauts. Responds to Maru Dubshinki's claim that the judge is the only good character, and mentions Graves and Gardner as comparisons; the post is off-topic for Wolfe.
disputes

[regarding Blood Meridian]

I have to admit I haven't read Bloom's criticism. The
book was recommended to me by a couple of friends. I
would agree that much of the goings on in it are
pointless and gratuitous but I would argue that that
is precisely the point of the book. At it's base level
the book is about the violent convulsions that created
the westward expansion in this country. And having
lived in the southwest desert for the past two years,
I have to say it is still one of the most casually
violent (in attitude if not physicality) places I have
ever resided. But for me, this aspect of the book on
its own would make a pretty pedantic read. It's the
biblical rhetoric and the elevation of every action to
the level of myth that sets this book apart for me. In
my mind the scalp hunters are no different from the
Argonauts (read Graves' Hercules my Shipmate or John
Gardner's Jason and Medea) or the Achaeans at the
walls of Troy, violent, unreflective men whose
unchecked impulses are raised by poetry to epic
significance. It's a western myth in an old world down
and dirty blood sacrifice kind of way that unsettled
me in my gut. I feel there's even more to it,
especially in the interplay between the Kid and Judge
Holden, but I'm not far enough away from my reading to
think about it clearly.
But that's my two cents. I think it's a brilliant book
but I know plenty of people who couldn't finish it
because of the violence or who thought it was a
pretentious waste of time. To each their own.

regards
Don
