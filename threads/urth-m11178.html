Seawrack's name

Urth list · 4 messages · 2010-08-30 to 2010-08-31

2010urthThe Book of the Short SunthalassocratDavid StockhoffLee Berman
The thread: Posters asked whether the name Seawrack points to sirens, mermaids, and European water spirits in Wolfe's Short Sun books. Lee Berman proposed the legend-based reading, linking her to undines, nixies, the Syrenka and a possible changeling origin. David Stockhoff first disputed that the name fits, arguing it is a plant name suited to a female character, then supported the siren link as a loose fit. Mr Thalassocrat argued Horn names her after the seawrack he finds on the island and her frailty, not from legend. The thread ends with the textual reading favored over the mythic one, though Stockhoff's partial agreement leaves the matter open.
  • Lee Berman: Seawrack links to sirens, mermaids and European water spirits, possibly a changeling
  • David Stockhoff: name is a fitting plant name, loosely fits a siren figure, Horn does not speak Polish
  • Mr Thalassocrat: Horn names her after the seawrack plant and her frailty, not legend

Themes: Water, the sea and drowning, Myth and folklore sources, Names and their meanings

2010-08-30 Lee Berman #

Argues that sirens, mermaids, and European water spirits are intertwined legends, linking Seawrack's identity to undines, manatees, and a possible melusine or syrenka inspiration in the Short Sun books. Grounds the case in BotNS mentions of undines and nixies, Juturna's seductive luring of Severian, and Seawrack's conversations with the Mother suggesting she was a changeling child.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Seawrack is a changeling child stolen from her mother; Seawrack is a piece of a larger water entity; Sirens and mermaids are intertwined legends

At the risk of mucking up David's perfectly good Andersenian reference I'd want to note that sirens and mermaids are not really distinct creatures. The sirens are often depicted as mermaids and mermaids were often supposed to have a seductive, deadly allure. I think the legends are all intertwined.

Manatees are supposed to be the real world inspiration for mermaids. As mentioned, their order classification is Sirenia. We know undines are confused with manatees in BotNS. I don't remember Juturna singing but she certainly had a seductive nature, luring Severian toward an underwater future.

Wolfe is certainly giving those European water nymphs/demons their due. Joining the undines and nixies and kelpies that are mentioned in BotNS, now we might have a melusine/syrenka inspiration to the siren of the Short Sun.

I like the idea of a pinched off piece of a larger entity as the origin for undines and Seawrack. But I notice in these European legends of the creatures there is often a Changeling aspect. And some of the Mother and Seawrack's talk does suggest Seawrack could have been a child stolen from her real mother. I suspect another one of those double possibility solutions WOlfe throws at us.

2010-08-31 thalassocrat replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that Horn names the girl Seawrack because of the green sea-plant wrack he pulls from the ground on the island, the similar sound of the name she hears, and her hair and frailty, and that trying to reconstruct her mother's name is not worthwhile. Responds to David Stockhoff's claim that the Syrenka links to sirens by offering a textual explanation grounded in Horn's own passages about seawrack.
proposes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Horn names the girl Seawrack from seawrack plant

Whatever the Mother calls her, I think it's pretty clear how Horn comes to call her "Seawrack".

While walking on the island he pulls up some of the ground cover and sees in his hand "a little, weak, torn thing", and "knew it for the green scum I had seen washed ashore after storms". In other words, what might be called "seawrack", http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawrack

Wouldn't be at all surprising if "Seawrack" is a recognised female Vironese name.

Shortly afterward that, he meets her and hears a name which suggests to him "Seawrack", because of a similarity in sounds, because of her hair "misted green by some microscopic sea-plant", and just because it seems to him to suit her - presumably in part because she appears to him then in some respects "a little, weak, torn thing".

Personally, I think that trying to reconstruct the name the Mother calls her by is one of those Wolfean invitations to drive oneself bat-shit - simply not enough information. My guess is that it's something Neighbor-ish. Anyway, Horn never seems to have trouble with the names of the human "foreigners" he encounters.
