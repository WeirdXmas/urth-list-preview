What's So Great About Ushas?

Urth list · 4 messages · 2008-07-18 to 2008-07-18

2008urthThe Book of the New SunJohn WatkinsRussell WodellPaul Bb sharp
The thread: The thread asked whether the Hierogrammates in Wolfe's Urth and New Sun books are angels and therefore morally exempt, and whether a reader's ethics or Wolfe's intentions should settle the matter. Paul B argued they are not angels, citing four textual points (human creation, self-interest, mortality, imperfect foresight) and making them morally culpable. John Watkins agreed they are not unfallen angels, rebutting the pro-angel evidence with Catholic doctrine. Russell Wodell argued the debate is less about dogma than about Wolfe deliberately building moral ambiguity, while b sharp pressed for textual quotations and questioned whether authorial intent matters. The thread ends with the positions stated but no clear resolution.
  • Paul B: Hierogrammates are not angels, so they lack angelic moral exemption and are culpable, based on four textual points
  • John Watkins: Hierogrammates are not meant to be unfallen angels; rebuts pro-angel evidence with Catholic doctrine
  • Russell Wodell: Wolfe, a devout Catholic, builds moral ambiguity on purpose to make readers reflect
  • b sharp: Asks whether Paul judges by his own ethics or Wolfe's, demands textual quotes, doubts authorial intent matters

Themes: Gods, angels and the Increate, Catholic faith and sacraments, Free will and choice

2008-07-18 Russell Wodell replying to b sharp #

Argues that the debate about the Hierogrammates' morality is not really about Christian or Catholic dogma, but that Wolfe, a devout Catholic, deliberately builds moral ambiguity into his work to prompt readers to reflect. Responds to b sharp's quoted question of whether Paul B is a Christian and whether Wolfe's intentions matter, offering Wolfe's presumed faith as the reason the ambiguity is there.
disputes · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe's moral ambiguity is deliberate and Catholic-motivated

I don't think this debate is specifically about Christian and/or Catholic dogma. I do think that Gene Wolfe is a devoute Catholic, and that by infusing his work with careful moral ambiguity, thus triggering this debate, he believes that he is encouraging his readers to do God's work.

2008-07-18 Paul B replying to b sharp #

Argues that the Hierogrammates in The Urth of the New Sun are not angels and so cannot claim the moral exemption enjoyed by Christian angels or God, making them morally culpable under Wolfe's presumably Christian deontological framework. Grounds this on four textual points: they are human creations, act in self-interest, are mortal, and have imperfect foresight, citing Apheta, Tzadkiel's son's death, and Tzadkiel's trial.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Hierogrammates are not angels and are morally culpable

I'm curious why you think it'd be more interesting - is it because then I'd have to solve the Problem of Evil? I already said I didn't have a solution for it.

As for a hypothetical Wolfe-view of the Hierogrammates, I suspect his moral worldview is certainly Christian-influenced. Christianity is a very deontological religion, I think, more so than say Judaism (the latter permits exceptions to things that are uniformly forbidden in Christianity). God (somehow - Wolfe solved this one in private) gets an exception from the moral duties the rest of us have, and the Christian angels - whom I've never heard to act in self-interest - can be privy to whatever exception God is granted as his tools. Everyone else has to play by the rules, unless God personally grants them an exemption (Abraham).

However, the Hierogrammates are quite different from Christian angels, if you'd like I'll restate the textual references I suggested earlier, and which were strengthened by Roy, me being without UotNS.

1.) Hierogrammates are human creations. (Apheta, UotNS) 2.) They are acting in avowed self-interest, so that the future Hieros will recreate them. (Apheta again)
3.) They are mortal. (Tzadkiel's son dies)
4.) Their foresight is imperfect - they do not have certainty of the outcome of their actions. (Tzadkiel at the trial)

This simply doesn't add up to an angelic nature. They might pass for Biblical prophets (except for the human creation and self-interest parts), but those were never under any moral exemption.
If God came down and literally told them they must drown Urth as such was his will, Apheta or Tzadkiel probably would have mentioned that as a much more compelling and palatable reason than what they gave (worked for Isaac, sorta). As mortal free agents, however, they have to obey all the deontological principles the framework of Wolfe's religion imposes on them.

Thus, even in this perspective, they aren't blameless.

Paul

2008-07-18 John Watkins replying to b sharp #

Argues that the Hierogrammates in The Book of the New Sun are not meant to be unfallen angels, weighing the case for and against that reading. Lays out the four pro-angel points (the name 'Holy', Tzadkiel's winged form, Severian's remarks, Wolfe's interview) and rebuts them with Catholic doctrine on angelic morality and direct creation, concluding 'not'.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Hierogrammates are not unfallen angels

I don't know about Paul, but I'd be more interested if we focus on the question of whether the Hierogrammates are intended to be (unfallen) angels by Gene Wolfe. Without my books at hand, the argument for this appears to be:

1) "Holy" is part of their name.

2) Tzadkiel has a winged form that Severian identifies as an angel.

3) Severian thinks Hierogrammates serve the Increate.

4) Wolfe referred to Tzadkiel as an "angel" in an interview.

The argument against the Hierogrammates being angels, as I understand it, is

1) Catholic theology posits that angels are perfectly moral, but the Hierogrammates frequently engage in morally dubious if not outright evil behavior.

2) Hierogrammates, unlike angels, are not direct creations of God.

3) Most of the arguments that Hierogrammates are angels are hearsay.

I think these are both colorable arguments, but I come down on the "not" side. The Hierogrammates are obviously *spoken of *as angels, but they are just as obviously not two things that are *essential *to the meaning of the word "angel."
