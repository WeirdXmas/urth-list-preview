The thread: Readers asked whether Wolfe's artificial people, such as the chems, puppets, Mr Million and the Horars, are best understood as toys, and Michael Andre-Driussi also supplied the forgotten title of a story. Andy Robertson argued they are grown-up toys or dolls rather than robots, citing the toy-soldier imagery of The War Beneath the Tree and tying the chems' sterility to that status. Jerry Friedman disputed this, holding that the artificial beings should be treated as equivalent to humans and Neighbors even though their creators regard them as toys, and offering Dr. Talos and the homunculus as examples. The thread ended with the two readings standing side by side rather than resolved.

2002-09-26 Andy Robertson #

Argues that Wolfe's artificial people, from the Long Sun chems to the puppets and Mr Million, are best understood as grown-up toys or dolls rather than robots. Grounds this in the toy-soldier imagery of The War Beneath the Tree and explains the chems' reproductive difficulty and sexual impotence as following from being toys, answering Crush's question about Tin Woodman.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolfe's artificial people are essentially toys; Chems' reproductive difficulty follows from being toys; Chems are nurse and soldier dolls

IMHO the real root is the nursery/toy/steadfast tin solder vibe seen well in "The War Beneath The Tree".

As I reflect, it seems to me that all Wolfe's artificial people- from the chems of the Long Sun, through the adult-sized puppets of <story whose name I forget>, through Mr Million and the Horars (note name of tank), are, more than anything else, to be understood as walking *toys*.

Gods' toys, maybe. But essentially toys.

Hence their sexual impotence.

Toys have nothing between their legs, and neither do robots, however "superior" they may be in other ways.

In the Long Sun books this is hypostatised into the difficulty the Chems have in reproducing, by their non-sexual, but plausible, means.

There is no reason why they should have this difficulty - it could be arranged otherwise - but they must have it, because they are toys, and toys do not have babies.

And also this is the key to their personal characteristics and the odd limitations of the way Wolfe uses them.

Stop thinking of them as robots.

They are dolls! Big grown-up dolls!,

Nurse and housemaid dolls for the female chems, soldier dolls for the male ones.

hartshorn

2002-09-27 Jerry Friedman #

Argues that Wolfe's artificial beings, such as the chems, puppets and Horn-like machines, should be seen as equivalent to humans and Neighbors rather than as mere toys, though their creators treat them so. Responds to Andy Robertson's toy-based reading, agreeing that the creators think this way and adding Dr. Talos and the homunculus in The Citadel of the Autarch as further examples.
disputes · author-and-biography · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolfe's artificial beings are equivalent to humans

I think you're right that this is how their creators think of them. I don't think Severian and Silk think of them as toys at all.

As for how we should think of them, I think you're right that in some artistic sense they're connected with various fictional toys you and others have mentioned. But I think we're supposed to see them as equivalent to humans (and Neighbors), just as we're supposed to see the human characters as humans and not, though Severian remarks people often do, see them as clients.

You can certainly add Dr. Talos to your list, by the way. What about the homunculus in _Citadel_? Or the aquastors and Hierodules?
