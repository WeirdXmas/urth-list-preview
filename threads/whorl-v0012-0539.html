merging

Whorl list · 5 messages · 2001-04-05 to 2001-04-06

2001whorlThe Book of the Long SunDan'l Danehy-OakesMarc AraminiAdam StephanidesMichael Straight
The thread: Posters debated whether the Horn-Silk narration in On Blue's Waters shows an explicit merger, focusing on a passage where Horn calls his son Horn and recalls catching the ball and winning a game. maa32 argued these are open signs of merging, supported by Horn's self-description and shared childhood toys in Lake of the Long Sun. Michael Straight argued the ball-game memory belongs to Horn's persona, that Nettle wrote the flattering Horn descriptions, and that Silk would not recall a childhood game. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Adam Stephanides offered counterpoints, with Adam suggesting the slips reflect Silk surfacing outside Horn's control and noting he lacked the book to check the passage. The thread ended without resolution, with competing readings of the same passages.
  • maa32: Horn's narration openly shows he has merged with Silk, citing the son-naming and ball-catching passages
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Silk would recall the game because it was when enlightenment came to him
  • Michael Straight: the ball-game passage belongs to Horn's persona, and Nettle wrote the flattering Horn descriptions
  • Adam Stephanides: the slips show Silk surfacing outside Horn's control, not Horn signaling the merger

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Memory and forgetting, Unreliable narration

2001-04-06 Michael Straight #

Argues that the ball-catching and winning-the-game passage in On Blue's Waters belongs to Horn's persona rather than Silk's, and that Nettle, not Horn, wrote the flattering Horn descriptions in Lake of the Long Sun. Supports this with the reasoning that Silk would not recall a childhood game while Horn would remember the first chapter of his Book of Silk as a metaphor for helping others.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn and Silk have merged into one persona; Nettle wrote the flattering Horn passages in Lake of the Long Sun

I'm sure Nettle put that stuff in about Horn in _Lake_ over his strenuous objections.

Also, see the archives for my argument that the bit about catching the ball and winning the game makes much more sense coming from Horn's persona than from Silk's. I don't see why Silk would even remember the details of that game, much less have a habit of reminiscing about his athletic triumphs over children, whereas Horn surely remembers well the first chapter of his Book of Silk and sees it as a metaphor for helping others.

-Rostrum

2001-04-06 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that apparent slips in On Blue's Waters, where Horn is named as the father of the son and a passage seems to say Silk caught the ball, reflect Silk surfacing outside Horn's control rather than Horn signaling awareness of the merger. Responds to maa32's claim that Horn explicitly communicates he has merged with Silk, though the poster does not have the book to check the passage.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk surfaces in Horn's narration outside Horn's control

While I don't have the book with me, so can't comment on this particular passage (which was the subject of discussion earlier), I would say that passages like these are the Silk in Silkhorn popping up, outside of Horn's conscious control, rather than the Horn in Silkhorn signaling that he is aware of Silk's presence.

--Adam

2001-04-06 Michael Straight #

Argues that the ball-game memory and the line about fairness in The Book of the Long Sun belong to Horn's persona rather than Silk's, since Silk was preoccupied with his enlightenment. Supports this by citing Silk's talk with the boys after the game, Horn's reply about playing your best, and a parallel idea Incanto voices to Mora in In Green's Jungles.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: The ball-game memory comes from Horn's persona, not Silk

Look at Silk's conversations with the kids right after the game. He's so preoccupied with his enlightenment he's barely paying attention when they try to talk about the game. Horn stays behind and talks to Silk about it. Silk apologizes for pushing him down and Horn says "You were their best player, it wouldn't have been fair to them if you hadn't played your best."

This seems to be the idea the Rajan has in mind after having helped the Gaonese win their war. He's saying "I am proud to have won the war for the Gaonese just as Silk won the game for the small boys." Silk wasn't proud of winning the game for the small boys. At the time, Silk said the fun of the game comes when a small boy knocks down a big boy.

We know Horn remembers the incident and his own interpretation of it, but it seems unlikely that Silk remembers it or places the same kind of importance on it--he was thinking about enlightenment, not lessons learned on the ball court.

(Incanto expounds a similar idea to Mora in IGJ, telling her that the only thing that is more "fair" to allow all to do their best than to ask the strong/rich/talented restrain themselves out of a sense of fairness, but since Incanto is part Silk, that doesn't count as evidence of whether this comes from Horn or not.)

-Rostrum
