Kiki

The thread: Posters debated what the old woman Kiki is and what the cart motif means, with David Stockhoff linking the cart to Le Morte d'Arthur and Matthew Keeley adding Lancelot's cart ride. Craig Brewer asked whether Kiki is a witch or hides another identity, pointing to the OED and to Crowley's Little, Big. Roy C. Lackey argued the growing house marks a doorway into Faerie tied to Crowley, while John Watkins and James Wynn compared the houses to House of Leaves and Peace. Several posts were brief agreements or jokes, and the thread drifted into comparisons across books without settling Kiki's identity or the cart's meaning.
  • David Stockhoff: the cart motif from Le Morte d'Arthur may explain Kiki's riding twice in a cart, though he cannot yet work out the meaning
  • Matthew Keeley: Lancelot's cart ride supports the cart as a special motif, as the Knight of the Cart precedent
  • Craig Brewer: asks whether Kiki is a witch or has a hidden identity, citing the OED and Little, Big
  • Roy C. Lackey: the growing house links Kiki to Crowley's Little, Big and marks a doorway into Faerie
  • John Watkins: the growing houses resemble House of Leaves, both with unreliable narrators, twins, and an epistolary section
  • James Wynn: the Black House resembles the ever-growing house in Peace

Themes: Buildings, cities and labyrinths, Unreliable narration, Myth and folklore sources

2010-04-19 David Stockhoff #

Suggests that the cart motif in Le Morte d'Arthur, where the condemned ride to the gallows and goddesses ride in processions, may give precise meaning to a witch's or fairy's riding 'twice in a cart' for Kiki. Admits he cannot yet work out the exact meaning, answering an earlier post that asks who Kiki really is.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Kiki's cart ride signals she is a witch or fairy

And there's also the eery significance of carts in /*Le Morte d'Arthur*/ and elsewhere: The condemned were carried in carts to the gallows, but goddesses were also carried in carts in procession long before that.

So a witch's or fairy's riding "twice in a cart" could mean something very precise, but I couldn't guess what.

2010-04-19 Matthew Keeley replying to David Stockhoff #

Supports David Stockhoff's suggestion that carts carry special significance by adding that Lancelot rides in a cart to save Guenivere in Le Morte d'Arthur. Notes that Lancelot was sometimes called the Knight of the Cart, offering this Arthurian episode as a precedent for the cart motif.
supports · source-and-allusion

At one point Lancelot, questing to save Guenivere, loses his horse, swallows his pride, and rides in a cart to save the queen. Indeed, Lancelot was sometimes known as "The Knight of the Cart".

-Matt

2010-04-20 Craig Brewer replying to Matthew Keeley #

Asks whether Kiki, the old woman who gives Doris and Bax a ride, is a witch or has some other hidden identity beyond being a faerie-flavored figure. Points to the OED's note that "cart" could mean carriage, and to the forest house seen from the road as recalling the house glimpsed in Crowley's Little, Big.
questions · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Kiki is a witch

The OED says that "cart" was also often used for "carriage," so it could just be Wolfe using older vocab.

I'm more interested in Kiki's role in the narrative. I mean, is she just there for "we're now in faerie" flavor, or does she have some other identity we don't really know. The character list says that she was befriended by Doris and Bax, but does giving her a ride really go so far as making friends? Since Martha mentions witches and sorcerers fighting witches, I like the possibility that she's a witch, but then I wonder what else might identify her as a witch, apart from just being old, bent, mysterious, and able to tell that there's a vampire in the trunk.

And, for some reason, when they mention that her house is just out of sight in the forest, something about that reminded me of one of the first glimpses of Faerie we get in Crowley's _Little, Big_ with the little house. Nothing specific, just one of many flashes of Crowley's book that kept popping into my head while reading. The houses that change shape/size is another obvious similarity, of course.

2010-04-20 Roy C. Lackey replying to Craig Brewer #

Argues that the house in Kiki that grows and shrinks, changing from nine rooms to over forty, links the novel to John Crowley's Little, Big and marks a doorway into Faerie. Supports this by quoting Martha's remark that the house grows when people live in it and shrinks when they do not, and by noting a parallel with Crowley's houses that are bigger inside than outside.
proposes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: the Black house is a doorway into Faerie

[snip]
sight in the forest, something about that reminded me of one of the first glimpses of Faerie we get in Crowley's _Little, Big_ with the little house. Nothing specific, just one of many flashes of Crowley's book that kept popping into my head while reading. The houses that change shape/size is another obvious similarity, of course.<

I, too, was reminded of Crowley's book, particularly that last bit about houses changing size, being bigger on the inside than they could possibly have been as judged from outside, IIRC. That is why I brought up earlier the issue of the Black house changing from nine rooms to more than forty. Martha said, "It grows when people live in it, and shrinks when they don't." (278)

The house, perhaps more than anything else, makes me believe the Faerie bits of the story are true. That house is a doorway into Faerie, and that is why George wanted it. (261)

-Roy

2010-04-20 John Watkins replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Compares the weird houses in Kiki that seem to grow with those in House of Leaves, noting both are told by unreliable narrators and involve twins and an epistolary section. Replies to Roy C. Lackey's comparison of Kiki with Crowley's Little, Big, admitting he has not read it yet and calling House of Leaves the more ambitious book.
proposes · genre-and-craft

I haven't read Little, Big yet (on my shelf but buried at the moment) but did anyone else get a bit of a House of Leaves vibe? House of Leaves is, IMHO, a more ambitious book and a better one, but both are stories about weird houses that seemingly increase in size, told by unreliable narrators and involving a pair of twins. There's even an epistolatory section of House of Leaves.

2010-04-20 James Wynn replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Compares the labyrinthine Black House to the large, constantly growing house in Peace, whose rooms the narrator knows of but struggles to find. Responds to Roy C. Lackey's mention of Crowley's house that changes size and his quotation of Martha saying the Black House grows when people live in it.
supports · close-textual-reading

The labyrnthian Black House's reminded me of Weer's large, constantly growing house in Peace which had rooms Weer knew of but had trouble or even no idea how to find them.
