The Saga of the Urth Mailing List: An Excerpt

Urth list · 7 messages · 2002-06-11 to 2002-06-12

2002urthThe Book of the New SunDan'l Danehy-OakesJames WynnAndy RobertsonAdam Stephanides
The thread: Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and James Wynn debated whether Severian in The Book of the New Sun is a Christ figure, with Danehy-Oakes arguing via Catholic sacramental theology and Wolfe's Christ-figure versus Christian-figure distinction that Severian may be Christ-like without being Christ. Wynn countered with Wolfe's interview remarks that Christ is both God and man, and that Severian is a Christian rather than a Christ. Adam Stephanides questioned how readers could know which universe holds the sacrificed God and suggested the Theoanthropos might be a future figure. Danehy-Oakes answered that the sacrifice saves every universe in Wolfe's cyclic scheme, making the question of which universe is right matter less. The thread ended without agreement, with the Christ-figure label left contested.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Severian is Christ-like through sacramental participation, not literally Christ; the sacrifice saves every cyclic universe
  • James Wynn: Wolfe says Severian is a Christian and Christ figure in position but not Christ, since Christ is fully God and man
  • Adam Stephanides: Asks how readers can know which universe is the true one; suggests the Theoanthropos may be a future figure

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Death and resurrection

2002-06-11 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Wolfe's Theoanthropos and Severian may be Christ-like without being Christ, since a spatiotemporally unique Incarnation can be eternally present to many points in time. Grounds this in Catholic sacramental theology of the Mass, the Outsider-possessed man, Old Testament Jesus-parallels, and Wolfe's own distinction between 'Christ figures' and 'Christian figures' in an interview with James Jordan.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Theoanthropos is a previous iteration of Christ; Severian figures Christ without being Christ; Urth lies in a past cycle of the universe

Emerging from the murky mists of the Internet,
composing on computers their provident remarks,
James and Jeff have jointly written:

I should clarify my point here ... it is _not_ that it could happen more than once in time; it is that the spatiotemporally unique event could be eternally present to many, any, or all points of spacetime. (Hey, how often do you get to use relatvisitic terminology in a christological discussion?) What I mean is, ignoring the question of other universes, in _this_ universe, we (the Roman Catholic church, and I am pretty sure the Orthodox churches also) believe that in the sacrifice of the Mass, we are not repeating the sacrifice of Golgotha but participating in it, being present with Christ at that eternally unique moment.

(If this is still a bit vague, forgive me; I'm trying _not_ to turn this into a theology list, so I'm making non-Lupine theological remarks as brief as possible.)

Okay, applying the above principle to the "Books" I think we need to look at it with two different heads.

We can regard the references to the "theoanthropos," etc., either as "allegorical of" Christ or simply descriptive of Him. Matters not: the point is that from this perspective, Wolfe is describing stuff which we are clearly intended to recognize as in some way referring to Christ.

But in trying to decipher the proposed internal reality of the books, the universe of the discourse, we need to take a different tack and ask whether the Theoanthropos, the man enlightened and possessed by the Outsider "is" Christ? Now, assuming (as I do assume) that Wolfe is not playing us false in claiming that he intends this as a previous iteration of the universe from Big Bang to Grand Gnab; and assuming equally that he, like myself and most Christians, regard the Incarnation and Sacrifice as a unique event, then either the Theoanthropos is not God, or the person we call Christ is not God.

A simple and, I think, elegant resolution, which is consistent with the text: in many universes, possibly in many worlds in a single universe, Man or some other sentient race has Fallen. In one and only one of these universes, the second Person of the Trinity became incarnate and allowed Himself to be sacrificed to reconcile these sentient races to the Godhead. In many other times-and-places, however, the Godhead "enlightened and possessed" a person to perform acts that, in the sense of the Mass but more so, are united with and copresent with the unique Incarnation and Sacrifice. For a person who knows only of these events, belief in them is essentially belief in the unique event.

God is, after all, a torturer.

Or that the Hieros, seeking to guide the recreation of the species that created their predecessors, do so with the kind of obsessive accuracy that actually does result in such recurrence?

The short of it is, though, that I disagree, because I think putting Urth in a past cycle seems to me to make sense, while admitting that I don't have an easy answer to such problems.

2002-06-11 James Wynn #

Quotes an interview exchange in which Gene Wolfe says Severian is a Christ figure in position but not Christ, since Christ is both God and man. Supports the point by reproducing Wolfe's remarks on Jesus being fully human and tempted as we are, responding to James Wynn's claim that Wolfe denied Severian is Christ.
informs · theology-and-religion · author-and-biography

Ah! Yes. Here's the quote:
JJ: The holy slaves [Heirodules], Famulimus or one of them tells Severian that he is the center of his race, the savior of his race. That is such Christlike language you can see why interpreters would say well Severian is a Christ figure. But is there a Christ figure in the book, or is he simply for this universe?
GW: In so far as there is a Christ figure it is Severian. That doesn't mean he has to be identified with Christ. He is in a position similar to that of Christ. But really it is a different position because Christ really is both God and man. Severian is not. Severian is a Christian rather than a Christ. But he is been taken as the representative of humanity by whom humanity is to be judged. This I think is what has happened perhaps with the actual human Jesus. He is or was is as fully human as you or I and we are saved by Him. By the fact that he passed. That the corruption did not destroy Him. I think that St. Paul is absolutely correct when he says that Jesus was tempted in all the ways that we are tempted. I think that Jesus was tempted to commit murder or any other sin that you want to name just as the rest of us are. And the difference is that He did not sin.
-- Crush

2002-06-11 Adam Stephanides #

Asks how readers can know their universe is the one where the incarnate God was sacrificed, and proposes that the Theoanthropos might be a future figure rather than a past one. Replies to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's quotation of James and Jeff, asking whether the books rule out a recurring Severian story and finding little gain in the past-universe resolution.
questions · theology-and-religion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Theoanthropos is an anticipated future figure; Severian's story recurs in future universes

[snip]

So in every universe, there is a figure who is regarded as a sacrificed God-become-man, but only one of these universes has it right? In that case, how do we know that we're the lucky ones?

What occurred to me when I first read Wolfe's past-universe comment was that the Theoanthropos could be an anticipated future figure, rather than a past figure. Is there anything in the books which rules this out?

If that's the case, then presumably Severian and his story would recur too, rendering the whole question academic.

Why? I don't see a single advantage to it, aside from partially answering the question "How did we get Sev's story?" which, after all, arises with every SF story set in the future.

--Adam
