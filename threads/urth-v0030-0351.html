2001-04-27 Alex David Groce #

Asks whether readers should track down Wolfe's story "Try and Kill It" from the November 1996 Asimov's, which was a preliminary Nebula ballot entry but was never anthologized. Notes that someone placed it in Faulkner territory by comparing it to "The Ziggurat", and calls the sfsite story "Copperhead" mildly interesting, adding that it might spark more misogyny debates.
questions

Hey, how hard should I try to track down "Try and Kill It." Nov. 96 Asimov's, someone recently compared this to "The Ziggurat*" and claimed it was Wolfe in Faulkner territory in conversation; a quick net search reveals it was preliminary Nebula balloted that year, but wasn't in _Strange Travelers_ or reprinted elsewhere. Anyone on the list read it and think it was top-flight Wolfe?

The story "Copperhead" at sfsite is mildly interesting, but mostly strikes me as the kind of thing we usually get from Wolfe when he dips below novelette length. It might spark a few more Wolfe-misogyny debates.

* which for me says "high quality work indeed," though as we've seen YMMV.

2001-04-28 Adam Stephanides #

Judges Gene Wolfe's short story "Try and Kill It" as well written but not top-flight Wolfe, finding it less thematically rich than "The Ziggurat" and disliking its capable, manly protagonist. Responds to Alex David Groce's question by rereading the story, noting its brevity and its hunting plot, and rejecting the Faulkner resemblance.
answers · genre-and-craft

on 4/27/01 3:42 PM, Alex David Groce at [email]

You post inspired me to reread the story. While I liked it better the second time, I wouldn't call it top-flight Wolfe. I didn't much care for "The Ziggurat," actually, but "Try and Kill It" is much shorter (nineteen pages) and less thematically rich as far as I can tell. The protagonist is one of Wolfe's capable, manly men, which is probably my least favorite Wolfean type, although he's probably the most tolerable example of this type I've read. (If you are morally opposed to hunting, you probably won't like this story.) As far as Faulkner goes, it's not like any Faulkner I've read, which doesn't include the short stories; I suspect the perceived resemblance lies in the fact that it's about a man hunting an extraordinary bear. Wolfe's style is very good, but if there's more to the story than a well-written adventure story, I don't see it.

Incidentally, the issue in which "Try and Kill It" appeared has other items of interest besides Wolfe, most notably a novella by Gardner Dozois and Michael Swanwick. The bulk of it was written by Dozois (in the 60s), and my memory is that it's very good. It also contains Bruce Sterling's "Bicycle Repairman, and a Nancy Kress novelette, if you're a Kress fan.

--Adam
