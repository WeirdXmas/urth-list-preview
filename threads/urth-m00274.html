Best order to read Sun books?

Urth list · 16 messages · 2004-09-29 to 2004-10-05

2004urthThe Book of the New SunWilliam H. AnsleyAdam StephanidesJames WynnJonathon McKitrickDon DoggettChristopher CulverAndrew BollenDan'l Danehy-OakesAnthony Ferrara
The thread: Posters debated the best reading order for Wolfe's Sun books, with Ansley and Danehy-Oakes favoring publication-date order against Ferrara's chronological proposal, while Wynn offered a sequence without much reasoning. A second strand argued whether The Urth of the New Sun was needed: Culver said Wolfe wrote it only under editorial pressure, Wynn countered that the added explanation helped, and McKitrick and Stephanides doubted the first four books stand alone. Bollen's back-story and Clarke recommendations drifted off the main question, and the thread ends without consensus.
  • William Ansley: read the Sun books in publication order, with The Fifth Head of Cerberus first
  • Anthony Ferrara: read the Sun books in chronological order
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: publication order, citing Narnia as an example
  • Christopher Culver: The Book of the New Sun was meant to stand alone; The Urth of the New Sun came from editorial pressure
  • James Wynn: the editors were right, since the added volume enriched the original four books
  • Jonathon McKitrick: the first book does not stand alone and Urth leaves many questions open

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Stories within stories, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

2004-09-29 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to William Ansley #

Argues that Sun books should be read in publication order, citing the Narnia books as a case where a chronological reading order spoils the earlier context a later book assumes. Says the related short stories need not be placed at any particular point, though "The Night Chough" fits between the Long and Short Sun books. Responds to William Ansley's endorsement of publication-date reading and echoes his chorus-of-agreement tone.
supports · genre-and-craft

"We are the chorus and we agree.
We agree, we agree, we agree."

Gross example: The Narnia books. CS Lewis decided after the fact to recommend a reading order that begins with _The Magician's Nephew_, which is his prerogative but _dumb_ because _TMN_ kind of assumes that you are already familiar with some of the other books. At least in small ways.

Anyway, I highly recommend reading the "Books" in publication order. I'm not so convinced that there's any special purpose to reading the various related short stories in any particular place - though I _do_ think that "The Night Chough" fits nicely in between the Long and the Short of it.

--Blattid

2004-09-29 William H. Ansley replying to Anthony Ferrara #

Argues that the best way to read the Sun books is by publication date so readers see how the author's mind and recurring concepts develop, and suggests reading The Fifth Head of Cerberus first. Responds to Anthony Ferrara's proposal to read in chronological order, offering a contrary recommendation and noting thematic parallels between The Fifth Head of Cerberus and the Sun books while denying they share one universe.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: The Fifth Head of Cerberus is not set in the Sun universe

I strongly believe that the best way to read any series of books is by publication date. That way, you can best see the workings of the author's mind and the ways the various characters, concepts, etc. evolve over time.

To maximize your confusion, errr, enjoyment, I suggest that you read _The Fifth Head of Cerberus_ first, before the Sun series. I don't subscribe the notion that 5HoC is set in the "same universe" as the Sun books, but there are very interesting thematic parallels between the two works (5HoC and the Book of the Various Suns).

Have fun!

2004-09-29 James Wynn replying to William Ansley #

Recommends a reading order for Wolfe's sun books: place the Latro novels just before The Book of the Long Sun, and read The Book of the New Sun before the Long Sun so its intentional references in the Silk segment are caught. Responds to William Ansley's suggestion to read The Fifth Head of Cerberus before the Sun series, grounding the advice in publication-order reasoning and the difficulty of The Book of the Short Sun.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic

William Ansley:

This is absolutely true - especially for the "Book of the Short Sun". So perhaps reading "Fifth Head" just before plowing into the last series could be useful. On the other hand, reading "Fifth Head" last of all would not be worse.

However, if you go the route William Ansley suggests, then I recommend reading the Latro novels just before starting "The Book of the Long Sun" since Wolfe seems there to have been appeasing creative demons that were left unsatisfied when Tor gently recommended another Urth book instead of another Latro book.

Incidentally, while I'm not sold on always reading a series by publication chronology, I think if one does not read the New Sun first, he will miss intentional references in the Silk segment that the author intended. Since "Short Sun" is (a little too) difficult as it is (there! I said it!), I don't see any point in making it tougher.

~ Crush

2004-09-30 Andrew Bollen replying to James Wynn #

Argues that the doll-like figure in "The Cat" is the Chateleine's soul, carried by her cat from a border realm back to her body, and links this to soul-dolls in the Long Sun books. Grounds the reading in the earlier sparrow scene and in Inire's order to bury the doll with her, and responds to a discussion the poster missed.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · close-textual-reading · theories: The doll is the Chateleine's soul carried by her cat; Souls-as-dolls recur from The Cat into the Long Sun books

There was some discussion of this story a while back, which I didn't follow as I've only just read it - so maybe this has been done to death, but FWIW, my interpretation ...

The doll-like figure is surely the Chateleine's soul, or some aspect of it. Her cat, existing in some border-land between the "other realm" and this, has grabbed it on its passage through and delivered it to its mistress (ie the Chateleine's body) in the same way that it had deposited the body of a sparrow at her feet many years before. We know it's her soul and not some random border-land denizen because Inire orders it to be buried with her.

Souls-as-dolls seems to be another of Wolfe's little tropes - carried over into the Long Sun series in the form of the soul-dolls which appear to Silkhorn & others in dreams.

2004-09-30 Christopher Culver replying to Jonathon McKitrick #

Argues that Wolfe meant The Book of the New Sun to stand alone and wrote The Urth of the New Sun only because of editorial pressure. Replies to Jonathon McKitrick's doubt that the first four books could stand on their own, pointing to archive discussions and Wolfe interviews and urging newcomers to read those before asking.
disputes · author-and-biography · theories: The Urth of the New Sun written under editorial pressure

Wolfe originally intended to let the story stand on its own in those four books. The only reason he wrote UotNS is because of pressure by his editor.

This is all discussed in the archives and in interviews with Wolfe available on the web. I'm glad that the list is getting new people, but be aware this issue, and many others, have all be discussed before, and you'd get more profit out of the lengthy discussion in the archives than curt answers now.

Christopher Culver

(I thought I remembered this from Castle of Days, but I just went and looked, and it doesn't seem to be there.)

--Adam

2004-10-04 William H. Ansley replying to James Wynn #

Recommends reading The Book of the New Sun in full before Castle of Days, and only the Castle of the Otter portion of that combined volume rather than all of it. Suggests reading the short story "A Solar Labyrinth" right after the main sequence and considering its relation to the longer work.
proposes

I have to endorse reading _Castle of Days_ (CAD), although I would read the entire Book of the New Sun (BOTNS) (_Shadow of the Torturer_, _Claw of the Conciliator_, Sword of the Lictor_ and _Citadel of the Autarch_) first, then CAD. In fact, you don't need to read all of CAD, just the part that is _Castle of the Otter_ (CATO). CAD is a volume that combines CATO and _Gene Wolfe's Book of Days_, both of which were relatively short books that had gone out of print.

You might also consider reading the short story, "A Solar Labyrinth" in _Storeys from the Old Hotel_ right after BOTNS and consider what relation, if any, it has to the longer work.

2004-10-05 William H. Ansley replying to James Wynn #

Recommends Susanna Clarke's Jonathan Strange and Mr. Norrell to list members despite its lack of Wolfe-style obscurity, comparing it to John Crowley's work. Praises its charming early-19th-century English setting, its length and pacing, and Clarke's handling of fairies.
proposes

Even though it won't satisfy anyone who primarily reads Wolfe for the complexity and obscurity of his books, I still recommend _Jonathan Strange and Mr. Norrell_ by Susanna Clarke to the members of this list. I certainly think this book will appeal to those among you who enjoy John Crowley, although again, it is quite a different reading experience.

JS & Mr. N is a charmingly and quite well-written book about an alternative England, set in the early 19th century and written in the voice of an author of the period. Although the book is long (782 pages in hardcover) and occasionally seemed to be proceeding at too deliberate a pace, having finished it I cannot say that I begrudge Ms. Clarke a single page. I must add that Susanna Clarke is particularly sound on fairies.
