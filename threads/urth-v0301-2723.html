Reading the Soldier books

Urth list · 8 messages · 2003-01-20 to 2003-01-25

2003urthThe Book of the Long SunJames WynnJosh GellerJames JordanMichael Straight
The thread: The thread asked whether readers of Gene Wolfe's Soldier books need prior knowledge of Greek myth and Zoroastrianism. James Wynn argued no, recommending a read-look-up-reread method with Graves' Complete Myths as a key source. Michael Straight countered that background in Persian and Spartan history, especially helot slavery, made a major Arete event intelligible. Wynn admitted that event had puzzled him until explained and asked for an explanation, and James Jordan proposed that the climax was a massacre of freed helots by their Spartan owners, noting pages seem to be missing. Straight later corrected the scene's placement to the end of Part 1 and supplied the historical basis, so the thread ended in agreement on the helot reading rather than on the general question.
  • James Wynn: readers need not be experts; read, look up gaps, then reread
  • Michael Straight: historical background in Persia and Sparta makes Arete much easier to follow
  • James Jordan: the final Arete episode is a massacre of freed helots by Spartan owners
  • Michael Straight: the massacre scene falls at the end of Part 1, and helots were armed then falsely promised freedom

Themes: Justice, torture and mercy, Slavery and service, Myth and folklore sources

2003-01-20 James Wynn #

Disputes the claim that readers must know Greek mythology and Zoroastrianism before reading the Soldier books, arguing that one need not be an expert. Compares this to the New Sun and Long Sun books and recommends reading, looking up gaps, then rereading, with Graves' Complete Myths as a key source, in response to Crush.
disputes · source-and-allusion

You might want to skim Bulfinch's Mythology before starting in. You've gotta know a bit, yea more than a bit, about ancient Greek mythology. And if the series continues, you're going to have to know something about Zoroastrianism also, I suspect.

Crush refutes:
I gotta respectfully disagree with this. This is no more true than that one needs to know a lot about Sun mythology and symbolism to enjoy the "New Sun" or (the "Long Sun"), or to know about world Serpent mythology and classical references to enjoy the "Long Sun".

When you read these books you will **want** to find out more about the things you read about there (e.g. Cybele, Spartan culture & history, etc.). But, one might know a decent bit generally about Greek mythology, literature, and history and still find he has plenty to learn while reading "Mist" and "Arete."

For reading the Soldier books, I recommend doing what one would do for Wolfe's other works:
1. Read the books
2. Look up the obvious blanks the stories imply to you (or read Graves' "Complete Myths" from start to finish because this was an important source for this work)
3. Reread the books
-- Crush

2003-01-21 Michael Straight #

Argues that the plot of The Soldier of Arete is much easier to follow with background in Persian and early Peloponnesian history, especially Sparta and its treatment of slaves. Responds to James Wynn's claim that readers need not know Greek myth, citing a major Arete event that only made sense once a historical source was explained and offering three links.
informs · source-and-allusion

I agree that you probably don't need to know a lot about Greek Myth to appreciate the books, but I think you'll find the plot *much* easier to follow if read up a little on the history of the Persian and early Peloponnesian wars (especially focusing on the role of Sparta and their relationship with their slaves). I found the one of the major events of Arete totally incomprehensible until someone clued me in to the historical event it is based on.

Here's a few good links:

http://www.atrium-media.com/bibliotheca/greekhistory/thermopylae.html http://joseph_berrigan.tripod.com/ancientbabylon/id28.html http://ancienthistory.about.com/library/prm/blevilempired.htm

-Rostrum

2003-01-21 James Jordan #

Explains the climactic event at the end of Soldier of Arete as a likely massacre of helots by their Spartan owners right after they were freed for fighting in the battle. Grounds the reading in the book's unclear final scene, noting that pages seem to be missing, and admits this was the episode that puzzled the poster most.
proposes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Arete's ending depicts a massacre of freed helots by Spartans

But it's far from clear in the book what is going on. There are even missing pages!

Bet I'm right. Anyway, that's the one that stumped me the most.

2003-01-25 Michael Straight #

Corrects Nutria's placement of the Arete massacre scene, saying it falls at the end of Part 1 rather than at the end of the book. Supplies the historical basis: Spartan helots were armed for the Persian wars, then falsely promised freedom and killed, as the Arete scene hints.
supports · source-and-allusion · theories: Arete's massacre scene is based on Spartan helot killings

Nutria is right, except the scene in question is in the middle of Arete, at the end of the Part 1.

Supposedly true history: Spartan slaves were forbidden from touching weapons under penalty of death, but the Spartans armed them for the war with the Persians. After the war, to address this contradiction, the Spartans pretended they were going to free said slaves, but instead killed them all.

--Rostrum
