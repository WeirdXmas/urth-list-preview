Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

1997-06-28 Kieran Mullen #

Argues that Quetzal cannot fill the Moses role because his serpentine image marks him as devilish, and that Wolfe's salvation story need not follow Biblical patterns. Responds to Alice Turner's question about casting Quetzal as Moses, and asks which science fiction novels use non-Christian spiritual traditions on a cosmic scale, citing Simmons's Hyperion.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Quetzal's serpentine image makes him devilish, not a Moses figure

He is never really seen as a leader of the people. His image is primarily serpentine, and therefore devilish in this mythology (IMO). I don't think that Moses led the Hebrews through the desert so they could get eaten in the promised land :-).

I also don't mean to press the point to strongly. Symbolism is not allegory. We don't have a Mr. Goodworks and a Ms. Gossip running around. It also occurs to me that Wolfe's soteriology (story of being saved) doesn't have to rigorously following the Biblical paradigms.

Can anyone think of sf novels that follow other spiritual traditions off planet? I think it's in Simmons _Hyperion_, where he replays the story of Abraham and Isaac. (There are lots of such novels that involve the Christian tradition). By "follow" I don't mean simply that there are Moslems or Buddhists on another planet. I mean novels that use their cosmology on an sf scale.

Kieran Mullen

1997-06-30 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Lists non-Christian religious elements in science fiction, naming Frank Herbert's Dune and Zelazny's Lord of Light and Creatures of Light and Darkness as examples. Responds to a request for non-Christian religion in SF by noting that a reference encyclopedia's religion section is mostly Christian and that the poster has not searched hard enough.
