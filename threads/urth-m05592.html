The thread: Roy C. Lackey reposted Marc Aramini's archived 2004 message quoting Gene Wolfe's private dismissal of Robert Graves's The White Goddess and King Jesus, to counter claims that Graves underlies Wolfe's work. Matthew Groves argued Graves is philosophically opposed to Wolfe and that Frazer's The Golden Bough is a better source, and Matthew Keeley agreed Frazer is more useful and pointed to an abridgment online. Lackey maintained Graves is a poor guide, Aramini argued Wolfe's reading is limited to a core of obvious influences, and thalassocrat and JWillard split over whether Wolfe's stories invite source-hunting. The thread ended without resolution, with JWillard asking to be removed from the list.
  • Roy C. Lackey: Graves is a poor guide to interpreting Wolfe, and Wolfe had not finished reading it
  • Matthew Groves: Graves is philosophically opposed to Wolfe, though useful for name etymologies
  • Marc Aramini: Wolfe's influences are a limited core of obvious books, not esoteric sources
  • Matthew Keeley: Frazer's Golden Bough is a better comparative-mythology source than Graves
  • thalassocrat: Wolfe seems to believe he can do better than his supposed models
  • JWillard: Wolfe's work invites source-hunting, citing Borges echoes such as Ultan

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Books, libraries and writing, Stories within stories

I asked Mr. Wolfe about a few books which have come up under discussion on the list as possible sources for his work. I thought some of us would be interested in his response, so I'll quote it. Keep in mind that I asked his opinion about the books, so he is just answering my questions.

"You asked about some books. I haven't read KING JESUS. I read enough of THE WHITE GODDESS (which is still around here somewhere) to conclude that Graves, with all his talent, was a crackpot. Have you read I, CLAUDIUS? Or HERCULES MY SHIPMATE? They're fine, fine books, both of them. I read one book by Mary Renault; I'm no longer sure of the title. It bored me halfway to madness, and I've never tried anything else."

I thought that was interesting.

Marc Aramini

2008-01-18 Matthew Groves replying to Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Robert Graves's The White Goddess is philosophically opposed to Wolfe's work, since it laments the move from goddess worship to Judeo-Christian belief, and that Graves's Greek myths book is useful for name etymologies. Responds to Roy C. Lackey's claim that Graves's book upsets long-held Wolfe interpretations by dismissing that concern and suggesting Frazer's The Golden Bough as a better source for Wolfe's comparative mythology.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Frazer's Golden Bough is a better source than Graves

"Upsets" nothing! Wolfe fans rejoice; you don't have to read The White Goddess! Philosophically, TWG is pretty much diametrically opposed to Wolfe anyway, TWG being in part a sort of lamenting of the great apostasy that is the shift from worship of the Goddess to Judeo-Christian worship.

I do recommend Graves's book on the Greek myths -- very handy for the etymology of names. But it sounds like Frazer's The Golden Bough is probably a better source Wolfe's reception of comparative mythology and religion.

2008-01-18 Matthew Keeley replying to Matthew Groves #

Argues that Frazer's The Golden Bough is a more useful source than Graves's book for Wolfe's comparative mythology, noting Frazer was critical of Christianity and that his material is hard to access. Responds to Matthew Groves's recommendation of Graves and his remark about Wolfe's reception of comparative mythology, pointing to the abridgment available on Project Gutenberg.
informs · source-and-allusion

Not that Frazer is much closer to Wolfe's views - he was very critical of Christianity. Parts of the Golden Bough are direct attempts to undermine Christian beliefs. Alas, I doubt any of us will read the whole thing - it's about thirteen volumes, and the only
readily-available print version is the Oxford University Press abridgment (which is still 800 pages long). Frazer's own two-volume abridgment is out-of-print and apparently doesn't have some of the most interesting and controversial material. The abridgment is available online at Project Gutenberg though:

http://www.gutenberg.org/etext/3623

Do check it out. Unlike The White Goddess, Frazer is fun to read.

-Matt

2008-01-25 JWillard replying to thalassocrat #

Argues that Wolfe's work invites source-hunting, citing the Kafkaesque torture device in The Book of the New Sun and the blind librarian Ultan as echoing Borges. Responds to Marc Aramini's claim that Wolfe would not benefit from rewriting Graves, Nabokov and others, by contending that resisting such explorations treats stories as sealed units, and ends by asking to be removed from the list.
disputes · source-and-allusion

And some people would seem to prefer that Wolfe's work was parthenogenic, thus blocking the troublesome avenues of unexplored possibilities in the work of an author who seems to constantly invite speculation. I would be more sympathetic to such an argument if this were Isaac Asimov. But this is a book where a torture device in the corner is lifted from Kafka and the blind librarian Ultan seems to invoke Jorge Luis Borges. I have never understood the cut-off point here or the threat felt by such explorations, but there does seem to be a correspondence between the act of 'faith' and the belief in 'story' as a self-enclosed, inviolate unit. It has the whiff of a flat-Urth mentality.

Forgive me if I step away from the dark ages. If anyone is still in charge, please remove me from this list.
