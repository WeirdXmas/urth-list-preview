What's So Great About Ushas?

Urth list · 6 messages · 2008-07-21 to 2008-07-22

The thread: The thread, titled around Ushas, began with b sharp asking whether The Book of the New Sun is cosmic horror and whether Severian is deceived or Christ-like. David Duffy argued that Severian is a Christ-like figure whose conduct is the best possible in a fallen subcreation, citing Wolfe's own statements. The discussion then drifted to Dan Simmons' Hyperion, which Dan'l Danehy-Oakes recommended and Matthew King warned was gory horror. Lane Haygood and Russ Allbery disputed that label, calling the books space opera and noting they are not gory. The thread ended without resolving the Ushas question.
  • b sharp: the cosmic horror reading has merit but is not yet convincing; asks whether Severian is deceived or Christ-like
  • David Duffy: Severian is a Christ-like figure, and the Hierogrammates' plans are right for him to follow
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: recommends Simmons' Hyperion and Fall of Hyperion as among the finest sf novels in twenty years
  • Matthew King: warns that Simmons' Hyperion books are gory horror despite the sf setting
  • Lane Haygood: disputes the gory-horror label; the Tree of Pain is unpleasant but not gory
  • Russ Allbery: Simmons' books are traditional space opera, heart-rending in places, and far less gory than other fiction

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Suffering and sacrifice

2008-07-21 b sharp #

Reflects on a month-long thread about whether The Book of the New Sun is a cosmic horror story, saying John Watkins's view of evil triumphing within Catholic theology has merit but is not yet convincing. Cites Father Inire's teratoid symbols, the witches' and Cumaean's gnostic symbols, and Ushas and Yesod, asking whether Severian is deceived or a Christ-like figure.
questions · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: BotNS as triumph of evil within Catholic theology

Aside from the recent snippiness I think this thread had been interesting and productive. A lot of ideas have come out I never would have predicted when I started it last month. I give credit to John Watkins; I haven't heard his approach before; sorry I impugned his motives.

It makes sense that some secular humanists might find BotNS a (did Dave Tallman call it a..) cosmic horror story where evil triumphs in the end. It makes sense because many consider Christianity and the Bible to be a cosmic horror story. But John Watkins seems to be taking a different approach, finding this story a triumph of evil within aspects of Roman Catholic theology. (sorry not familiar with The Shrike).

I have harped on ancient mythological gods and monsters as antagonists or at least competitors to modern Judeo-Christianity but I guess Gnosticism could also fall into the category? It seems surely relevant that Father Inire's area is marked by teratoid (monstrous) symbols while the witches' and Cumaean's tower is marked with gnostic symbols. Not being a religious scholar I don't know how those might relate to Kabbalism and Tzadkiel and Yesod and Ushas. But it makes me wonder if John Watkins is onto something or not.

Still I'm not ready concede this as a horror story. Other Wolfe stories have left me with a feeling of horror and dread but not this one. Have I been fooled by The Great Deceiver into a toleration of genocide? Or is this really meant to be a story about a Christ-like character who helped humanity approach heavenly divinity?

-bsharp

2008-07-21 Lane Haygood replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Recommends reading Simmons' Endymion and The Rise of Endymion to complete the series, while noting the first two Hyperion books are stronger, and disputes Dan'l Danehy-Oakes' description of Simmons' novels as gory horror. Points out the genres involved (cyberpunk, Chaucer and Keats homage, mystic fantasy) and says the Tree of Pain is unpleasant but not gory.
disputes

And in all fairness, one should as well pick up "Endymion" and "The Rise of Endymion" to round out the series, although the first two Hyperion Cantos are superior.

But "gory horror novels?" Really? I could see pseudo-cyberpunk (the first two), homage to Chaucer and Keats, and mystic fantasy (the last two), but I don't recall there being a whole lot of gore. The descriptions of the Tree of Pain were not pleasant, but were hardly gory, at least as I am imagining "gore."

Lane

2008-07-22 David Duffy replying to b sharp #

Argues that the Book of the New Sun is not a cosmic horror story, since Severian is a Christ-like figure whose good conduct is the best possible within a fallen subcreation, and suggests that the Hierogrammates' plans are right for him to follow. Points to Wolfe's own statements and The Urth of the New Sun, and compares Pirate Freedom, The Fifth Head of Cerberus and Peace, which imply technological bootstrapping is bad, while noting a book the poster has not read, Attending Dedalus.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Severian is an imitatio christi; The Hierogrammates' plans are the best possible; Book of the New Sun is not cosmic horror

I haven't read it, but isn't this the thesis of _Attending Dedalus_?

If you look at _Pirate Freedom_ and _Cerberus_ and perhaps _Peace_, Wolfe does write quite extended horror stories, and the former two seem to imply that kind of bootstrapping via technology is a bad thing.

But Wolfe has explicitly said, and shown esp in UotNS, that Severian is an imitatio christi, so he is "good" in as far as he can be. Using Tolkien's terminology, Wolfe has a subcreation that isn't the best of possible worlds, like ours is ;). That is, the Hierogrammates and their actions are the best that can possibly be in this iteration, and so Severian should fall in with their plans.

David Duffy.
