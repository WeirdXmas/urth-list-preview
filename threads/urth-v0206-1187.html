Some problems if the Pancreator/Outsider is theHoly

Urth list · 10 messages · 2002-06-10 to 2002-06-12

2002urthThe Book of the New SunMatthew MalthouseAdam StephanidesJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-OakesJames JordanMichael Straight
The thread: The thread asked whether The Book of the New Sun is set in a prior universe, with the Pancreator and Outsider identified with the Holy, as James Jordan proposed. Jordan argued the Briah cosmos is an earlier incarnation of the Second Person and cited a Wolfe interview saying the universe is a long past one we repeat. Jeff Wilson, Matthew Malthouse and Adam Stephanides disputed the reading, citing the internal evidence of a far-future Earth, the spoiled sense of wonder, and Wolfe's own introduction and appendix describing Severian's era as 'futurity'. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Michael Straight pressed the textual case, with the thread ending in impasse over whether Wolfe's framing text settles the time direction.
  • James Jordan: Briah's Theoanthropos is an earlier incarnation of the Second Person, and Wolfe's interview places the setting in a long past universe
  • Jeff Wilson: the crucifixion repetition may be allegory, Yesod is a previous universe per Mantiss, and the appendix contradicts earlier claims
  • matthew.malthouse: internal evidence puts the book in Earth's far future, and a distant past strains credibility
  • Adam Stephanides: identifying the Pancreator with the Holy destroys wonder and the uncanny, and the original books show no previous universe
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Wolfe's introduction and appendix describe future buildings from the distant past, making history cyclic
  • Michael Straight: Wolfe's introductions call Severian's era 'futurity', which bears on the time-direction question even if Wolfe erred

Themes: Time loops and causality, The dying sun and the cosmos, Deep time and ruins

2002-06-10 James Jordan #

Argues that the Theoanthropos in the Briah cosmos is an earlier incarnation of the Second Person, the original Conciliator of which Severian is an application, and asks which textual hints show the manuscript comes from a prior cosmos. Responds to a prior post on whether the Briah events predate Noah's promise, admitting that Wolfe's liberties make the hints hard to sort out.
proposes · theology-and-religion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Theoanthropos is an earlier incarnation of the Second Person; Severian is an application of the original Conciliator

Yes.

Well, the fact that is does somehow pass through the singularity, on board the Ship perhaps, or in the Yesod Library, is the intra-textual indicator that these events are in our past. That's always the problem with Wolfe: which of the tiny hints are important and which of the hints are not hints at all?

Protestant missionaries of the Theoanthropos. Well, like the Apollo picture, somehow this earlier BangganB of the cosmos is much like ours, but not quite the same. I do think we have an incarnation of the 2nd person of God in this earlier cosmos, the aforementioned Theoanthropos, the original Conciliator of which Severian is an application, but as Dan'l mentioned, from some Roman Catholic perspectives the atonement of the Son is "eternal" so it could "happen" in time more than once. If this was not in Wolfe's mind, then I doubt if we are supposed to press the problem of a repeated crucifixion.
But then, if we allow Wolfe the "liberty" to have a previous incarnation and atonement-of-infinite-worth in the Briah cosmos, then how are we to "know" that Severian's manuscript is from a previous cosmos and has made it into ours? Which "hint" do we take as simply a liberty we grant the author, and which "hint" we take as internal to the argument of the narrative? After all, maybe Wolfe just "somehow" got Severian's book from the future and "somehow" (golden spectacles?) was able to translate it, and we are not supposed to ponder further? Or, maybe it fell back to him through the corridors of time, instead of proceeding forward to him from the previous BanggnaB. Is there another "hint" that resolves the question? This is the problem and frustration when reading Wolfe!

JBJordan Nutria

2002-06-10 Jeff Wilson #

Questions whether a claimed fact about the Outsider and the Pancreator in Wolfe's books is documented, and argues that the crucifixion repetition could be read as allegory rather than literal. Points to the appendix of Shadow reversing earlier statements and to the problems of a book traveling backward in time, which conflict with promises attributed to God.
disputes · theology-and-religion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: The crucifixion is allegorical, not literally repeated

Did he explain why he has completely reversed his statements in the appendix of Shadow?

Where is this "fact" documented?

Whay can't this just be regarded as allegorical? The crucifixion would not be repeated so much as represented, it avoids all problems with time-binding statements and promises attributed to G-d, doesn't make a liar out of the translator, and doesn't require the Book to travel half a universe backward, get translated, then come 1 1/2 forward to be published.

2002-06-11 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that identifying the Pancreator or Outsider with the Holy spoils the sense of wonder and the uncanny in The Book of the New Sun, comparing it to revealing the Dying Earth as a dying other planet. Responds to James Jordan's one-word agreement, noting no sign in the original books that they occur in a previous universe and speculating Wolfe devised the idea later.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Pancreator or Outsider is the Holy

Textual and theological difficulties aside, I really don't like this idea. I won't say it ruins the books for me, but it does detract substantially from their sense of wonder and the uncanny. It's as if had declared that THE DYING EARTH was really the Dying
some-other-planet-that-happens-to-be-called-Earth.

Since, afaik, there's no indication in the original BotNS that the books are taking place in a previous universe, I'm tempted to speculate (and hope) that this was an idea Wolfe thought of after writing the original books.

--Adam

2002-06-12 Matthew Malthouse #

Argues that placing The Book of the New Sun in a distant past of the Whorl strains credibility, since the book's ancient world mirrors ours too closely and needs extra holes or wraps to explain the manuscript. Responds to James Jordan's quoted exchange with Wolfe about a long past universe, citing Wolfe's remark that the setting is something we repeat, and adds a playful idea about Ultan's library as part of L-space.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: The Book of the New Sun set in a distant past of Urth

Thanks.

But that does seem like mischief because the explanation of the origin of the manuscript is in the book, so we have to add a further wrap around it to account for the book arriving in our hands.

On a metaphysical level I also cavil somewhat - the similarities of the books ancient past with our world are too similar. Either it's not just cyclic but the repetition is near identical or we have to interpolate additional holes through which things like the moon picture have been moved.

For sheer fun I quite like the idea of Ultan's library being part of L space. Just as the shelves reach might the archive of the House Absolute so too one might hope to turn an unexpected corner and come across a russet furred Librarian subjecting a minor grimoire to some remedial book-binding.

But no, placing tBotNS in a distant past distresses me. It's too complex. Not that we expect anything but complexity from Wolfe; but this is purposely complex and purposelessness is not a lupine characteristic.

Matthew
