Themes: Names and their meanings

1999-02-25 Dan Parmenter #

Questions whether the name Triskele means Triskele is literally a dog, testing a proposed rule that Wolfe's names mean exactly what they say, and points out that the Salamander is no newt, so the rule does not always hold. Builds on the earlier quoted remark that Urth's animals are alien replacements. Playful and punning, with the Salamander joke about burning bridges.
questions · source-and-allusion · theories: Wolfe's names mean exactly what they say; Triskele is a modified dog

From: "Kevin J. Maroney" <[email]>

By the first rule of Wolfean naming ("everything is exactly what it says it is") is it fair then to assume that Triskele is indeed a dog? Possibly a "modified" dog, but a dog no less? The Wolfean Naming Rule clearly doesn't always work, since the Salamander is no newt (though it does resemble a certain Newt after a fashion, it flames and flames until its flames burn its bridges and it conveniently disappears :-)

1999-02-25 C R Culver #

Explains that the salamander in Wolfe's work comes from ancient Greek belief that it could survive fire, and that Wolfe took it from Borges's Book of Imaginary Beings, as he did with other Borges creatures like the Baldanders. Responds to Shellac's remark about the Wolfean Naming Rule by noting the salamander is no newt, offering a real-world source for the name.
informs · source-and-allusion

<<The Wolfean Naming Rule clearly doesn't always work, since the Salamander is no newt (though it does resemble a certain Newt after a fashion, it flames and flames
until its flames burn its bridges and it conveniently disappears :-)>>

The Salamander, according to ancient Greek belief, could survive in fire. Jorge Luis Borges, in _El Libro de Seres Imaginarios_ (The Book of Imaginary Beings), wrote an article on the Salamander and Wolfe took it, like many of Borges' creatures (Baldanders, etc.) for the BotNS.

- Christopher R. Culver <[email]>
