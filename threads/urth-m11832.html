Father Inire: teratoid

Urth list · 21 messages · 2010-12-14 to 2010-12-16

2010urthThe Book of the New SunJordon FlatoJerry FriedmanRyan DunnLee BermanGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffJames WynnSon of WitzJeff Wilson
The thread: Readers debated the teratoid glyph in Father Inire's house, which Severian cannot read: is it a monster warning, an alien word for mirrors, a misreading of 'teratogen', a 'Christ' riddle, or Inire's maker's mark? David Stockhoff, Jerry Friedman and Lee Berman offered rival readings, and Jordon Flato and Son of Witz argued over whether the missing word is 'Christ' or 'last'. Lee Berman then proposed a gnostic Urth with Inire and Tzadkiel as opposed avatars, which Son of Witz and Jeff Wilson took up alongside the Green Man and Ushas. Gerry Quinn disputed the verticality pattern and the maker's-mark reading, and the thread ended with the maker's-mark claim rejected and no consensus on the glyph.
  • David Stockhoff: glyph is a monster warning and the mirror-born monster is the viewer's own reflection
  • Lee Berman: glyph is a misreading of 'teratogen' and a gnostic Urth framing Inire and Tzadkiel as opposed avatars, later rejected as a maker's mark
  • Jerry Friedman: glyph is the alien word for mirrors
  • Son of Witz: the riddle word is 'last' and the foes of the Increate form a verticality metaphor
  • Gerry Quinn: the verticality pattern is unconvincing and no other text links the glyph to Inire as a mark
  • Jeff Wilson: Green Man depicts a future Ushas humanity beyond sacrifice, supported by Wolfe interviews

Themes: Stories within stories, Mirrors, pictures and art, Gods, angels and the Increate

2010-12-14 David Stockhoff replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the teratoid glyph in Father Inire's house is a monster warning, a 'don't come in unless you risk being eaten' sign, and that its link to Hethor comes from mirrors used to summon monsters. Responds to Lee Berman, who had argued the sign rests on a confusion between 'teratoid' and 'teratogen' and tied Father Inire to Hethor, by granting that mirror use could cause monstrous births.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Teratoid glyph is a monster warning sign; Mirror use caused monstrous births in First Empire

That's not far off from my thought, but of course you have to know the numerology to "get" it. The meaning of a monster sign could not be more simple: don't come in here unless you want to risk being eaten. House servants appreciated this, I'm sure. Any association with Hethor comes from the fact that mirrors can be used to summon monsters, as Hethor did. But there is always the possibility that in the First Empire people still---perhaps even especially, if birth rates were a problem---cared about causing monstrous births. Who knows what occupational hazards regular use of a mirror might cause.

Yes, some may be sentient, or even human---who knows? Perhaps the worst monster to emerge from a mirror is the one we see there?

Proposes that Urth in The Book of the New Sun is a gnostic universe ruled by an evil demiurge, with Father Inire as its central avatar and Tzadkiel as the opposed archangel, forming a yin-yang pair. proposes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Urth is a gnostic universe ruled by a demiurge; Father Inire is the demiurge's central avatar; Tzadkiel replaces Inire as his opposite counterpart; Urth and the ship form a gnostic yin-yang

Well, I have some thoughts but they are difficult to put into words. I have been trying with my Father Inire theory.

For me, this is a gnostic, pre-Christian universe (some prefer it to be a futuristic post-Christian era of our own universe; I dunno).

As such, a nasty Urth is ruled by an essentially evil, materialistic demiurge who has created and who controls everything. (some pagans posit that this demiurge was Jahweh, the genocidal, jealous god of the Old Testament.) This demiurge controls everything with various agents in various shapes and forms from the noble Ultan to the demonic Hethor. But all are aspects of the demiurge. Important to notice that his mirrors produce heavenly images, proving that he isn't really all bad. The central avatar of this being in our story is, of course, Father Inire.

Conversely, gnostic heaven is ruled by an essentially good, spiritual archangel, Tzadkiel who mostly stays above the fray (obeying the Prime Directive, heh) but not entirely. Both Yesod and The Ship (while travelling) exist outside Urth and the material universe and so may be considered as spiritual realms. Important to notice that mirror sails on the ship produce monsters leading to bloody, deadly conflict on the Ship and Yesod, proving that the heavenly archangel isn't really all good. (Hethor's mirrors produce monsters because they are sail scraps).

Together, Urth and The Ship/Yesod, Inire and Tzadkiel essentially form a gnostic Yin Yang symbol, dark balancing light with a mirror image dot of the opposite embedded on each side.

I agree with Jeff that Father Inire disappears from the story because his work on Urth is done. But I can't go with the idea that he just dies and is gone. For me he is replaced in the story by his similar but opposite counterpart, Tzadkiel. They, like Dionysus/Great God Pan, are opposites but aspects of the same essential thing or being or greater god who is divided.

I'm not so good at religious, especially Christian, philosophy. But I get the impression Gene Wolfe believes in the reality of the polytheistic pagan gods and the gnostic pre-Christ world of angels and demons. A universe ruled by a divided god. This is our BotNS story.

I get the impression Wolfe feels we on Earth are lucky enough to have gotten Christ, so not only is there forgiveness of our sins, a reconcilliation between God and man, but also a joining of the various opposite aspects of gods from our gnostic past. They have been reconciled into one true, unified God. (that never quite happens in Briah or on Ushas).

Argues that the teratoid symbol in Father Inire's chambers is not meant as his maker's mark, since alien glyphs in the book are meant to be meaningless to humans, and that Severian's reading of them reveals his own view of Inire as a monster. disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Teratoid symbol is not Inire's maker mark; Severian considers Father Inire a monster

I don't interpret the teratoid symbol as Inire's maker mark. I only mentioned that possibility in response to somebody who was asserting their only possible meaning was as a factory stamp or something like that.

I think all alien glyphs in the story are meant by Wolfe to be incomprehensible and meaningless to human beings. That includes us, the readers. I consider Severian's meager interpretation of the alien symbols he sees to be the best he can do to add HIS meaning to them.
