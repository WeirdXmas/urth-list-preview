Rudesind/Inire/Lunar Picture

Urth list · 35 messages · 2010-07-27 to 2010-08-01

2010urthThe Book of the New SunLane HaygoodJohn WatkinsRyan DunnJerry FriedmanLee BermanAdam ThorntonMo Holkar / UKGJosh GellerAntónio Pedro MarquesDavid StockhoffJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: The thread began as a debate over whether Father Inire, Rudesind's master, is a time traveler, with Ryan Dunn arguing for time loops and mirrors while John Watkins and others disputed the textual support. Lee Berman proposed Inire as a fallen, non-human form of Tzadkiel, and Lee Berman, Jerry Friedman and David Stockhoff pushed back on textual grounds. Later posts drifted into the angel-and-wings analogy and then into film recommendations such as Wings of Desire and City of Angels. The discussion ended without agreement, trailing off into off-topic film chat.
  • Ryan Dunn: Inire is a time traveler who built mirrors and is Rudesind's master, with a time loop in the Atrium scene
  • John Watkins: The text does not support time travel or Inire as Rudesind's master; disguises fool only those who never met the person
  • Lee Berman: Inire is a disguised non-human fallen form of Tzadkiel, and one can serve as one's own master
  • Jeff Wilson: Textual evidence ties Inire to Rudesind and the Botanic Gardens, and the angel plot leaves motives unexplained
  • Jerry Friedman: Isangoma is not metallic and the Moses reference need not make Inire Isangoma
  • David Stockhoff: Characters reveal themselves through speech, and the cacogen choice is only metaphor

Themes: Time loops and causality, Hidden identities and parentage, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting

Proposes that Inire, the Father of Rudesind's master, is a time traveler who built the mirrors that carry people to other places and times, and questions why Inire never mastered them. proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Inire is a time traveler who built the mirrors

INIRE AS RUDESIND'S MASTER:

"Rudesind, we already know the answers to the question you think we are going to ask. We know your master is what the people call a cacogen, and that for whatever reason, he is one of those few who have chosen to cast their lots entirely with humanity, remaining on Urth as a human being."

INIRE AS TIME TRAVELER:

Are cacogens immortal? If so, then my theory may be unfounded. But...

"Have you heard of the mirrors of Father Inire? Do you know how they work?"

"I've heard of Father Inire's Mirror, but I couldn't tell you where I heard about it. You're supposed to be able to step into it, like you'd step into a doorway, and step out on a star. I don't think it's real."

"The mirrors are real. I've seen them. Up until now I always thought of them in much the same way you did?as if they were a ship, but much faster. Now I'm not nearly so sure. Anyway, a certain friend of mine stepped between those mirrors and vanished. I was watching him. It was no trick and no superstition; he went wherever the mirrors take you."

Are we to assume Inire built these mirrors that take you to other places (and times from what I can gather) yet has never mastered them?

2010-07-29 Jeff Wilson replying to Adam Thornton #

Notes that the Wolfe-adjacent angel plot never explains why the fallen figures wanted to return to Heaven without repenting, and recommends the film The Rapture as an even-handed treatment of theodicy. Responds to Adam Thornton's chat about Wings of Desire and Dogma, drawing on the thread's discussion of the plenary indulgence and whether the fallen angels needed to become mortal.
chats

I didn't think it was necessary for them to become mortal to partake of the plenary indulgence, but it would have been kind of futile trying to stop transcendent spirits. And there never was an explanation of why Loki and Bartleby wanted to go back to Heaven without repenting - I suppose that Jesus did not die to save fallen angels from sin.

While we are on the subject of movies about the more ineffable Christian spirituality, I recommend netflixing _The Rapture_. As far as I know, it's the only R-rated biblical epic and is one of the few treatments of theodicy that can be considered even-handed without being (too) ambiguous or noncommittal.
