Atlantis and Gondwanaland

Urth list · 24 messages · 2011-01-02 to 2011-01-04

The thread: The thread asked where the Shadow Children in Wolfe's Shadow novels came from, and why lost-land names like Atlantis and Gondwanaland appear in their lessons. Lee Berman first linked the Cyclops episode to the question of who is really human, and David Stockhoff answered that Wolfe lets myths hold truth alongside lies. Positions split: Tony Ellis argued the Shadow Children are telepathic shapechangers who picked up names from passing starship crews, Gerry Quinn argued they are degenerate humans, and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes proposed a non-telepathic star-travelling race. The thread ended in an impasse, with Berman saying all readings had equal merit given the contradictory text.
  • Lee Berman: Shadow Children are forgotten colonists or part of a wider evil-in-Creation reading; evil is not purely human, an outside presence draws it out
  • David Stockhoff: Wolfe's myths hold truth alongside lies; Shadow Children may predate humans, and Wolfe's evil is human and rational
  • Tony Ellis: Shadow Children are deluded telepathic shapechangers who picked up names from passing starship crews
  • Gerry Quinn: Shadow Children are degenerate humans, and the human reading is the only one without serious problems
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Shadow Children descend from a non-telepathic star-travelling race humans have not met
  • Marc Aramini: Asked whether the false coin symbolizes Severian's deception and undercuts the high-and-low theme

Themes: Stories within stories, Myth and folklore sources, Doubles, twins and clones

2011-01-02 Lee Berman #

Proposes a hidden link between the Cyclops episode David reads in Fifth Head of Cerberus and the list of possible homelands, suggesting the characters may be blind to not being men. Grounds this on the Noman trick in the Odyssey and on the Atlantis and Gondwanaland references in Number Five's lesson and the Shadow Child's list.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Characters are blind to not being men

Perusing the text to respond to Gerry's post, I found an interesting section. During their lessons, David is peeking at "Polyphemus, The Cyclops and Odysseus". This story involves blinding the giant and tricking him by pretending Odysseus' name is "Noman". Sort of a stretch but I wonder if this could hint that all involved (even Dr. Veil) are blind to the fact that they are not men. If not that, it must connote something.

Anyway, the main thing is that during this lesson, Number Five says he glosses upon "the Etruscans, Atlantis, and the tenacity and expansionist tendencies of a hypothetical technological culture occupying Gondwanaland".

As has been discussed a couple times in the past year, the Old Wise One Shadow Child provides the rather impossible list of possible home nations on earth as, "Atlantis or Mu-- or Gondwanaland, Africa, Poictesme, or The Country Of Friends."

What might be the connection between these two references?

2011-01-03 David Stockhoff replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Shadow Children and similar pre-human myths in The Fifth Head of Cerberus are not literal history for Wolfe but stories humans tell that generate meaning, a view tied to his interest in myth. Responds to Lee Berman's list of readings by explaining that Wolfe lets myths hold truth alongside lies, citing Margaret Atwood's line that religion is a shadow of God.
disputes · myth-and-structure · theology-and-religion · theories: Shadow Children are myths not literal history

Of course I meant them in a limited sense (i.e., limited to Fifth Head), but they do seem to recur in Wolfe. I'd relate them to his general idea about myths, how ancient myths reflect attempts by intelligent people to understand the world and approach divinity, etc. Stories we tell ourselves about who-came-before are all grist for Wolfe's mill, or meat for his fangs, or .... In other words, I wouldn't go so far as to say Wolfe thinks giants (or shadows) did once roam the earth, or that they did on Urth, or that they did on Blue, etc. Just that we humans always talk about such ideas and they hold---and generate---meaning for us. (Incidentally, have you ever noticed how sickeningly popular prophecies are in Hollywood and cheap fiction? Wolfe shows comparatively little interest in them---he prefers myths about the past.) I just read Margaret Atwood's latest novel, Year of the Flood. She says (probably quoting?Plato?by way of?someone else) "All religion is a shadow of God, and shadows of God are not God." I think Wolfe agrees, and this lets him play with myths while suggesting that they hold truth along with lies (both "true" lies and false ones).

Links this to Severian's musing on high and low at the end of The Citadel of the Autarch, asking whether it undercuts the high-and-low wiping out in Urth of the New Sun.

2011-01-03 David Stockhoff replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that nothing in Wolfe is truly fake, since Vodalus, the fake coin, the autarch and the Hieros games are false yet also real and true. Responds to Marc Aramini's coin post by extending the view that all myths are more or less true and all lies contain truth.
proposes · theories: Nothing in Wolfe is truly fake; All myths are more or less true; All lies contain truth

You mean aside from your medieval prejudice against cannibalism? More seriously, I question whether anything on Urth is high. Can you give an example? And I would theorize that nothing is truly fake in Wolfe. This ties to my current thread with Lee and a topic I return to often: all myths are more or less true. All lies contain truth. As fake as Vodalus and the coin and the autarch and the Commonwealth and the games of the Hieros are---all of them, in every sense and on every level---they are also real and true. Or so my theory forces me to conclude. The open question is what that means.

2011-01-03 David Stockhoff replying to Lee Berman #

Proposes several readings of The Fifth Head of Cerberus: the Shadow Children may have preceded humans on Earth and be our descendants, and Dr. Veil may merely be repeating literary myths. Builds on Lee Berman's Cyclops remark about the character David; the poster says these ideas aim to make readers doubt what they think they know about human identity.
proposes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Shadow Children are humanity's ancestors; Dr. Veil merely repeats literary myths

There are a few thoughts that I had on reading FHOC and I have not moved forward much from them:

(1) The Shadow Children preceded us on Earth, having left Earth before we evolved. We may be their descendants.

(2) #1, but as an in-joke.

(3) The possibility of #1 is meant to make us imagine sharing a planet with a closely-related species, or fairies, or whatever, i.e., induce a sort of disorienting paranoia about our own identity as humans. (As you suggest for David et al.)

(4) He doesn't know what he's talking about. He's just repeating myths---literary ones that are becoming "real" ones. Perhaps he got them from reading human minds or something.

(5) All these are meant to make us question what we think we know.

2011-01-03 Jordon Flato replying to Adam Thornton (IO) #

Argues that Severian's movement through the books is a progressive shift of allegiance from earthly power toward a higher source, the increate, with the counterfeit coin given him after he reaches the throne marking that the Autarchy is not his final purpose. Grounds this in the first coin from Vodalus that Severian vows to serve, and in the later coin he is told is fake, and says the idea was developed with Witz.
proposes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's allegiance moves toward the increate; Counterfeit coin shows Autarchy is not his purpose

I was talking over this exact thing with Witz the other day. Witz and I are both of the mind that there is a progressive movement in Severian's Character toward giving allegiance to a higher and higher source as the book moves forward, or maybe is shedding layers of earthly allegiance and discovering a real impulse toward the increate. I think there are a lot of examples of this throughout the books. Early on, Severian receives the coin, as you say, from Vodalus, whom he internally vows to serve from that point on. And arguably that choice sets him on his path toward the throne. So no having attained the throne, reached the pinnacle of urthly power, where many would be more than satisfied, he's given another coin, which reminds him of that first coin, but which at this point he recognizes (is told) is counterfeit. For me, I see this as a final symbol of Severian being reminded, through symbol, that the Autarchy isn't his final purpose, that he serves something higher. I'm running short on time right now, so I can't go into this more subtly, and I think there is more there to say.

2011-01-03 David Stockhoff replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe's evil is human and rational rather than a conscious eternal evil like Satan in Paradise Lost, citing Baldanders, Typhon, and Severian's remarks that wrongdoers differ little from others. Extends the point to Abaia and Erebus, likening them to hungry aliens, and concludes that the Neighbors, being sentient, are capable of evil.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Wolfe's evil is human and rational; Abaia and Erebus are hungry aliens; Evil requires rationality so Neighbors can be evil

I guess I'm thinking of a conscious Eternal Evil, not one that says "I am Evil" but one that, like Satan in Paradise Lost, is self-pitying and prideful and jealous and will do anything to gain power over others, even destroying the world so no one else can rule it. I don't think this kind of evil exists, because there is enough stupidity and callous ignorance in the world to explain all the symptoms of Eternal Evil. (Occam's Parsimonious Razor.)

The evil we see in Wolfe is human and rational (usually---you could make a case for the woman who turns her children into furniture). His characters are often ordinary people trying not to do evil, and who know the chance for evil lies within themselves. Those characters who do evil are often specifically described by Severian as being no or little worse than others who succeed in avoiding doing evil---just a little hungrier or more desperate. Baldanders' cruel experiments are an exception, but even he is not a devil; he does nothing to humans he would not do to himself. Typhon may have superpowers, but if he doesn't, the evil of his empire lies in his servants, not himself.

Occasionally, evil is distant and ambiguous. We may think of Cthulhu when we read of Abaia and Erebus, but isn't he really just a large hungry alien? It's not his fault if humans will betray other humans to supply him with dinner or if his emanations give sensitive people nightmares. While these creatures may be evil in the sense of being mostly appetite---and having no interest in the divine spark of humanity---whose intelligence goes toward luring prey the way a deep-sea fish lures smaller fish or a spider builds a web, I get no sense that these beings are any different. Just bigger and capable of defeating and eating humans.

Evil requires rationality. The Neighbors, being sentient, are therefore capable of evil.

2011-01-04 Tony Ellis replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Shadow Children in the Long Sun books are deluded fantasists who wrongly believe they are human, since telepathic shapechangers could naturally come to think they are the beings they imitate. Grounds this in Sandwalker's observation that they are bent, weak and run from men, and in the Old Wise One's later rebuttal of their claims about themselves.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Shadow Children mistakenly believe they are human

They also say "'we are taller than you, and stronger. And wrapped in terrible glory... our glance withers, and we sing death to our enemies."

None of this is true. The reality, as Sandwalker muses, is that they are bent and weak and ill-looking, and run from men.

The Shadow Children are crackheaded fantasists. They like to *think* they are the humans. Later it is the Old Wise One himself who rebuts this.

My reading is that it would be quite natural for a race of telepathic, emphatic shapechangers to mistakenly believe that they are the thing they have tried to imitate.
