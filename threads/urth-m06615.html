Severian as a student

Urth list · 15 messages · 2008-05-31 to 2008-06-06

2008urthThe Book of the New SunLane HaygoodTony EllisthalassocratAdam ThorntonJeff WilsonDave TallmanRoy C. LackeyJohn Smith
The thread: The thread began with Dave Tallman arguing that Severian feigned poor memory as a student under Palaemon, a reading Roy Lackey and Jeff Wilson took up while debating whether textual inconsistencies were Wolfe's errors, Severian's omissions, or redaction slips. Roy Lackey insisted the errors lie within the single text, and Tony Ellis defended the doeskin/manskin slip as an ordinary copy-editing mistake. A side debate on human skin as fabric, with Lane Haygood and Jeff Wilson on scarcity versus habituation, and a later exchange on whether the Conciliator is Christ-like, both stalled without resolution.
  • Dave Tallman: Severian's memory is deliberately feigned as a student and his inconsistencies come from unjudged rewriting
  • Roy C. Lackey: inconsistencies are errors within the single text, and the Conciliator cannot be equated with Christ
  • Jeff Wilson: Roche/Drotte error is redaction hiding a fourth boy, and Severian would not be accepted as the Sleeper
  • Tony Ellis: the doeskin/manskin pouch error is an ordinary writer's word-substitution slip
  • Lane Haygood: scarcity and moral change could be one cause, and skin-leather may reflect resource loss or shock value
  • thalassocrat: the corpse-glove simile may be a deliberate link rather than a substitution

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Books, libraries and writing

2008-05-31 Dave Tallman #

Argues that Severian's supposed poor memory in the guild is a deliberate act, comparing him to I, Claudius feigning foolishness to survive among other apprentices. Supports this with Palaemon's remark, Severian's lies and feigned ignorance about Vodalus, and reconciles the pistol handoff from Vodalus to Hildegrin and Thea as consistent.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Severian feigned a poor memory as a student; Severian's reconstructed narrative is a second rewrite

The feat of reconstructing the entire "Book of the New Sun" from memory is sufficient to prove to me that Severian had a photographic memory as he claimed. Nobody would even attempt such a task without one. If they did, it wouldn't read like something carefully written; it would be full of mistakes and backtracks.

But when he left the guild, Master Palaemon tried to give him advice for the road. Severian missed it because he wasn't paying attention. Palaemon said, "Now you must do without it, but doubtless you would have forgotten everything anyway."

At first it may seem strange that Palaemon regarded him as having a poor memory. But there is a simple explanation. Early on, Severian must have learned that it was a poor strategy to be a teacher's pet in a school filled with other boys trained in the art of excruciation. He learned to act the part of a poor student. This is similar how "I, Claudius" had to act the part of a fool to survive.

"I had lied often to Master Gurloes and Master Palaemon, to Master Malrubius while he still lived, to Drotte because he was captain, to Roche because he was older and stronger than I, and to Eata an the other smaller apprentices because I hoped to make them respect me."

Here's an example of how it worked. "Outside, feigning ignorance, I asked Master Palaemon who Vodalus of the wood was." Severian got a rebuke for listening to the words of a client, and another for keeping his hands in his pocket (a deliberate act to deflect the Master's anger). Just as he intended, he got Drotte to tell him about Vodalus without revealing that he already knew the name.

They say it's more difficult to lie than to tell the truth because a liar has more to remember. Severian wouldn't have a problem with that. Then also suggests that discrepancies should be harmonized where possible instead of treating them as signs of lies. Severian should be most consistent when he is lying. If this book was a rewrite from memory he got a second chance to fix any mistakes.

The hand-off of the gun from Vodalus to Hildegrin or Thea is easily reconciled. Hildegrin said he couldn't use it, so it was clearly with the approval of Vodalus that Thea took it next. Thus he "gave the pistol to his mistress" even though he handed it first to Hildegrin.

2008-06-01 Roy C. Lackey replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that Severian's copy of the manuscript, written days or weeks after the original, cannot be compared with the original, so memory errors in the text are open to question. Cites the gun hand-off mentioned in Shadow and repeated in the Citadel appendix to suggest the error is Wolfe's, and notes that Severian's omissions are his worst deceptions.
disputes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Memory errors in the text are Wolfe's, not Severian's; Severian's worst deceptions are omissions

By "reconstructing", I can only conclude you are referring to the copy Severian made from memory shortly after boarding the Ship.

[snip]

Again, what do you mean by "rewrite" and "second chance"? His original manuscript was written shortly before boarding the Ship and sent to Ultan. Whichever version Wolfe "translated", we have no means of comparing the Ship copy to the original. The events recounted in the manuscript were first written down ten years after Sev became autarch. There was no running account made over the years that he could redact at leisure. The copy seems to have been written out only days or weeks after completing the original manuscript.

I made a longish post here years ago about mistakes and inconsistencies of memory in NEW SUN. IIRC, I concluded that the mistake about the gun was probably Wolfe's, not Sev's, because the second mention of the hand-off, this time to Thea, is repeated by Wolfe in the appendix to CITADEL. (It is possible that the first mention in SHADOW is a left-handed hint by Wolfe that Sev's memory isn't entirely to be trusted, but the revelation would be rather far from the source.)

Most of the mistakes of memory are about things that are not, in themselves, worth lying about. It doesn't really matter whether the pouch Dorcas sewed was made of doeskin or manskin, or if Roche or Drotte said they saw the pikes on the second page of the story. Unless they are all mistakes by Wolfe, the errors were put there by Wolfe to let the careful reader know that Sev's memory isn't quite as ostentatiously accurate as Sev loudly claims it to be.

Sev is an admitted liar, but his worst deceptions are his omissions.

-Roy

Builds on Roy Lackey's point that Severian's worst deceptions are omissions, responding to his quoted remarks on memory mistakes and lies.

2008-06-01 Dave Tallman replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the Ship copy of Severian's manuscript may have been written with resources an autarch could consult, so a second version would have let him catch inconsistencies. Responds to Dave Tallman's point that the surviving text cannot be compared with an original, reasoning that the close detail and style suggest a deliberate rewrite rather than ordinary memory recalling a book he just wrote.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Ship copy is a deliberate rewrite of the original manuscript

Perhaps I am making an unjustified assumption, but I thought it was a given that the copy made on the time-traveling Ship was the only copy that Wolfe could have obtained and "translated." If the copy given to Ultan is the version we are reading, then my point is not valid. Severian could consult all the resources available to an Autarch to write the first one. If this is the second manuscript, it's too good in the level of detail and style to be the work of a person with an ordinary memory attempting to re-create the book they just wrote.

Even though it was written soon after the other one, it still gave Severian a second chance to notice "Hey, what I wrote here is inconsistent with what I wrote there." The fact that it was rewritten within a short time makes the inconsistencies even more strange. His memory has to play tricks on him not only about the events themselves, but in his memory of writing about them.

2008-06-03 Roy C. Lackey replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the inconsistencies in Severian's manuscript must be errors within the single text rather than differences between copies, since his claimed perfect memory would conceal any mistake he later noticed. Grounds this in the doeskin/manskin pouch discrepancy and the question of who received Vodalus's gun, dismissing the copy-source debate as irrelevant.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's perfect memory conceals his own errors; Doeskin/manskin pouch discrepancy is a real mistake

For purposes of this discussion, it doesn't matter which document Wolfe translated. The text we have is all there is. The conceit is that, thanks to Severian's vaunted memory, the copy he made is identical in content to the original manuscript -- and we have no way of knowing otherwise.

We are talking about inconsistencies within the same document, not comparing documents. When Sev relates some incident in his document, then has occasion later in the document to make reference to the same incident, and the two accounts are inconsistent, then obviously at least one mistake has been made. It doesn't really matter whether Hildegrin or Thea received the gun from Vodalus; neither used it. The only question is whether Wolfe or Sev made the mistake.

If Sev ever had occasion to notice that he had contradicted himself, then he had the opportunity to correct his account by amending it, either by changing the first mention to agree with the second, or the other way around. And we would never know that he had made a mistake of memory. He takes such pains to insist that his memory is perfect that he would never knowingly demonstrate that it wasn't.

The doeskin/manskin pouch thing is not as easily dismissed as most of the other mistakes. Doeskin and manskin have very different connotations. It is almost impossible for me to believe that Wolfe made that mistake. It is almost equally difficult for me to believe that dear Dorcas would sew a pouch made from human skin to house the most celebrated religious icon on the planet.

-Roy

2008-06-04 Dave Tallman replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that Severian's memory in The Claw of the Conciliator is vivid but imperfect, and that his inconsistencies may come from unjudged rewriting, with a theory that he feigned poor memory as a student under Palaemon. Cites Severian's remark about recalling objects on a table, his claim to be Palaemon's favourite pupil, and Malrubius calling him careless.
proposes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Severian feigned poor memory as a student

There in a line in Claw which helps clarify matters:
"But I can remember more than many would credit: the position of each object on a table I walked past when I was a child, and even that I have recalled some scene to mind previously, and how that remembered incident differed from the memory I have of it now."

Severian's memory is vivid and detailed to him, but not necessarily perfect. He also mentions weak judgement. Perhaps he records the memories as they occur to him at the time of writing, not judging between versions if the differences are trivial.
It may also be that the rewriting just used memories of the act of writing itself in order to re-create the book quickly. Sev may not have exercised additional editorial judgement in the process.

The sheath of Terminus Est was manskin, which was an appropriate material for it. Probably Dorcas used doeskin and Sev's memories of the sheath got mixed in somewhere.

I found an additional reference to his student days: " It was my power of recollection that made me the favorite pupil of Master Palaemon." Going back to my theory, I think that this may have been true in the early days of working with Palaemon until Sev decided it was dangerous to show up the other students. Then he went into the act of having a poor memory.

The eidelon Malrubius said, "You were always the most careless of my boys." Sev at first pretended that he didn't remember studying the seven principles of governance, but when pressed he could name them. That also fits the poor-student-act theory.

2008-06-04 Tony Ellis replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the doeskin/manskin pouch error in The Book of the New Sun is an ordinary writer's slip, not something Wolfe would be unlikely to make. Grounds this in the poster's experience as a copy editor who regularly sees accidental word substitutions, answering Dave Tallman's claim that the mistake is hard to believe.
disputes · close-textual-reading

For what it's worth, speaking as a copy editor I have absolutely no problem believing Wolfe could have made that mistake. Writers make all sorts of mistakes, and one I regularly see is the accidental substitution of one word for another that apparently sparked some of the same synapses in the author's brain.

2008-06-04 Lane Haygood replying to John Smith #

Argues that scarcity of resources and a society's moral changes need not be separate causes, suggesting that practical conditions could shape morality, especially if morality is judged by consequences. Responds to John Smith's question about human skin used as fabric by offering that the practice may reflect resource running out or simply serve shock value.
disputes · theology-and-religion

Why assume that the two things are unrelated? Form follows function; morality follows, in many respects, the practicalities of the situation, at least insofar as we're willing to define morality by consequences. Running out of resources could have directed the moral change.

Or it could just be for the shock value.

Lane

2008-06-05 Roy C. Lackey replying to Dave Tallman #

Argues that the Conciliator cannot be meaningfully equated with Christ, because voluntary and involuntary sacrifice differ, and Severian sacrificed the world and its people while surviving as a pampered god. Responds to Dave Tallman's claim that the greatest sacrifice honors the greatest sacrifice, using the ending where Severian writes his memoir on the beach at Ushas.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: The Conciliator is not a Christ figure

There are two basic types of sacrifice -- those made voluntarily and those in which the subject of the sacrifice is a reluctant participant. The overwhelming majority of sacrifices involving human skin are of the second kind.

Theories that equate the Conciliator in any meaningful way with Christianity are fundamentally flawed. The latter is about the sacrifice of a god for the world and the people in it. Severian the Conciliator sacrificed the world and the people in it, while he got to survive, sitting on the beach in Ushas, writing his second memoir while watching the girls go by and otherwise enjoying the pampered pastimes of a petty god.

-Roy
