Saltus Mine Deep One

Urth list · 7 messages · 1998-06-14 to 1998-06-16

1998urthThe Book of the New SunJoshua A. SolomonWilliam H. AnsleyPeter StephensonPeter WestlakeJason VoegeleMichael Andre-DriussiMarty Light
The thread: Readers debated what the creature at the Saltus mine in The Fifth Head of Cerberus is. M.Driussi proposed a riddle pointing to Abaddon with Christ's descent to hell, while Peter Westlake argued it is a machine, not a beast, rejecting the riddle reading point by point. Marty Light suggested an oversized pile driver, and Joshua Solomon proposed a rocket or ICBM, tying it to the Citadel. Peter Stephenson and William Ansley then took up Lovecraft parallels, with Ansley correcting the claim that shoggoths are engineered beings. The thread drifted into Lovecraft and a side search for out-of-print titles, ending without resolution.
  • m.driussi: Severian's remark is a riddle pointing to Abaddon, with a hint of Christ's descent
  • Peter Westlake: the creature is a machine, not a living beast, and the riddle reading is wrong
  • Marty Light: the machine is an oversized pile driver that Severian may have triggered
  • Joshua A. Solomon: the machine may be a rocket or ICBM, as the Citadel is a collection of rocketships
  • Peter Stephenson: Wolfe's engineered beings differ from Lovecraft's ancient creatures
  • William H. Ansley: shoggoths are engineered beings in Lovecraft's fiction, per At the Mountains of Madness

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Myth and folklore sources, Names and their meanings

1998-06-14 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that Severian's remark about the creature at the Saltus mine is a riddle pointing to a specific name, possibly Abaddon, the angel guarding the pit, with a hint of Christ's descent to hell. Grounds this in Severian's claim that he now knows the creature's name and the clues Wolfe has supplied, and tentatively links the earthquakes to Dante.
proposes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Saltus mine deep one named Abaddon; Creature's name hinted in Severian's narration; Earthquakes echo Christ's descent to hell

It is good to step back and recall that the reason why we continue to turn this one over (and why alga waxes huffy against Wolfe, showing that he is both extra special and unique <g>) is that Severian basically says, IIRC, yeah, I didn't know the name of that critter then but I do (or I think I do) now. Which makes it into a clear riddle for the reader, suggesting that we've been given enough clues to figure it out.

The central answer, then, is a specific name (what, again?! <g>). For all we know, it might be "Abaddon," the angel guarding the pit of hell (if "Abaddon" <> subspace and "Abaddon" <> planet Mercury!).

Maybe there is a bit of Jesus's visit to hell--in Dante's work wasn't there something about landscape shifting earthquake(s) caused by the passage of the saviour?

=mantis=

1998-06-15 Peter Westlake #

Argues that the creature Severian and his companions called from the roots of the continent is a machine, not a living beast, citing the walking-tower imagery and the 'more terrible' mine reference in The Citadel of the Autarch. Responds to mantis's claim that Severian's remark is a riddle pointing to a name, rejecting that reading point by point and disputing the claim with textual quotations.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: the creature in the mine is a machine; the walking tower is the creature

Disagreeing with so learned a one as mantis is a perilous undertaking, especially for a bear. However, needs must.

Agreed. To be precise:

"What creature it was we had called from the roots of the continent I think I now know."

Nothing about a name there. There is the grinding sound, which does suggest that the thing is freeing itself from long imprisonment in the rock, perhaps. Then the footsteps:

"From far below I heard a step that might have been the walking of a tower on the Final Day, when it is said all the cities of Urth will stride forth to meet the dawn of the New Sun."

Now there isn't much about towers walking elsewhere in the book (reasonably enough!) so the occurrence of it in Citadel is the Clue that mantis suggests must exist. It even reads like one:

"Far behind it loomed a machine that flashed fire, a machine that was like a tower walking."

It's hard to see how it could be much more explicit than this. I was really quite surprised to find a reference on the next page but one:

"I opened my mouth to cry for help, then closed it again, thinking I might call upon myself something more terrible than that I had once waked in the mine of the man-apes."

a) see above

b) would Severian fear something "more terrible" than the angel of hell turning up on the battlefield?

c) *all* large living creatures on Urth have to live in the water. So it must be a machine.

SBear.

1998-06-15 Marty Light #

Proposes that the machine awakened at the Saltus mine in The Fifth Head of Cerberus may be an oversized pile driver, which would explain the sparks, noise and walking-tower appearance, and suggests Severian might have triggered it unknowingly while passing through. Also asks whether the Wolfe-cited titles Endangered Species and Planet Engineering are still in print, noting a listing in the Tor catalogue.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: the mine machine is an overgrown pile driver; Severian unknowingly triggered the mine machine

Ditto on Alga's comment about Peter S.'s translation! It's been great lurking in the background, watching the posts proliferate. Not that anyone ever needs a reason to re-read Wolfe, but 5HC is getting another perusal by me. "A-bo, a-bo, it's off to read I go..." No, I'm not going off on some "Three Fingers" discussion; rather, some idle speculation of mine own which mantis/borski/etal's comments will dwarf in no time:

Peter's Stephenson & Westlake (aka SBear) have been guessing about the type of machine awakened at the mine. I've always assumed it was some sort of major-league mining equipment -- perhaps an overgrown pile driver. In a vertical orientation, a pile driver could generate some sparks, lots of noise, and appear like a tower walking. Serverian could have unknowingly triggered the equipment into action as he traveled through the mine.

Final question: Are "Endangered Species" and "Planet Engineering" still in print? My copy of 5HC lists "...Species" on the Tor list, but I have been unable to turn up a copy. Thanks!

1998-06-16 Peter Stephenson #

Agrees with Peter Westlake's Lovecraft overtones in the Saltus Mine passage, arguing that Wolfe's engineered beings such as the tower and the chems differ from Lovecraft's creatures in being made rather than merely ancient. Quotes Lovecraft's prose, including lines from At the Mountains of Madness, and jokes about the Necronomicon and his own Vironese name.
supports · genre-and-craft · source-and-allusion

(pedantically, GW wrote and PW typed in and added quotes)

Just wanted to agree (belatedly --- fast list!) with the Lovecraft overtones. (The Baba Yaga joke was good, btw.) All we need is the word `eldritch' and a reference to the forbidden Necronomicon of the mad Arab Abdul Alhazred. The ancientness and otherworldliness of Lovecraft's creatures translates well to Wolfe, although we don't usually get the horror element as clearly as here.

As for created beings: I left the first quote in as a contrast --- what's missing in Lovecraft is the sense of something having been `engineered', as a tower is but a shuggoth (have I got that right?) isn't. There are parallels: the are lots of cases in Lovecraft of inventors or people who are making something nameless in their basements which tend to remind me of the things you find in the back of the fridge (or refrigerator for you lot over there) a few months too late. It somehow seems more chemical than physical, though. Strange that Wolfe picked the name `chems' for his whorl creatures --- maybe that was a deliberate way of making them seem more organic, in comparison with taluses etc.

By the way, Lovecraft has some of my favourite `how not to do it' pieces of prose, I'm glad GW wasn't too heavily influenced: "Dark the caverns where no man has trod and evil the mind that is held by no head" [who was it said `backward ran the sentences until reeled the mind'?], and my all-time favourite: "What we heard was not the fabulous note of any buried blasphemey of elder earth from whose supernal toughness an age-denied polar sun had evoked a monstrous response" which says it all, really (it's from `At the Mountains of Madness' and I think what they heard was something like `tekelili' --- come to think of it, the Antartic remains in this story, one of Lovecraft's longest, are rather Wolfean, too).

If you need a Vironese name for me (though last time I came near the place His Cognizance made the sign of addition at me and vanished) you can call me `Corncrake', if it hasn't been taken. No associations, I just like the word. I will continue to respond to Peter S or pws.

pws

1998-06-16 William H. Ansley #

Corrects the earlier claim by arguing that shoggoths are in fact engineered beings in Lovecraft's fiction, citing a passage from At the Mountains of Madness describing manufactured protoplasmic slaves. Supports the point with a quotation from the Joshi annotated edition, page 272, and adds a Dr. Seuss joke about the Old Ones.
informs · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading

Actually, a shoggoth is engineered, rather surprisingly for Lovecraft. "At the Mountains of Madness" is about as close as he ever comes to writing "hard sf."

They had done the same thing on other planets; having manufactured not only necessary foods, but certain multicellular protoplamic masses capable of moulding their tissues into all sorts of temporary organs under hypnotic influence and thereby forming ideal slaves to perform the heavy work of the community. [p. 272, _The Annotated H. P. Lovecraft_, S. T. Joshi, ed. ]

"They" above are the "star-headed Old Ones" (please don't confuse them with the "star-bellied sneetches").

William Ansley
