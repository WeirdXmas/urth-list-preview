mystery of the guild

Urth list · 13 messages · 2009-02-10 to 2009-02-12

2009urthThe Book of the New SunMatt TeelO'Donnell, Tim (BOSI)Tim WaltersMo Holkar / UKGDavid StockhoffChris PSon of WitzJeff WilsonRoy C. LackeyMatthew Weber
The thread: Participants asked what the Torturers' Guild's "mystery" in The Book of the New Sun is. Matt Teel proposed it echoes the Greek mysterion, meaning the secret art of torture, and Tim O'Donnell supported that reading while recalling the guild's two secrets, one being "We obey." Jeff Wilson argued obedience is no real secret and that the secret may be the identity of the one they obey, while O'Donnell proposed the mystery concerns rearing Autarchs or stands for the Catholic Church. Roy Lackey tied the guild's willingness to do the unthinkable to blind obedience. A separate dispute over whether Severian has real guilt about a planet's population (Son of Witz versus Chris P) and David Stockhoff's Catholic-fragment idea ran alongside, and the thread ended without consensus.
  • Matt Teel: the mystery is the Greek mysterion, the secret art of torture
  • Tim O'Donnell: the mystery ties to rearing Autarchs and may stand for the Catholic Church
  • Jeff Wilson: "We obey" is no real secret; the secret may be who they obey
  • Roy C. Lackey: the guild's blind obedience to authority explains its acts
  • David Stockhoff: the guild may be a surviving fragment of the Roman Catholic Church
  • Chris P: Severian's stated reservations are after-the-fact apologia for an audience

Themes: Hidden identities and parentage, Power, rulers and politics, Slavery and service

2009-02-10 Matt Teel #

Proposes that the Guild's "mystery" in The Book of the New Sun echoes the Greek word mysterion, meaning unknowable or initiates-only knowledge, so the secret is how to torture a person while keeping them alive. Grounds this in the word's sense as used in mystery religions, offering it as a guess about Wolfe's intent rather than citing a passage.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: The Guild's mystery is how to torture without killing

I've always assumed he was hearkening back to the Greek word 'mysterion,' which generally refers to knowlege that is unknowable, but also means knowlege that is secret, for initiates only (the mystery religions would be one example of this usage).? The mystery would therefore be the knowledge of how to torture a person?... probably in such a way that you didn't actually kill them, but sort of dangled them over the precipice of death, so to speak, until you got what you required. ?
Matt Teel

2009-02-10 O'Donnell, Tim (BOSI) replying to Matt Teel #

Supports Matt Teel's reading that Wolfe's 'mystery of our guild' echoes the Greek mysterion, meaning the torturers' secret art. Cites two Severian passages using 'mystery of our guild' for torture practice and recalls that the guild has two secrets, one being 'We obey', but cannot locate the second citation.
supports · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: The guild's mystery is the art of torture

Severian certainly uses the word "mystery" in that sense. Two examples:

"I'm beginning to think that by
practicing the mystery of our guild while you and I are travelling, I'll be able to support us."

Let it be sufficient to say that I performed the
prescribed operations on the cattle thief, which terminated in his execution; in the future, when I describe my travels, you are to understand that I practiced the mystery of our guild where it was profitable to do so, though I do not mention the specific occasions.

However, I seem to recall that Severian mentions that his Guild had two secrets and that he would betray both over his narrative. One is certainly "We obey" but I don't think the other one is mentioned directly. Having said that I can't find the citation here. I thought it was at the very start of Shadow...

2009-02-11 Roy C. Lackey replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the torturers' guild's obedience to authority explains their willingness to do the unthinkable, citing the final lines of Severian's chapter where he kills Katharine and the lesson from Malrubius on the principles of governance. Responds to Jeff Wilson's question about the guild's mystery by pointing to the text's own explanation of blind, unreasoned obedience.
answers · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Torturers are raised for blind obedience to authority

I don't see what is so mysterious about this. If you look at the last two sentences of the chapter I cited when I answered the original question, it seems obvious enough:

"No one truly obeys unless he will do the unthinkable in obedience; no one will do the unthinkable save we.
"How could I refuse to the Increate what I had willingly given to the Autarch when I struck off Katharine's head?" (SWORD, chap. XXXI)

Think back to the lecture/lesson Severian had with Aquastor Malrubius the night before he left Nessus, about the "seven principles of governance" with the "Divine Entity" as the highest rank. The torturers were raised from early childhood to respond with blind, unreasoned obedience to authority.

Most people would balk at causing the death of a planet's population, even if they had assurances that a greater good would come of it, by and by. Severian had no such scruples.

-Roy

I don't find that hard to swallow. This society is like the last vestiges of human culture. It's so decayed and amnesiac that it barely even knows it's history. In a time where people "knew" the sun was dying out and there was essentially no future, except some prophetic hope, people might lose interest in just about everything other than mere survival and simple hope.

There must be a connection between the Order for Seekers of Truth and Penitence and The Pellerines. I read the Pelerines as the church, and the guild as the Inquisition.  Two branches of the same thing, that have evolved, as has everything else, with a sort of forgetfulness of their roots.

That a monial might have been sent to the torturers for hiding (or punishment, it's not clear, I'd guess hiding) could imply the connection.
I'm fairly convinced of the idea that Catherine is the Katherine actress of the Feast. The key symbolic thing that seals the deal for me is the moment where he has to symbolically behead her. If we read that as his mother, in light of what we know he has to do to Mother Earth, (and human mothers) it ties the two together perfectly.

Disputes the claim that Severian had no scruples about the death of a planet's population, arguing Wolfe gives him real reservations and guilt. Cites Severian's lament in Urth of the New Sun about abandoning the survivors and his lifelong yearning for the New Sun as what overcame those reservations.

2009-02-11 Chris P replying to Son of Witz #

Argues that Severian is writing for an audience, so his professed reservations about causing a planet's death may be after-the-fact apologia rather than real guilt. Grounds this in his habit of deflecting responsibility and framing his acts as inevitable, and responds to Son of Witz's quotation, noting Wright's reading of Severian as a pawn of alien forces.
disputes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Severian's remorse is after-the-fact apologia; Severian frames his actions as predestined

Part of what makes those passages difficult to take straightforwardly is that Severian is clearly writing for an audience, and there's an element of apologia. Many times I see him write about certain sentiments which would make him a more human or sympathetic character - and he succeeds at that - but those sentiments are not necessarily evident in his actions (as you and others note). And that raises the question of, in your example, whether he actually had reservations or whether (after the fact) he felt that it would sound better in the ears of posterity to *say* he had reservations. This is compounded by the fact that he has a sort of theme, throughout the series, of deflecting responsibility for his own actions, or casting them in a light where they seem inevitable/pre-ordained.

This tactic works so well that Wright, for example, goes so far as to essentially remove all responsibility from Severian by interpreting him a hapless tool/pawn of alien forces.

2009-02-12 David Stockhoff replying to Matt Teel #

Argues that the Torturers' Guild could be a surviving fragment of the Roman Catholic Church, possibly preserved in some pocket of far-future Urth, since the Guild in The Book of the New Sun always served the monarch and never seemed powerful. Responds to Matt Teel's skepticism by noting that 20,000 or more years separate Severian from the present, and that the name Commonwealth and the age of Nessus leave room for a predecessor elsewhere on the planet.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · source-and-allusion · theories: Torturers' Guild descended from the Roman Catholic Church; RCC survived in a hidden pocket of Urth

True. Although where the Inquisition grew in power, and power can corrupt anything, we're talking about an order that must have shrunk in power and gained little from its practices. And there's no other hint in TBotNS that the guild used to be *that* powerful---it always served the monarch.

But anything could have happened in the minimum of 20,000 or so years Wolfe has put between Severian and ourselves. And Urth is a wide place, where the RCC could have survived in strength in a pocket somewhere to await its return to global power, many times. The name "Commonwealth" suggests as much. That is, its immediate predecessor might have been located elsewhere on the planet. Nessus does not appear to be *that* old.
