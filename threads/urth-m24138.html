Any Pynchon Fans?

Urth list · 5 messages · 2013-08-19 to 2013-08-20

2013urthDavid DuffyMarc AraminiAntonin ScriabinLarry Miller
The thread: Christopher Simon opened the thread asking whether other Pynchon fans were on the list, noting Bleeding Edge's September release and that Pynchon and Wolfe are his two favourite authors. Marc Aramini disputed any close parallel, arguing Pynchon is more postmodern than Wolfe because his disordered plots never resolve, so interpretive methods suited to Wolfe would fail on Pynchon. Decanus1284 offered a brief personal aside about treating the identity mystery in V as a puzzle, as with Wolfe. Simon later compared Pynchon's later novels favourably with Gravity's Rainbow, and David Duffy said there are parallels with Wolfe but no known direct allusions, comparing it to Neal Stephenson. The thread ended without real debate, drifting into casual admiration and a single unresolved contrast on postmodernism.

  • Decanus1284: Reads Pynchon's V as a puzzle, as he reads Wolfe

2013-08-19 Marc Aramini replying to Decanus1284 #

Argues that Pynchon is more postmodern than Wolfe, since his disordered plots do not resolve, so the interpretive methods used on Wolfe would not work on his chaotic, decentralized novels. Grounds this in Gravity's Rainbow's bizarre episodes and the poster's stalled reading of Against the Day, responding to Christopher Simon's thread opener about Pynchon fans.
disputes

I think Pynchon is decidedly more postmodern - when things fall apart they tend not to come together again. Scatophages(i?), giant octopi, erections summoning death from above ... I like Gravity's Rainbow but am currently perennially half way through Against The Day - his hippie economic sensibilities in that one seem a bit off putting.

I think in the final analysis the kind of interpretations I try on Wolfe would not by and large work on the more chaotic and decentralized Pynchon.

2013-08-20 Antonin Scriabin replying to Christopher Simon #

Compares Thomas Pynchon's later novels, Mason and Dixon, Inherent Vice and Against the Day, with his earlier Gravity's Rainbow era, finding the later work more formal, enjoyable and emotionally engaging. Offers this as a casual aside on an off-topic thread about Pynchon fans rather than a Wolfe reading.
chats

Pynchon is definitely more scattered and difficult to interpret, insofar as interpretation is possible with his books. That said, latter-day Pynchon (Mason and Dixon, Inherent Vice, and Against the Day) is much more formal and enjoyable in storytelling aspects than GR-era Pynchon. Fewer hallucinatory adenoids and more emotionally interesting characters and adventure, I guess.
