The Conciliator

Urth list · 5 messages · 1999-11-24 to 1999-11-29

1999urthThe Book of the New SunJim HenleyRoy C. LackeyJames Jordan
The thread: Readers debated whether the Conciliator is a messiah-like earthly ruler or an eschatological figure, and how Erebus survives on Antarctica and why he allies with Abaia. Roy Lackey argued the Conciliator is an earthly messiah and that the aquastors' forgiveness of Severian is a feeble rationalization. Jim Henley argued Talos's play conveys the eschatological New Sun, questioned why water-dwelling Other People would care about a flood, and doubted Erebus's survival. Jim Jordan proposed reading Erebus as a Dantean, frozen, immobile Satan-figure operating through agents. The thread ended without resolution, with Jordan admitting uncertainty about the Abaia alliance.
  • Roy C. Lackey: the Conciliator is an earthly messiah-like ruler, not eschatological; the aquastor trial is a feeble rationalization
  • Jim Henley: Talos's play conveys the eschatological New Sun; questions Erebus's survival and the saline explanation; Severian is a Christ figure, not Christ
  • Jim Jordan: Erebus is a Satan-figure frozen in Antarctic ice like Dante's hell, seeking worship rather than annihilation
  • Jim Henley: praises an Erebus-and-Abaia theory but doubts Erebus is a land-bound giant; suggests he may be a chem

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Gods, angels and the Increate

1999-11-24 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that the Conciliator is a messiah-like earthly ruler figure rather than an eschatological one, so the New Sun's coming benefits Urth's people and was never presented as heaven-bound salvation. Grounds this in the Jewish messianic analogy and claims the Pelerines are a faith in decline, and attacks the trial in which aquastors forgive Severian as a feeble rationalization built from his own memory.

From what we are shown in the text, the Conciliator was known to most people of the Commonwealth in Severian's time, but his cult was not widely established; that is, people had heard of him but he was not the center of a popular religion. The Pelerines and their like were relics of a faith in decline, if it had ever been catholic. More importantly, whenever the Conciliator was mentioned, usually in terms of being or bringing the New Sun, it was done in a manner suggestive of Someone who would somehow bring about a renewal of Urth--who would make well again the weakened sun and usher in an era of peace, plenty, and prosperity. The analogy is with the Jewish concept of the Messiah current in some quarters two millennia ago. The Messiah was to be an earthly ruler, a temporal authority over a kingdom overflowing with milk and honey on earth, not some celestial everafter.

Nowhere--absolutely nowhere in NEW SUN--is there even a hint that the coming of the Conciliator is conceived of as an eschatological event. So far as the coming of the New Sun is known to the people of Urth, it is a good thing--for them. The man on the street had no inkling that he and all he knew and loved would have to die for someone else to get to heaven--that is, Ushas.

Had the people of Urth known that they would have to pay the price of complete obliteration for a "sin" that wasn't even theirs, and if they had had a choice in the matter, I have little doubt what their choice would have been. The feeble attempt at rationalization at the phony trial by having people whom Sev had wronged elect to fight on his side, thus tipping the balance of justice in favor of the New Sun, is just that--feeble. All but Gunnie were aquastors; aquastors are only of the dead; the dead have nothing to lose. They weren't real.

The psyches of the aquastors come from memory--Sev's memory. They existed in his memory as he wanted to see them, idealized to suit his notions of Justice and Right. He hardly knew some of the people--little Severa he had never even met. And they all forgave him--sure, that's human nature, very believable.

Oh, and happy birthday Alex.

Roy

1999-11-24 Jim Henley #

Argues that Talos's play in The Book of the New Sun was meant to convey the eschatological nature of the New Sun, and asks why the water-dwelling Other People would care about a flood that wipes out Urth's population. Raises doubts about Erebus surviving on Antarctica and about the saline-change explanation, and compares Sev to a Christ figure rather than Christ himself, responding to Roy Lackey's messiah analogy.
questions · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Sev is a Christ figure, not second coming; Talos's play aimed to convey eschatology

This is a fascinating point, Roy. It dovetails with the idea of Sev as Christ _figure_ rather than the second coming of Christ himself. And suggests a different angle on what the book is "about" - here's what a "worldly" messiah would look like, fellas; how do you like it?

FWIW, there are some interesting parallels with what the Branch Davidians actually believed (as opposed to the caricature of their beliefs in the popular press) about a sequence of Anointed (Christos) through history.

I might plead "insufficient evidence" on this one. Certainly Talos knew, though of course he's a little freak - the whole purpose of his play was to get the eschatalogical nature of the New Sun across to folks. Well, and to get people to drop their goodies and run in fear, but is that really a _different_ thing? <g>

So Talos is Minister of Propaganda for the Other People, which returns us to a question I still have no answer for: Why are a bunch of folks who live in the water all worried about the prospect of a flood? And they're supposedly the enemies of humanity in general, or they should stop confusing people by attacking our ships. So why is it any skin off their nose if most of Urth's population is wiped out?

Okay, Erebus rules the pole and his domain would shrink, so he has a motive. Unfortunately, I'm not quite sure why Erebus is still alive in the first place. If he's a giant and he's living on the Antarctic land mass - we're told his namesake mountain is his seat of power - why hasn't he collapsed into a puddle of bile and bone meal already?

I think awhile ago, someone suggested that they were all leary of the altered saline content that would characterize the new oceans. But this doesn't, um, wash. (Bad Jim!) Juturna seems happy to swim way up Gyoll, and Baldanders heads first for Lake Diuturna before, at some presumed later time, entering the ocean.

I'm less convinced. My personal reaction would be, "I'd rather not drown thank you." What's more, tonight I seasoned a strip steak with black and cayenne pepper, seared it in a cast-iron skillet, poured a quarter-cup of dilute bourbon over it and finished it in the oven for five minutes at 400 degrees. Then I salted it and ate it hot. We can't afford that kind of meal every night in the Henley household (it was on sale!), but right now the idea of living off algae in my bloodstream holds little appeal. Green Man, phooey.

Nevertheless, we've seen groups of people embrace their own deaths in the name of religious fulfillment and we'll see it again before we're dead. A whole world without New Sun dissenters? Probably not. Would it be wrong to proceed in the face of objections from 49% of the population? For the sake of ten just men? One? I have my own answer, but if there _is_ a god, what is my answer worth?

Which reminds me: this very existential question (if there _is_ a god, what do our human standards of ethics matter) is explored to terrific effect, IMHO, in a movie of the last decade called THE RAPTURE. I recommend it as supplemental material for our studies in recent threads.

An excellent point.

Likewise.

Best,

Jim

1999-11-27 Jim Henley #

Jim Henley praises a colleague's theory of Erebus and Abaia for explaining the literary and religious levels of The Urth of the New Sun, while noting it leaves unexplained how Erebus survives as a land-bound giant and why Abaia allies with him. He floats two possibilities: Erebus is not really a giant, or he is a chem. The post replies to Jim Jordan's objection, which concerns Erebus's survival and his motive from the quoted message.
disputes · puzzle-solving · allegory-and-typology · theories: Erebus isn't really a giant; Erebus is a chem robot

Good job, Nute: that's two out of three, and an excellent two. The Urthes say that everything in the saga has three levels - the science-fictional (plot/world) significance, the literary (allusive) significance and the religious significance. Your theory does nicely for the latter two: alas, it still doesn't explain how Erebus survives being land-bound and giant-sized. (One possible explanation: Erebus isn't really a giant. Another: He's a "chem," giant robot division.) Nor does it quite explain why the warm-water Abaia is in league with the ice-god Erebus. But you've reduced the number of things I worry about by one, for which I thank you.

Best,

Jim
