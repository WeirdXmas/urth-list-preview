5HOC - no. 5, Marsch, Eastwind and Sandwalker

Urth list · 4 messages · 2007-05-06 to 2007-05-17

2007urthDaniel D JonesJeff WilsonJames WynnDan Rabin
The thread: The thread asks whether 'Maitre' in the first story names a clone or the officer Marsch. Dan Rabin traces the French word to Latin 'magister' and reads it as a servant's title for a household head, not a personal name. Jeff Wilson proposes a double-entendre, pointing to Number 5's mother as well as his father. James Wynn argues that Marsch wrote the April 23 diary entry in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, died from the cat's bite, and that V.R.T. then took his identity. Daniel D Jones disputes this, saying V.R.T. seized the chance only after Marsch died. The thread ends without agreement.

Themes: Names and their meanings, Doubles, twins and clones, Hidden identities and parentage

2007-05-06 Dan Rabin #

Explains that "Maitre" is the French word for "Master", noting the circumflex marks a lost etymological s and that both words likely descend from Latin "magister". Answers Jessica Maclean's question about whether Maitre is a clone or the officer Marsch by treating Maitre as a title servants use for a household head, not a personal name.
answers · source-and-allusion

Jessica Maclean asks,

"Maitre" is the French word corresponding to the
English "Master". It's actually spelled
"Ma?tre", where the circumflex, as is often the
case in French, indicates an original "s" in the
etymology. I suspect both words descend from the
Latin "magister".

It's what servants call the head of the household
in which they serve--a title or honorific, not a
name.

-- Dan Rabin

2007-05-06 Jeff Wilson replying to Dan Rabin #

Proposes that the title Maitre in the first story is a double-entendre, figuratively meaning Number 5's mother as well as his father. Draws on Jessica Maclean's etymology of the French word, which Dan Rabin quoted, linking maitre to the Latin magister and to a servant's honorific for the head of a household.
proposes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Maitre is Number 5's mother as well as father

I believe that it also serves as a double-entendre in the first story; Maitre is also Number 5's figurative "mater", or mother, as well as his father.

2007-05-12 James Wynn replying to Dan Rabin #

Argues that the April 23 diary entry in The Fifth Head of Cerberus was written by Marsch, not V.R.T., and that Marsch died from the cat's bite, with V.R.T. then taking his identity to conceal the death. Responds to Dan Rabin's view that V.R.T. wrote the entry, reasoning from the coincidence of Marsch's death and from how Adam's timeline fits together.
proposes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: V.R.T. murdered Marsch for reasons tied to the event; Marsch died from the cat's bite and V.R.T. assumed his identity; April 23 entry was written by Marsch

I have always presumed the April 23 entry was written by Marsch. That was why I presumed VRT had murdered Marsch for some reason relating to this event. Otherwise, it just seemed too coincidental.

Once again, the idea that Marsch died by accident seemed way to coincidental, however (something about the way Adam put this timeline together) it does seem reasonable that the cat's bite killed Marsch and so VRT took up his identity to hide the nature of Marsch's death.

I like this idea very much.

2007-05-17 Daniel D Jones replying to James Wynn #

Argues that V.R.T. did not plan to take Marsch's identity from the start but seized the chance after Marsch died in 5HOC. Responds to James Wynn's quoted post about the April 23 entry by saying the coincidence disappears if Marsch died first, while admitting the poster has not reread the story recently.
disputes · puzzle-solving · theories: V.R.T. seized the opportunity after Marsch died

To my mind, it only seems too coincidental if you assume that VRT intended to assume Marsch's identity from the beginning. If, however, you assume that Marsch died and VRT seized the opportunity, then it doesn't seem like a huge coincidence. It's been awhile since I read the story, so I don't know how well this interpretation jibes with the text. It's something I'll keep in mind the next time I read it.
