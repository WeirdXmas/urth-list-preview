The thread: Matthew Keeley asked the list for writers for a thesis on Christianity's influence on detective fiction and whether any Wolfe stories fit. The discussion wandered through Chandler, Hammett, and Marlowe, with Adam Thornton praising Marlowe as a not-quite-reliable narrator and dcooperv reading Hammett and Chandler as existentialists who can coexist with religious belief. thalassocrat argued Hammett's The Glass Key is the most Wolfean of the two. Aaron suggested Wolfe's 'The Detective of Dreams' as a religious detective story, and Bryan Alexander recommended Michael Cisco's The Divinity Student. The thread ended in social exchange rather than resolution, with Keeley planning to buy Wolfe books.
  • Adam Thornton: Marlowe is smarter than he lets on, a favorite not-quite-reliable narrator
  • dcooperv: Hammett and Chandler are existentialist, not strictly irreligious, and belong in a religion-in-crime discussion
  • thalassocrat: Hammett's The Glass Key is the best and most Wolfean of the pair
  • Matthew Keeley: Marlowe is smarter than he pretends; still building a thesis reading list
  • aaron: 'The Detective of Dreams' fits the detective-and-religion interest
  • Bryan Alexander: Michael Cisco's The Divinity Student is amazing, close to Wolfe

2008-05-17 Matthew Keeley #

Asks the list to suggest further writers for a senior thesis on Christianity's influence on detective fiction, and offers his own list split into irreligious and religious authors. Also asks whether any Wolfe stories fit, noting his detective stories and mystery elements in The Book of the Long Sun, and mentions Tim Powers as a possible fit.
questions

Seeing as we've been quite off-topic lately, I figured another mostly-Wolfe-unrelated thread wouldn't hurt. As I mentioned in an earlier thread, I'm trying to decide what to do for a senior thesis. I think I've decided on writing about Christianity and influence on detective fiction - I've got a list of writers I think are relevant, but if anyone could suggest further writers, I'd be grateful. The Urth list seems pretty well-read after all...

My list, as it stands now:

"Irreligious" Writers
Arthur Conan Doyle (In the early stories Holmes has a dislike for religion, though Doyle believed in some form of afterlife)
Umberto Eco (Lapsed Catholic, murder in a monastery)

Religious Writers
Margery Allingham
P.D. James
G.K. Chesterton
Ronald Knox (Priest and friend of Evelyn Waugh)
Dorothy L. Sayers
Ellis Peters (Not sure)

I may be forgetting some here, and my knowledge of crime fiction is far from extensive

Also - any Wolfe stories that would fit in this framework? I know he has a few detective stories, plus of course he draws on mystery a lot in The Book of the Long Sun. Tim Powers might be relevant too - he seems to go in for a mystery-like setting sometimes.

Thanks a lot.

-Matt

2008-05-17 dcooperv replying to Matthew Keeley #

Argues that Hammett and Chandler belong in a discussion of religion in crime fiction, reading them as existentialist rather than strictly irreligious, and notes that existentialism can coexist with religious belief. Replies to Matthew Keeley's thesis request and offers a side note about Holmes and Buddhism; no Wolfe text is discussed.
disputes

You could probably put Hammett and Chandler in there, possibly on the irreligious side. I say this only because (IMO) it's near-impossible to have an intelligent discussion of the genre without acknowledging these two, given the significance of their contributions. I always saw them as existentialist more than anything else really. Of course, existentialism does not preclude religiosity by any means (and nothing bugs me like a false dichotomy!). Read Dostoeyevsky and Martin Buber for good examples of this. Can you give us a more specific idea of your thesis? If nothing else, this could spark some enlightening conversation.

-Dan Vince

PS: Didn't Doyle have Holmes study Theravada Buddhism at some point? You might want to do some research into that.

2008-05-19 thalassocrat replying to Matthew Keeley #

Argues that Hammett's The Glass Key is the best story by either Hammett or Chandler and the most Wolfean, praising its showing-not-telling and its puzzle of the protagonist's inner state read from his actions. Responds to Adam Thornton's defense of Hammett, which was itself replying to dcooperv's praise of Chandler's The Big Sleep and Marlowe as unreliable narrator.
supports · genre-and-craft

On Sun, 18 May 2008 13:36:32 +1000 Adam Thornton <[email]>

But don't downplay Hammett. On the weekends & Wednesdays I'm sure he was a better writer than Chandler. Of all the stories written by either of them, I think "The Glass Key" is the best, as well as the most Wolfean. All showing, no telling, and the biggest puzzle is trying to work out the protagonist's interior state, just from his actions.

Asks the list which other writers share the feeling of Dashiell Hammett and Roger Zelazny, whose prose makes reading others awkward and who add scene detail without slowing the narrative. Prompted by a thread about Chandler and the unreliable narrator in The Big Sleep, the post makes a comparison of style rather than discussing Wolfe's books directly.
questions

My mind has always connected Dash Hammett and Roger Zelazny. It took me awhile to figure out why, if I have. Rich is the milieu? They both make, on immediately finishing something of theirs, reading another writer difficult because of how awkward the language feels and they both had such an incredible ability to add detail to a scene without it getting in the way. Anyone else the group can think of like that?
