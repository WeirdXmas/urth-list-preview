The thread: Participants debated whether Severian truly rejected Daria in The Book of the New Sun and whether the word cacogen marks all aliens as monstrous. Jerry Friedman argued the rejection was not real, pointing to a later sexual encounter after Daria's escape attempt and the tearing off of her shift. Jeff Wilson countered that Severian would not have slept with Daria given his claim to desire only one living woman, presumably Agia, and that such an encounter would undercut that portrait. Andrew Mason argued cacogen is applied to all aliens and that Daria's rejection may have been an act, while Jeff Wilson also held that Inire and the Cumaean are not widely known to be nonhuman. The thread ended without resolution, with the Daria question still divided between the two readings.
  • Jerry Friedman: Severian's rejection of Daria was not real; he had sex with her after she fled
  • Jeff Wilson: Severian would not have slept with Daria, given his claim to desire only one woman
  • Andrew Mason: cacogen applies to all aliens, reflecting a general sense that aliens are monstrous
  • Jeff Wilson: Inire and the Cumaean are not widely known to be nonhuman, partly due to their cousins' masquerade

Themes: Women, sex and gender, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Beasts and the animal in us

2011-01-16 Jeff Wilson replying to Andrew Mason #

Argues that Inire and the Cumaean are not widely known to be nonhuman, partly because of the masquerade their cousins maintain. Responds to Andrew Mason's point that cacogen applies to aliens beyond monstrous ones, citing a quoted description of Inire as a little man with a wry neck and bow legs.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Inire and the Cumaean are nonhuman aliens passing as human

Inire and the Cumaean are not widely known to be nonhuman, outside their circle of acquaintances. "'Just a little man he is, with a wry neck and bow legs.'" This is possible in part because of the masquerade their "cousins" maintain.

2011-01-16 Andrew Mason #

Argues that the word "cacogen" in The Book of the New Sun is used for all aliens, not only for hierodules in monster masks, so it reflects a general sense that aliens are monstrous. Responds to Jeff Wilson's point about Severian rejecting Daria, noting Daria may have been an act and that Lee's suggestion that kahibit means servant of the throne also fits.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Cacogen applies to all aliens, not only masked hierodules; Severian's refusal of Daria may be an act

He did at first. I'm not sure what happened after that. I think he does sometimes list her with other women who have been his lovers , so it may be that the refusal was an act. But as Lee pointed out, the kahibit might do for 'servant of the throne' as well.

'Cacogen' is used at times for all aliens, incuding Inire and the Cumaean, who don't disguise themselves as monsters. Certainly the name reflects a sense that aliens are monstrous, but it doesn't seem to be limited to those who wear monster masks.

2011-01-16 Jerry Friedman replying to Jeff Wilson #

Questions whether Severian rejected Daria, noting he had sex with her after she ran away and tearing off her shift, and doubts a quoted line about desiring only one woman. Raises a textual variant and asks whether the stripping is meant to show he does not want her.
questions · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's sex with Daria is on his terms

If that means he's not willing to have sex with any woman but one, it started after the encounter with Pia, and I remember no trace of this change in him.

I'm sure I've read that many times as "There is only one living woman I desire, and I fear no man but myself." Yet my edition has it the way you quoted it. Just my unconscious editor?

My first guess would have been Dorcas, and my second, Valeria.

Severian's behavior in this whole scene is rude, crude, and socially unacceptable (as we said in junior high). Is your idea that he strips her naked to underline the point that even then he doesn't want to have sex with her? If he does reject her, that's pretty serious humiliation, and I'm surprised she's so friendly with him before the battle (Ch. XXI). But if he doesn't reject her, maybe she can write his actions off as the odd behavior of johns.

I think the point he's making is that she has no power over him, so their sex is going to be on his terms.

Jerry Friedman
