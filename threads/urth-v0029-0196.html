Mopsa the Fairy

The thread: Adam Stephanides flagged Gene Wolfe's choice of Jean Ingelow's Mopsa the Fairy for an anthology and suggested it might hold allusions for Wolfe readers, noting thematic links to There Are Doors. William Ansley argued the choice was odd because Baum's Ozma is a conventional fairy princess, claiming Wolfe overlooked Baum and questioning what Wolfe meant by "really". 2000-08-29 Adam Stephanides #

Reports that Gene Wolfe chose Jean Ingelow's nineteenth-century children's story "Mopsa the Fairy" for an anthology of favourite fantasy stories, and suggests it may hold allusions for Wolfeans to mine. Notes that the poster found no clear influence on first reading but sees thematic similarities with There Are Doors.
informs · source-and-allusion

An anthology recently appeared edited by Martin H. Greenberg entitled "My Favorite Fantasy Story" (published by Daw) in which a selection of prominent fantasy authors were each asked to "pick the one fantasy story that spoke to them, touched them, made them reexamine the genre in a new light." Wolfe was one of the authors included, and his choice was "Mopsa the Fairy," a nineteenth-century children's story--really a short novel--by Jean Ingelow. Among the things that Wolfe says about the story in his brief introduction is that "Ingelow dared answer a question that no other such writer has even dared ask: If there really were a fairy princess, what would she be like?"

So we Wolfeans have another text to mine for possible allusions by Wolfe. I confess that after a first reading, no examples of influence sprang to mind, though there are thematic similarities with THERE ARE DOORS.

--Adam

2000-09-06 William H. Ansley #

Argues that Wolfe's choice of Ingelow's Mopsa the Fairy as his favourite fantasy story is odd, since Ozma of Baum's Oz books is a fairy princess in the conventional sense. Grounds this in a claim that Wolfe read Baum, citing the evidence of "The Eyeflash Miracles", and questions what Wolfe means by "really" in his remark about a real fairy princess.
disputes · source-and-allusion · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe read Baum's Oz books

I find this a very odd thing for Gene Wolfe to say, considering it is a certainty (based on the evidence of "The Eyeflash Miracles") that Wolfe read Baum's Oz books. Ozma is nothing if she is not Baum's idea of what a "real" fairy princess would be like. She may not be a terribly *inspired* fairy princess, but she is definitely a fairy princess.

I suppose it all depends on what Gene Wolfe means by 'really' in "if there really were a fairy princess". Based on my (mostly failed) attempts to understand his fiction, I must admit I have no idea how I would go about trying to understand his reality.

2000-09-07 Adam Stephanides #

Points out that Wolfe's introduction to Ingelow's Mopsa the Fairy explicitly names Baum and Oz, correcting William Ansley's claim that Wolfe overlooked Baum. Quotes Wolfe's remarks on Baum's eeriness and Ingelow writing for boys, then admits he cannot see why Mopsa is realer than other fairy princesses and does not share Wolfe's enthusiasm.
disputes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography

Wolfe explicitly mentions Baum in his introduction; he says: "You will find much here that recalls Oz; you will also find the eeriness that L. Frank Baum set out to banish from his own fairyland. (He was thirteen when 'Mopsa' was issued, and it must have frightened him.)" And: "Ingelow was writing for boys as single-mindedly as Baum ever wrote for girls..." I haven't read enough Baum since childhood to judge the truth of these statements.

And I must admit that after a single reading of "Mopsa," I don't know what it is about the fairy princess in this story that makes Wolfe regard her as realer than any other fairy princess. Nor do I share Wolfe's enthusiasm for the story, although that's a common reaction of mine to the writers Wolfe lauds (with the exception of Lafferty).

--Adam
