PEACE: Death; lying (was Re: (urth) Grounded in the text?)

Urth list · 5 messages · 2000-12-08 to 2000-12-08

2000urthThe Book of the New SunAlex David GroceAdam StephanidesMichael Straight
The thread: Posters debated whether Weer in Gene Wolfe's Peace is dead, with Michael Straight arguing that the two elm-tree passages point to his death as a ghostly afterlife glimpse. Adam Stephanides disputed that the text proves it, holding that Weer's death best explains unexplained details without any single decisive passage, and that undisclosed lying by Weer would make him deceive the reader. Adam then conceded the text proves only that someone died, though he still believes it was Weer, and noted an end-of-the-world hypothesis raised by others. Alex David Groce supported the death reading, contrasting Peace's narrative loop with The Arabian Nightmare and drawing a modernism-versus-postmodernism distinction. The thread ended without agreement, with the death question left open.
  • Michael Straight: the elm-tree passages show Weer is dead and glimpsed in the afterlife
  • Adam Stephanides: Weer's death best explains the details, but no passage proves it
  • Alex David Groce: the final episode breaks the loop and implies Weer has died
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: questioned whether Weer's account is a lie (position inferred from replies only)

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Unreliable narration

2000-12-08 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that Weer's death in Peace is the best explanation for a set of unexplained details, though no single passage proves he is dead. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes by holding that if Weer lies in his account without signalling it, he is implicitly lying to the reader, and by discounting Wolfe's interview statements as evidence.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Weer is dead in Peace

I, too, am convinced that Weer is dead, but I wouldn't say the evidence is overwhelming, especially if you don't regard Wolfe's interview statements as probative. If somebody were to insist that Weer was alive, there is no one passage in the book (even the elm-tree passages) I know of that I could point out to him/her and say "See? This proves you're wrong." Rather, it's that Weer's being dead ties together a number of details which, while not contradicting his being alive, remain unexplained in that case.

If he lies to one of the people in his account, and he gives no indication to us that he is lying or that he habitually lies in such situations, then he is implicitly lying to us, I think. This is not just a convention of narrative, but of ordinary conversation.

Thanks for the very interesting post about book covers, by the way.

--Adam

2000-12-08 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Peace's final episode breaks the narrative loop and implies Weer has died, unlike The Arabian Nightmare, which stays coherent beneath its fragmentation. Responds to Alex Groce's and Adam's end-of-the-world reading by quoting Adam's earlier 1998 post on the two novels and drawing a modernism-versus-postmodernism distinction.
supports · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Peace ends with Weer passing on

Adam, re: the end-of-the-world hypothesis:

I don't know that I originated it, but:

Adam also wrote, about THE ARABIAN NIGHTMARE:

Because although the book tells a story about utter disintegration and what you might call utter narrative collapse (no story is ever complete, nothing ever "bottoms out"), I think the overall story is actually in a sense very coherent and focused, even down to the final revelation

that our narrator all along has been "The Ape of God," Shaitan, and that our poor protagonist is in Hell--and one of the most nightmarish visions of Hell literature has managed to come up with. PEACE on the other hand, "breaks the loop"--rather than suggesting that Weer undergoes continual disintegration, it suggests that the stories are done in some sense, and he has passed on. But they both use a fragmented, complex style to arrive at, ultimately, I think, coherent images. But perhaps this is just my take on modernism as (in a way) shattering the narrative but making sure that in the worst case the pieces add up to a picture of broken glass, while postmodernism is all about making sure there is no authoritative way to put the pieces back together at all. Which, now that I think about it, leaves few writers who are really postmodernists (or at least few of any interest).
