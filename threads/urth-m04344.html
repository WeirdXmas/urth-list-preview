Tzadkiel's ship

Urth list · 21 messages · 2007-11-28 to 2007-12-03

2007urthThe Book of the New SunMark MillmanMatthew MalthouseMatthew KingAndy RobertsonJosh GellerJeff WilsonStanisław BocianTony EllisDan'l Danehy-OakesRoy C. Lackey
The thread: Thread asks whether the ship on Tzadkiel's armorial device in Wolfe's Book of the New Sun is a spaceship or a sailing vessel, and whether the Captain's 'only ship' claim fits a Catholic-Church or time-travel reading. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Tony Ellis argue for a spaceship via the heraldic 'volant' (flying), while Stanislaus, Andy Robertson and Mark Millman argue the heraldic ship would be drawn as a sailing ship, and Jeff Wilson argues the word's meaning shifts and the hull is unlike any seagoing craft. Matthew and Andy Robertson offer multiple Urth ships or time travel to square the 'only ship' claim with earlier vessels. Late posts by Roy C. Lackey cite the book's own uses of 'ship' for sea vessels, leaving the heraldry question largely unresolved.
  • Matthew King: Ship in Fifth Head of Cerberus represents the Catholic Church, citing an earlier post rather than the text
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Tzadkiel's ship is certainly a spaceship since 'volant' means flying; 'ship' covers river, sea and space craft alike
  • Tony Ellis: Tzadkiel's volant ship is a spacecraft; several spacecraft exist, so the Captain's 'only ship' claim is doubtful
  • Jeff Wilson: Heraldic 'volant' should not be read literally as a spaceship clue; the Ship's bottle-brush sails and hull are unlike seagoing vessels
  • matthew: Urth has several kinds of ships, and Tad's ship alone crosses universes and time
  • Roy C. Lackey: Wolfe uses 'ship' for sea vessels in the books, citing the Samru's swivel gun and Baldanders' remark

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Robots, androids and artificial life, Names and their meanings

2007-11-28 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the ship on Severian's tomb is certainly a spaceship and probably Tzadkiel's, since heraldic 'volant' means literally flying rather than figuratively moving under sail. Corrects Jeff Wilson's attribution of an earlier post, then backs the point by explaining that heraldic terms instruct an artist and that a ship in water would be 'natant'.
supports · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: The armorial ship on Severian's tomb is a spaceship; The ship on the tomb is Tzadkiel's ship; The tomb ship may be one Typhon commanded

Actually, that wasn't me, but I agree with what was said. "Volant" in heraldry most definitely *does* mean "flying," and is not to be taken figuratively - indeed, none of the descriptive terms in heraldry are to be taken figuratively, as they are intended to instruct an artist exactly how to paint the arms. If the ship were in water, it would be "natant."

There is no reasonable question that the ship in question is a spaceship. I think it quite clear that (this being Sev's tomb, or one of them), it is Tzadkiel's ship, but I can see some room for debate - i.e., it *could* be a ship of the sort in the Citadel, a sign of Autarchial (or pre-Autarchial) power.

(If the old "Sev is Typhon's son/clone/etc." theories still have any adherents, btw, this would be a minor point in favor, modulo the suggestion that the ship on the tomb is one of those Typhon once commanded.)

Cheers.

2007-11-28 Tony Ellis #

Argues that the ship on Severian's tomb is Tzadkiel's spacecraft, citing the armorial phrase 'a ship volant' and the fact that 'ship' is used for both sailing vessels and spacecraft in The Book of the New Sun. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes by rejecting the claim that the Captain says there is only one ship, pointing to at least two other spacecraft mentioned earlier and doubting humanity's empire rests on one borrowed ship.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: The ship on Severian's tomb is Tzadkiel's spacecraft

And Jeff Wilson:

Ah, but it doesn't have to resemble a water vessel, does it? In TBotNS, the word 'ship' is used interchangeably for sailing vessels and spacecraft. A ship on a coat of arms could be either.

The fact that we're told this armorial ship is 'a ship volant' is a punning clue that in this case, it's probably a spaceship. Since Tzadkiel's ship plays such a key role in Severian's life, it seems a pretty safe bet that it's meant to be Tzad's.

The whole "The Captain says there is only one ship" business, on the other hand, has never made much sense to me, as we've been told of at least two different (space)ships previously. The idea that humanity forged their great space empire solely by hitching a lift on someone else's spacecraft doesn't exactly sound plausible, either.

2007-11-28 Jeff Wilson replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the capital-S Ship in The Book of the New Sun, propelled by Inire-mirror sails sprouting from all its surfaces, would look like a bottle-brush rather than a sailing vessel. Grounds this in the text's description of the Ship and in how Severian-the-boy would picture a ship from the watercraft he knew, answering Tony Ellis's point about the word 'ship'.
proposes · close-textual-reading

But it has to visually resemble *some* kind of ship to be recognizable as such, and...

Apparently, there is only one capital-s Ship, distinguished from other, lesser ships by its Inire-mirror sails that propel it through the void and between Briah and Yesod. These sails resemble conventional rigging, but sprout from all outer surfaces of The Ship, so that a picture of The Ship would look more like a bottle-brush than a sailing vessel, or any of the other airborne or spaceborne craft described. Severian-the-Autarch might still call such a picture "a ship" in his writing of the incident, but the actions and thoughts he attributes to Severian-the-boy are consistent with seeing a drawing resembling watercraft visible nearby or in books and art available to him.

2007-11-28 Matthew Malthouse replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the Urth ships come in several kinds: conventional golden-age SF hulks forming the Citadel towers, Typhon's return vessels, and Tad's ship, which alone crosses between universes and time. Grounds this in the isolation of Urth from the galaxy, suggesting Tad's ship may be the only one seen in Urth's skies for generations, and rebuts Tony Ellis's claim that there is only one ship.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Tad's ship is the only ship between universes

Undoubtedly there are other types of space ship. First (in narrative sequence) are the golden age of SF style ships the hulks of which form Severian's Matachin tower, the Witches tower and presumably the other "towers" of the Citadel - which is itself referred to as the "old spaceport" at one point.

Then there are the ships by which Typhon returned to Urth - which might be

Second is the ship that the Heirodules descend to Baldander's tower in although I seem to recall some doubt that this was a really a space ship. Was it not implied at some point that it was a fake, or at most a tender from Tad's ship?

My take is that humanity undoubtedly made its empire "teeming among the stars" with ships that we'd deem (in SFnal terms) as very conventional while Tad's ship is a fantastic object being able not only to travel the corridors of time but also pass the boundaries of Briah.

Given the isolation of Urth from the galaxy it's quite possible that not only is Tad's ship unique in making the voyage between universes (and passing itself many, many times in doing so) but is also the only ship seen in Urth's skies for many generations. Not a ship but The Ship.

Matthew

2007-11-28 Mark Millman replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues against Jeff Wilson's reading that Severian's picture of Tzadkiel's ship resembles watercraft, holding that the phrase 'ship volant' implies a full spread of sails rather than an oared vessel. Grounds this in the lack of subtlety in heraldic art and in Severian's education and unusually tenacious memory, which may have supplied images of spacegoing ships.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's picture of Tzadkiel shows a sailing ship; Severian recalls images of spacegoing ships

Dear Mr. Wilson:

No; it's unlikely, for example, that the vehicle in question is a flyer, as Severian distinguishes those quite clearly from ships.

I suspect that "ship volant" is meant, in this case, to imply that the ship bears a full spread of sail or something close to it, so it's also unlikely to be an oared ship, such as the thalamegus (I believe) that Severian encounters on Gyoll.

Heraldic art, as you're no doubt aware, is not remarkable for its subtlety. It seems very likely that the sails are prominently featured, and in such a way that the result does not resemble a bottle-brush. Whether or not they obscure the hull is another question; but it may not be a relevant one.

Recall also that the torturers' apprentices are relatively well educated, and that Severian possesses an unusually tenacious memory. By the time he encounters the tomb, or at least by the time from which he recounts the memory, he may well have seen, and having seen will remember, images of spacegoing ships.

Best,

Mark Millman

2007-11-28 Tony Ellis replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the 'volant' ship on Tzadkiel's coat of arms, in heraldic sense of depicted in flight, should be read as a spaceship given the book's deliberate ambiguity between sailing ships and spaceships. Responds to Tony Ellis's suggestion that volant means moving swiftly under sail, pointing out the heraldic meaning and noting the story's build-up to a space journey.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Tzadkiel's ship volant on coat of arms is a spaceship

I'm not sure Dan'l would thank you for putting my words in his mouth :-)

The ship is on a coat of arms, and volant has a special meaning in heraldry: 'depicted as in flight'. Or even 'with wings spread as in flight', I see after a little Googling.

For all I know Wolfe had in mind a sailing ship with bird wings on it. Or a 'conventional' spaceship with wings and booster rockets. I don't think it matters. In a book where a deliberate ambiguity is created between sailing ships and spaceships, and where the whole story is leading up to an all-important space journey the hero will make, I think it's a pretty good bet that for 'ship volant' on his coat of arms we're supposed to read 'spaceship'.

disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion

If you insist on strictly historical heraldry, "a ship volant" is as meaningless a device as "a serpent rampant"; you can't draw it because historical ships have no wings any more than historical serpents had feet.

Argues that heraldic ships are symbolic conventions, not pictures, so a 'ship volant' would be drawn as a standard heraldic sailing ship rather than a spaceship. Supports this with the heraldic panther, whose Incensed form descends from its symbolic description in the Physiologus, and links it to brunians's picture of Tzadkiel's ship as a polygonal hull with masts.
supports · source-and-allusion · theories: Ship volant is a standard heraldic description

Remember that heraldry works by symbolism. In good heraldry you don't use naturalistic depictions. The whole idea is to have a picture which can be adequately described by words, so that any particular reproduction of it (by an artist working only from the description) would be identifiable. In heraldry it is the description which is important. A particular picture painted by a particular artist has no independent meaning, and any rendering, provided it follows the rules, is as good as another.

If you use one word to mean both sea and spaceships, you would use only one symbol to mean both - simply because your painter would have no idea which one you meant. If "ship volant" is a standard heraldic description (and it looks like it) it would be depicted in a standard manner - as a "heraldic ship" (probably some kind of sailing ship).

Eg, that is why you have heraldic animals - they often started as inaccurate descriptions of normal animals and got codified. Eg heraldic panther:

2007-11-29 Roy C. Lackey replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that Wolfe uses the word "ship" for sea vessels in The Book of the New Sun, answering Tony Ellis's claim that the word means spaceship on Urth. Cites Severian's description of the Samru's swivel gun in The Citadel and Baldanders' remark about people on ships in the air or void in The Urth of the New Sun.
answers · close-textual-reading

CITADEL, chapter XXXIII, second page. Severian, speaking of the _Samru_, "The ship's only real armament, a swivel gun . . ."

The appendix also mentions "Severian's failure to distinguish clearly between space-going and ocean-going craft." [in the "manuscripts"]

Baldanders also says, in answering Valeria's question about who will live if the continents die, "Those on ships, possibly. Those whose ships are in the air or in the void, certainly." It is clear from the context that the first sentence refers to ships at sea. (URTH, chapter XLII, p-300)
