1998-07-10 Dan Parmenter #

Asks why Wolfe's characters so often drink mate and whether this reflects a South American or Borges influence, and notes that characters shake their heads rather than say no. The poster sees the habits across several books and stories but says he doesn't think they carry any significance.
questions · source-and-allusion

So why do Wolfe characters almost always drink mate (that's ma-TAY, no accent ague here, alas) and if they don't, at least mention it? More of the Borges/South America influence?

I've also noticed that Wolfe characters are more likely to shake their heads than say "no".

I don't think there's any significance to these things, but I have noted them in quite a few of his books and stories.

D

1998-07-11 William H. Ansley #

Observes that Wolfe's characters often travel without food or shelter while injured and fear growing too weak to survive, citing Severian, Cutthroat in "Tracking Song", and Mierax Andros in "Lukora". Notes that "Sweet Forest Maid" pokes fun at this pattern, with Lenor packing light and not worrying about her strength, and says Wolfe mocks his own quirks.
informs · close-textual-reading

I am now skipping around in my Gene Wolfe collections. One thing I've noticed that happens to his characters frequently is that they must travel without food or shelter and often with an injury. These characters then usually worry about becoming too weak from hunger and cold (and their injury, if they have one) to continue long enough to find food and shelter (and medical attention).

Severian muses this way in the mountains and, I think, other places. Cutthroat (who is pretty close to being a yaup) in "Tracking Song," thinks along these lines more than once. In "Lukora" [1] Mierax Andros is described after a long day hiking without food: "She was very tired by then, and she knew that she was no longer so strong as she had been." I am sure there are many, many other examples.

In light of this it amused me to find this passage in "Sweet Forest Maid" [2]. (This seems to me to be rather a pointless tale, but I'm probably just missing it.)

A man would have tried to carry enough food for three large meals a day. Lenor took tea and matches and some hard baking chocolate, a box of dried figs, sugar, and a small pan. She expected to lose weight, had fasted before (although she called it dieting), and suffered no neurotic doubts about her strength of today as compared to her strength of yesterday.

You can't get too irritated at Wolfe for his quirks when he pokes fun at them himself.

[1] _Endangered Species_, TOR paperback, p. 356.
[2] ibid., pp. 371-372.

William Ansley
