The thread: The thread asked whether Wolfe's ranking of governments in The Book of the New Sun, with divine attachment highest and Divine Right near the top, is meant as Wolfe's own politics or as something Severian's teachers present to be questioned. Craig Brewer, John Watkins and James Wynn read the ranking as endorsing a sacral, Thomist or Lewisian view of rule, while Jerry Friedman argued Malrubius's questions expose its flaws and that monarchy may be merely the least evil. Matthew Weber sided with a Thomist reading, and Stanisław Bocian tied the ranking to real history. Son of Witz kept doubting that a monarch could be ideal in practice. The thread drifted into Gwern Branwen's Ascian-as-mandarin theory and a side dispute over Mao quotations, and ended with Friedman and Son of Witz trading textual evidence about democracy without clear agreement.
  • Craig Brewer: ranking shows a right and wrong kind of attachment, supported by the Torturers' obeisance and Lewis on democracy
  • John Watkins: Wolfe never presents monarchy as ideal unless the ruler is God, drawing on Lewis's Membership
  • James Wynn: democracies slide into personal rule, and Divine Right ranks just after Anarchy
  • Jerry Friedman: Malrubius's questions show the classification is meant to be questioned, with monarchy possibly the least evil
  • Matthew Weber: Wolfe depicts God-established governance and human departure from it, fitting his Thomism
  • Son of Witz: doubts a monarch can be ideal in a non-ideal world and asks whether the ranking jokes about present politics

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Gods, angels and the Increate, Justice, torture and mercy

because of specific leaders or laws, but because of
abstract qualities such as popular voting, representation rather than deciding everything by referendum, checks and balances, rule of law, protection of individual freedoms, and maybe others.

Of course some would. Not me.

I take "ideal" to mean "existing in the realm of ideas".

Because it allows for personal attachment, a praiseworthy trait in humans and dogs (as Master Malrubius's questions are designed to point out) and possibly a more reliable
kind of loyalty than an abstract system can claim.

I think we're supposed to take the classification

democracy is the highest form. Condition of Primitivity? Or just a way of putting the reader's likely preconceptions on the table in order to subvert them?

Hello!

Yes, the classification of types of government in Wolfe repeats modern views. It can seem unfamiliar because it uses somewhat more precise terms than in school class, but if you will look into some good handbook, eg Giovanni Sartori, THE THEORY OF DEMOCRACY REVISITED.

http://www.ou.edu/cas/psc/booksartori.htm

you will see essentially that kind of definition. "Attachment to an abstraction conceived as including the body of electors, other bodies giving rise to them, and numerous other elements, largely ideal."

This has many functions. It makes us think whether our instinctive support for our own form of government is necessarily right, of course. But it is also historically correct. The ecumenical empires (this is a technical term) of such a sort as Commonwealth, do not give rise to new theories; they arise after the previous form of government failed, very often out of desperation.

In the Roman Empire the emperors were worshipped as gods, as a matter of common-sense policy. At the same time in elite schools the future administrators of Empire were composing speeches praising Harmodius and Aristogeton, the Tyrannicides, and proclaiming that the freedom was the highest value, and the killing of a tyrant the greatest service to the Republic. Copies of their statues were mass-produced.
