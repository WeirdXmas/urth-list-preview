Evil Guest Reviewed in Washington Post SF Roundup

The thread: Thread began with James Crossley linking a Strange Horizons review of An Evil Guest and asking whether Wolfe's recent books hide depths but put off first-time readers. Matthew Keeley noted a Washington Post SF roundup that omitted Wolfe's column. Crossley called the novel enjoyable but generic, with clunky dialogue and obscure motivation, and suspected Wolfe's plainness hides sophisticated ideas. Aaron and Fernando Q. Gouvea pushed back, calling it a page turner and fully accessible on a first reading, and Gouvea said its unexplained mysteries need not be solved. Stuart Hamm sided with Wolfe's frustration over fans' demands for familiar material. The thread ended without agreement, with the first-read accessibility question left open.
  • James Crossley: recent Wolfe books hide depths but may be off-putting on first reading
  • Fernando Q. Gouvea: An Evil Guest is accessible and fun, and unexplained mysteries need not be solved
  • aaron: An Evil Guest is a page turner that holds interest throughout
  • Stuart Hamm: sympathizes with Wolfe's frustration at fans wanting familiar material
  • Matthew Keeley: reports the Washington Post roundup omitted Wolfe's column

2008-10-13 James Crossley replying to Fernando Q. Gouvea #

Argues that An Evil Guest is fairly enjoyable but offers little beyond generic potboiler fare, citing clunky dialogue and obscure motivation, and suspects Wolfe's plain exterior hides sophisticated ideas. Qualifies an earlier use of 'off-putting' while responding to Fernando Q. Gouvea's view that the book is accessible and fun on a first reading.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: An Evil Guest is smuggling ideas beneath a simple pirate yarn

I may have overstated my case by using the word "off-putting," I suppose. I found _Evil Guest_ fairly enjoyable to read, after all, even without considering what subtleties might be present in the text. But for someone who's not on the lookout for such subtleties, I'm not sure that the book offers anything that any number of (for lack of a better word) generic potboilers do, and it provides a fair share of obstacles to the casual reader, too, including clunky dialogue and obscure motivation.

Being an admirer of Wolfe, I realize that many of these apparent flaws are probably calculated and may actually offer inroads to deeper understanding, but it seems to me that once upon a time, Wolfe also offered more superficial pleasures in his prose. Not every book can be written with as rich a vocabulary as the New Sun series, of course, but there's certainly a middle ground between that and the frankly pedestrian writing in this latest novel.

I just get the feeling that Wolfe is taking pleasure in disguising glittering sophistication with the blandest exteriors he can possibly create, almost as if he's smuggling his ideas. "Pirates popular these days? I'll give you a pirate novel that traffics in all the swashbuckling cliches imaginable, and I'll include a straight-shooting narrator/protagonist who explains everything that's happening. Wouldn't want anyone to think I was one of those smarty-pants authors who's trying to make my readers feel dumb. Nope, I'm just a safe, simple yarn-spinner. Buy my book instead of that Star Wars tie-in novel . . ."

James

2008-10-13 Fernando Q. Gouvea replying to James Crossley #

Argues that An Evil Guest is perfectly accessible and fun on a first reading, that its moral status for Reis and Chase stays unclear, and that its unexplained mysteries need not be solved. Responds to James Crossley's claim that Wolfe's recent work may be off-putting, citing Orson Scott Card's earlier view of Free Live Free and comparing Wolfe's refusal to explain with Finney's Circus of Dr. Lao.
disputes · author-and-biography · theories: Reis and Chase both try to be moral

I don't find the initial experience of reading Wolfe off-putting, no. Orson Scott Card once wrote a review (of, I think, Free Live Free, but I may be misremembering) saying that for him Wolfe had reached or passed that point. So folks can disagree on that.

I'm a little amazed at the Strange Horizons review. I find An Evil Guest perfectly accessible on a first reading, and a great deal of fun. It certainly doesn't clarify the moral status of Reis and Chase, but hey, when does life ever do that? (My tendency is to think that both of them are trying to be as moral as they can, though they approach this with very different premises.) There are certainly mysteries all over the place, but not all mysteries need to be explained.

Someone mentioned that there is some sort of connection between Finney's "The Circus of Dr. Lao" and "An Evil Guest" (at the very least because of the name "Woldercan"). But one of the remarkable features of "Dr Lao" is exactly Finney's refusal to come up with any explanations at all. The circus is there and it is what it is, and the novel is concerned with other things. Similarly, "Guest" doesn't explain a lot of its mysteries; perhaps it's just that Wolfe is interested in other things.

It seems to me that "An Evil Guest" is something of a romp, and it can and should be enjoyed as such. Yes, Wolfe's conservative take on a lot of things is evident in it, and yes, there are a lot of mysteries left over after my first reading. So what?

Fernando
