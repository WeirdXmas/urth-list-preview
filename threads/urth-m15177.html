SINGLE Gene Wolfe literature universe?

Urth list · 18 messages · 2010-05-10 to 2010-05-11

2010urthThe Book of the New SunAllan AndersonMilton JacksonJohn WatkinsJosh Gellersean akoCraig BrewerStuart HammJeff WilsonRob ThorntonJohn Smith
The thread: Sean Ako proposed that all of Gene Wolfe's works share one universe on a single timeline, citing coincidences, similar names and cameos. Craig Brewer recalled Wolfe saying only the Sun books connect, and Jeff Wilson argued the cosmologies are incompatible and that linking the series adds nothing since the books already share thematic unity. Milton Jackson repeatedly defended a shared universe as compatible with originality, citing Howard and Burroughs, and later called the question academic while saying he personally cares more about Wolfe's ideas. Wilson kept doubting that real linkage can be established. The thread ended in impasse, then drifted into a light joking vote for favourite heroines.
  • Sean Ako: all Wolfe works share one universe and timeline
  • Craig Brewer: Wolfe said only the Sun books connect; universes may be reconcilable via mirrors and time travel
  • Milton Jackson: a shared universe is legitimate and does not reduce Wolfe's originality
  • Jeff Wilson: incompatible cosmologies make a single universe pointless and thematic unity suffices
  • John Smith: joking, no argument, declared Hyacinth the hottest heroine

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Deep time and ruins, Time loops and causality

2010-05-10 Jeff Wilson replying to Milton Jackson #

Argues that linking Wolfe's works into one shared universe is a trend among big-name SF authors that adds nothing, since his books already have thematic unity. Cites Heinlein's later Future History stories and Asimov's Foundation and Robot crossovers as examples of digressive continuity-building, and replies to Milton Jackson's defense of Wolfe's shared universe.
disputes · genre-and-craft

It has become increasingly common for big name SF authors to link together their various works as if they were intended to form a grand design from the beginning, lack of continuity or thematic unity be damned.

Even Heinlein, who really did have a grand design in mind fairly early in his career, went on to hook up stories unrelated to his Future History timeline in his later works, which were generally considered digressive and inferior to his previous actioners. Asimov's later Foundation sequels branched out to explicitly touch his Robot series and some former stand-alone novels, and the unruly vinculum kept growing posthumously, even.

GW certainly has thematic unity enough, but what's the point of hooking it all together? It's not like the stories aren't deep enough as it is.

2010-05-10 Milton Jackson replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that linking Wolfe's books in one shared universe, like Robert Howard's and Edgar Rice Burroughs's series, does not make his work cliched or reduce his originality. Responds to Jeff Wilson's claim by listing Howard and Burroughs series and Wolfe's The Sorcerer's House, An Evil Guest and Pirate's Freedom, noting such works share only a universe.
disputes · genre-and-craft

Well, what of that? Robert Howard tangentially linked his Kull, Bran Mak Morn, and Conan series and by extension much of his other work, but each was an independent series reflecting in a different way the author's ideas. In a similar fashion, Edgar Rice Burroughs tangentially linked his Barsoom, Amtor, Tarzan, Caspak, Moon Maid, and Pellucidar series. Having multiple series set in the same universe at different times that explore the author's ideas in different ways is not something that makes the author's work cliched. If the Book of the New Sun is set in the same universe as The Sorcerer's House or An Evil Guest or Pirate's Freedom or even Shields of Mars, the stories themselves have nothing in common except maybe a common universe. That detracts nothing form Wolfe's vision as an author or his originality in the conception of his literary worlds.
