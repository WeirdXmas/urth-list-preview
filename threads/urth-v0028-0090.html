Zoroastrianism? Buddhism?

Urth list · 5 messages · 1999-09-03 to 1999-09-05

1999urthThe Book of the New SunJim HenleyDhSunandaAlex David Groce
The thread: Participants debated whether Wolfe's Vajjian-style speech restrictions in the Ascian passage parody Buddhist texts or only the Ascians' misuse of them. DhSunanda argued the book parodies the Ascian use of Buddhist teaching to enforce conformity, citing memorised texts replacing spirit and a Shadow of the Torturer remark on concepts and words. Alex David Groce questioned whether the parody targets Buddhism or an Ascian version, stressed that speech is not truly prevented, and later argued against a simple innovation-versus-tradition reading, pointing to Vodalus and the guild's customs. Jim Henley only acknowledged the speech-precept explanation without analysis. The thread stayed among the two main posters and ended without resolution.
  • DhSunanda: Wolfe quotes Buddhism fairly straight; the Ascians misuse its teaching, so the parody is of the Ascian use
  • Alex David Groce: speech is not truly prevented; restrictions enforce translation through lies; Wolfe avoids simple tradition-versus-innovation contrasts
  • Jim Henley: acknowledged the Buddhist speech-precept explanation without adding analysis

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Power, rulers and politics, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

1999-09-03 DhSunanda #

Argues that the Ascian's speech in the quoted passage parodies Buddhist texts rather than only Communist ones, identifying its question and four causes of disagreement with a Buddhist source. Grounds this on the Buddha's answer to King Ajatasattu about the Vajjians' state and the traditional four speech precepts, which the poster says the Ascian's list rearranges.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Ascian's speech parodies Buddhist texts

Hi, I'm new too, and the archive search doesn't seem to work for me, so I don't know if the Buddhist side of the Ascians has been discussed before. I know nothing about Zoroastrianism, but the Ascian's talk struck me on first reading as a parody of both Communist and Buddhist texts. Leaving aside the Communist....

'How can a state be vigorous?' is a question asked of the Buddha (via an intermediary) by King Ajatasattu about the Vajjians. (Ajatasattu wants to use the answer to subvert the Vajjian State, and thus wage a winning war). The Buddha lists 48 conditions that under which a state might be expected to prosper and not decline.

The four causes of disagreement are a direct translation, if slight rearrangement, of the traditional four speech precepts ('I undertake to abstain from false speech, harsh speech, useless speech, slanderous speech'). Traditional Buddhist precepts (guidelines for ethical action) are divided into those for the body (physical actions), those for the speech (communication acts) and those for the mind (wrong views about reality).

--Sunanda.

1999-09-03 Alex David Groce #

Asks whether Wolfe is parodying the Buddhist text on the Vajjians' state, or an Ascian version of it, given what Loyal-17 says about restricted speech. Argues that the Ascian restrictions on language enforce translation through a medium of lies, and that speech is not really prevented, offering a John 8:32 epigraph as a counterpoint to Orwellian and Whorfian readings.
questions · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Ascian speech restrictions parody Buddhist speech precepts

So, then, is Wolfe parodying the Buddhist text, or the Ascian version thereof? After all, what Loyal-17 says most strongly suggests that the problem is (A) the restrictions imposed on the Ascians are in fact enforcing "real speech" to be translated through a medium that repeats lies (about the wonderfulness of the Ascian way) and (B) it doesn't, in the end, prevent much from being said (the anti-Orwell/Whorfian subtext).

1999-09-04 DhSunanda #

Argues that Wolfe quotes a Buddhist text fairly straight while the Ascians use its teaching to enforce mindless conformity, making the book a parody of the Ascian use rather than of Buddhism itself. Grounds this in the idea that memorised texts replace acting on their spirit, and points to a remark in The Shadow of the Torturer about concepts being weaker than the words that bind them, plus a recurring tradition-versus-experience theme across the Urth books.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Ascians parody Buddhist teaching rather than Buddhism itself

Hi Alex,

Well, Wolfe quotes the Buddhist text pretty straight (if that is what he is doing; he's a tricky writer to pin down), but the Ascians are using the teaching to create a State of mindless conformity. That's the exact opposite of what Buddhism aims at.

In that sense it is a parody of the Buddhist text. Wolfe could be parodying the followers of religions in general, saying '"look at the effects of memorising texts rather than acting on their spirit").

Of course, as you point out reality can always break through cliche. That's true, but difficult. Doesn't Shadow somewhere near the beginning have a remark to the effect that concepts are much weaker than the words we use to bind them? (Sorry for not looking it up. My copy has unaccountably vanished).

The theme of innovation and experience versus tradition and ritual runs through all five Urth books.

--Sunanda.

1999-09-05 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Wolfe does not set innovation against tradition in simple black-and-white terms, citing Vodalus as a false innovator and the torturers' guild's surviving ancient customs as good traditions. Suggests Severian finds the Increate's reality through direct experience, and wonders whether Wolfe's Buddhist-like textual parallels make Communism a secular parody of Buddhism as well as Christianity.
proposes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Vodalus is a false innovator and parody of Typhon; Communism is a secular parody of Buddhism

I'm not sure what the reference is to--Severian's statement about symbols (the "We believe we create symbols..."), perhaps?

At any rate, as usual Wolfe is not presenting a simple black/white dichotomy between innovation and experience and "cliches". Vodalus is, in a real sense, the "innovator" as opposed to the Autarch. But Vodalus' "reach for the stars, reclaim the future" turns out to be a false coin, at best a weak parody of Typhon's malignant grandness, used as a tool both by the Autarch and by Erebus and Abaia. The torturer's guild is another prime example--the best of it is what remains of ancient traditions--the feast of St. Catherine, etc. On the other hand, Severian's purpose in many ways seems to be to discover the points where the Increate's Reality shines through the fabric of life on Urth, and the good traditions arise from these direct experiences of a higher order.

The textual similarity to the Buddhist texts is striking. I'm just wondering what Wolfe's aims were--maybe nothing more complicated than suggesting that just as Communism can be seen as a secular parody of Christianity, it can be seen as a secular parody of elements of Buddhism as well.
