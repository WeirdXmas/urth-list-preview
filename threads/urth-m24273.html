Editor in China requesting help with translation of certain sentences in BotNS

Urth list · 7 messages · 2013-12-16 to 2013-12-17

2013urthThe Book of the New SunAndy RobertsonGwern BranwenDavid StockhoffFred KiescheAntonin ScriabinDaniel Petersen
The thread: A Chinese editor translating The Book of the New Sun asked the Urth list (relayed by Daniel Otto Jack Petersen) to explain obscure words, including 'bluer of cheek', 'elector' and 'patroness' from a card-game passage, and an ambiguous 'she'. Antonin Scriabin offered a blue-blood reading of 'bluer of cheek' and a Solitaire reading of the court cards, which David Stockhoff built on with Holy Roman Empire and Commonwealth evidence. Fred Kiesche proposed a plainer heavier-beard meaning, and Gwern Branwen argued the suits are Tarot minor arcana. Stockhoff then argued the card-game reading fails because electors could be kings, and Andy Robertson gave the beard and swamp-mat glosses, leaving the readings competing without a settled consensus.
  • Antonin Scriabin: 'bluer of cheek' alludes to noble blue blood; elector and patroness are Solitaire jack and queen
  • Fred Kiesche: 'bluer in cheek' simply means a heavier beard
  • David Stockhoff: elector takes a Solitaire jack's place, king is an autarch; card reading fails since electors could be kings
  • Gwern Branwen: the suits are Tarot minor arcana, so elector and patroness are court cards
  • Andy Robertson: 'bluer of cheek' means darker stubble; 'She' is the swampy plant mat
  • Daniel Otto Jack Petersen: relayed the editor's questions without offering answers

Themes: Names and their meanings, Power, rulers and politics, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

2013-12-16 Antonin Scriabin replying to Daniel Otto Jack Petersen #

Offers glosses for several obscure words from The Book of the New Sun for a Chinese translator: 'bluer of cheek' as a blue-blood reference to nobility, 'elector' and 'patroness' as the Jack and Queen of a solitaire layout, and 'theocenter' as godhead. Responds to Daniel Otto Jack Petersen's relay of a Goodreads query, admitting uncertainty on one term and offering to check the full context in his copy.

Well, "bluer of cheek" is a variation on the term "blue-blood", of course a reference to nobility ... you could see the surface veins of those who, because of their stature in society, had very pale skin (they didn't have to work outside at physical labor, thus acquiring a tan).

In Solitaire, a black (or red) Jack is placed "on top" of a Queen of the opposite color (and a different suit, necessarily) ... so perhaps the "elector" is the Jack and the "patroness" is the Queen?

"Theocenter" might simply mean "godhead", or a reference to the oneness and simplicity thereof, as the root of all existence, in a Judeo-Christian sense. But don't quote me.

Not sure about the other one, I'll have to page through my copy at home to get the full context.

2013-12-16 David Stockhoff replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Proposes that the elector in the translated sentences takes the place of a Solitaire jack, the king is an autarch with the queen as patroness, and asks whether the autarchy was once elective. Builds on Antonin Scriabin's blue-blood and Solitaire reading by citing Holy Roman Empire electors and the Commonwealth's sparse noble titles, such as waldgrave, as evidence.
proposes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Solitaire card system encodes ranks in the sentences; Elector stands in for the jack; Autarch's queen becomes a patroness; Autarchy was originally elective

Blue cheeks are cheeks with one-day stubble (if you have black hair and pale skin).

Otherwise, I agree. The Solitaire question is especially interesting, since Wolfe could not possibly have slipped those in without working out a system. An elector is an HRE prince
(archbishop/count/duke/margrave/king), thus probably taking the place of the jack.

I can only assume, given the milieu, that the king is an autarch, who of course has no queen so the queen becomes a patroness. This sounds like an ecclesiastical rank but it could mean "patrona" (city patroness) or female "patron saint."

Either way, one must wonder who it is that the elector elects. Could the autarchy originally have been at least theoretically elective?

(There seem to be very few noble titles in the Commonwealth; the highest title I can recall is "waldgrave." The autarch can't allow any ranks near his own.)

Proposes that the suits in the card-game passage of BotNS are Tarot minor arcana rather than playing cards, so 'elector' and 'patroness' correspond to court cards. Responds to Daniel Otto Jack Petersen's question by citing Wolfe's earlier Tarot references in The Sorcerer's House and 'The Computer Iterates the Greater Trumps', and by linking 'elector' to German kings.

So to put this in further context:

Now, when I hear 'soothsayers' in close proximity to 'one suit', my instant expectation is that we are talking about Tarot and fortunetelling. Foretelling a love affair is exactly the sort of thing Tarot might be used for. And this would be far from the first reference to Tarot by Wolfe: I'm sure we haven't forgotten _Sorceror's House_ or "The Computer Iterates the Greater Trumps", and a search on urth.net will turn up still others.

The suits or minor arcana of the Tarot include the knave, the knight, the queen, and the king. 'elector' sounds like the German kings, and 'patronness' is a reasonable description of a queen.

2013-12-16 David Stockhoff replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that a card-game reading of the 'one suit' passage in The Book of the New Sun fails, because the Holy Roman Empire's electors included actual kings, so an elector can be a king while only one autarch exists apart from the nobility. Responds to Daniel Otto Jack Petersen's Tarot reading by offering a rank-based argument drawn from the court structure described in the book.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: solitaire=elector=jack identification is wrong

That works out a lot better than solitaire=elector=jack.

One of the HR electors was an actual king, so there can be several kings/princes; there can be only one autarch, and he is completely separate from the nobility. That means the elector can easily be a king.

Knights can woo queens, but pages, never.

2013-12-17 Andy Robertson replying to David Stockhoff #

Explains two passages in The Book of the New Sun for a Chinese translator: 'bluer of cheek' means darker stubble on the face, shaven or bearded. Glosses the pronoun 'She' as the swampy plant mat under Severian, which the boat cannot reach because it lacks a sharp edge.
answers · close-textual-reading

"Bluer of cheek"= more and darker stubble on theface, if shaven. A thick black beard if not shaven.

And in this bit

"She" refers to the condition of the ground, or rasther the swampy mat of plants, on which Severian is walking. This has no sharply defined edge, and
so the boat, which is in a relatively weed-free bit of water, can't get close to the nearly solid bits where Severian is.
