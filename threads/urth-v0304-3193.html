Nabokov: Pale Fire

Urth list · 5 messages · 2003-04-16 to 2003-04-16

2003urthgordon meuseTerry LagoGareth JelleyDan'l Danehy-OakesMichael Straight
The thread: Readers on the Urth list discussed Nabokov's Pale Fire as a possible read for Wolfe fans. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and one poster found it too opaque to finish, though appealing in a Wolfe-like way, while Michael Straight and Terry Lago praised its unreliable narrator, puzzles and prose, with Lago calling it his best Nabokov so far. gordon meuse recommended D. Boyd's book on the novel, calling its proposed solution complex but well-documented, and Gareth Jelley suggested Kazuo Ishiguro's novels as Wolfe-like alternatives. The thread ended as a set of recommendations rather than a settled debate.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Pale Fire is hard to get into but appealing to Wolfe readers, and Eco's The Name of the Rose may suit Lupine-style readers
  • Michael Straight: Pale Fire is a fine Wolfe-appreciation read, praised for its unreliable narrator and puzzles, and he asks for more Nabokov
  • Terry Lago: Pale Fire is Nabokov's best book so far, with Lolita a must-read and fewer puzzles than Pale Fire
  • gordon meuse: Boyd's book on Pale Fire helps readers who gave up, its puzzle solution is complex but well-documented
  • Gareth Jelley: Ishiguro's The Unconsoled, Remains of the Day and When We Were Orphans suit Wolfe fans through unreliable narrators and games with the reader

Themes: Unreliable narration, Books, libraries and writing, Stories within stories

2003-04-16 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Reports that the poster found Nabokov's Pale Fire too opaque to get past twenty pages across three attempts, though admiring its Wolfe-like appeal. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's remark that Pale Fire is easy to read, and ends by suggesting Eco's The Name of the Rose as a Lupine-style book.
chats

Rostrum writes (excerpt, excerpt):

Alternative view: I tried three times to read PALE FIRE and never once succeeded in getting more than 20 pages into it. It is, to me, one of the most opaque pieces of writing in the world. Which was a damn shame, as I could see that there's a lot of stuff in there that I would enjoy -- would "tickle the Wolfe pleasure centers" -- but Nabokov's writing lies in a range that I can't process for some reason.

(I'll try again in a few years, I'm sure. Sometimes things like this suddenly clear up. The first few times I tried to read Charles Harness's THE ROSE -- which, by the way, I recommend very, very highly -- I had the same experience; when I turned 30 it suddenly became a transcendant reading experience. Some things I just have to grow into, I guess.)

I suppose that some people might consider this a drawback. 8*)

Hey, has anyone mentioned THE NAME OF THE ROSE as a rather Lupine book in many ways?

2003-04-16 Michael Straight #

Reports that the poster has just finished Nabokov's Pale Fire and found it appealing to Wolfe readers, praising its unreliable narrator, ambiguous plot puzzles, playful index and prose. Notes it was recommended as a Wolfe-appreciation read and asks for other Nabokov recommendations.
chats

I just finished "Pale Fire" by Nabokov. I read it because someone said "If you like Wolfe you'll like this," and it was mentioned a while back when we tried to put together a reading list of books that would help you appreciate Wolfe (search the archives for "Wolfe Library").

I think this book hit the Gene-Wolfe-Pleasure-Centers in my brain more than any other book not written by Wolfe. It's got the ultimate unreliable narrator, ambiguous plot puzzles left for the reader to put together, fun games with the text (the index is great -- I loved the bit about the crown jewels, ghosts, and absolutely beautiful writing. I was also surprised that it was not a hard book to read, certainly not as hard as some of Wolfe's.

The only drawback is it might make you more self-conscous about spinning theories about Wolfe's stories.

Are there any other Nabokov books you would recommend for Wolfe fans (or just for me)?

-- Rostrum

2003-04-16 Terry Lago #

Chats that the poster loves Nabokov's Pale Fire and considers it his best book among those read so far. Adds that Lolita is the other must-read, praising its prose despite its ugly subject, and notes it has fewer puzzles than Pale Fire.
chats

I *love* _Pale Fire_...definitely Nabokov's best...at least of what I've read so far. I'd say the other must-read is the infamous _Lolita_. It's a beautifully written book (even though the subject matter is rather ugly) though it certainly has fewer puzzles than _Pale Fire_.

T.

2003-04-16 gordon meuse #

Recommends D. Boyd's book Nabokov's Pale Fire to readers who abandoned Nabokov's Pale Fire, calling Boyd's proposed solution to its puzzle complex, almost unbelievable, but well-documented. Responds to the quoted complaint that the novel is opaque by noting that Boyd's book quotes the novel extensively and is itself a good read.
informs

For those who have either read "Pale Fire" or have attempted it and put it down, I highly recommend "Nabokov's Pale Fire", by D. Boyd. I admit that I read it before I read Pale Fire, but it is such a good read itself and quotes extensively from Pale Fire itself. It attempts to solve one of the most complicated literary puzzles ever. His solution is complex and almost unbelievable, but very well-documented. You will never read Nabokov the same ever again.
