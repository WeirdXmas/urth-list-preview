Alzabo elixir not so far-fetched?

Urth list · 6 messages · 2013-07-13 to 2013-07-14

2013urthThe Book of the New SunJerry FriedmanAndy RobertsonAntónio Pedro MarquesGerry QuinnDavid Stockhoff
The thread: Gerry Quinn posted a news article suggesting planarian worms could regrow learned memories, and the thread asked whether this makes Wolfe's alzabo elixir, which transfers memory by eating, less far-fetched. Jerry Friedman argued the experiment concerns regaining learned behaviour after decapitation, not memory transfer through consumption, and David Stockhoff countered that the alzabo only makes sense if memories live outside the brain. Andy Robertson said the worm-eating studies were confounded by diet, and entonio objected that planarians are too simple to illuminate complex animals. Every participant disputed the science, and the thread ended with the skeptical reading prevailing rather than any agreement that the elixir is plausible.
  • Jerry Friedman: planaria results show regained behaviour after decapitation, not memory transfer by eating
  • David Stockhoff: the alzabo only works if memories are stored outside the brain, a conceit key to Wolfe
  • Andy Robertson: worm-eating experiments were confounded because control worms had a poorer diet
  • entonio: planarians have too little neural structure to say much about complex animals

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Robots, androids and artificial life

2013-07-13 Jerry Friedman replying to Gerry Quinn #

Argues that the planarian experiment reported in the linked article does not support the idea that eating part of a creature can transfer its memories, as in Wolfe's alzabo elixir. Responds to Gerry Quinn's link by noting that trained planaria regaining learned behaviour after decapitation is a different claim from memory transfer by consumption.
disputes · close-textual-reading

Planaria that had been trained to overcome their instinctive avoidance of bright lights and open spaces were decapitated.? After regenerating their heads, they could be trained to do the same things in only one lesson.

That doesn't have much to do with gaining someone's memories by eating part of them.

Jerry Friedman

2013-07-14 David Stockhoff replying to Jerry Friedman #

Argues that the planaria experiment only makes sense as support for the alzabo's memory-eating if memories are stored elsewhere in the body than the brain, a conceit the poster calls key to Wolfe's alzabo. Responds to Jerry Friedman's objection that the planaria results have little to do with gaining memories by eating part of someone, while noting other explanations for the planaria behavior exist.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Alzabo memories stored outside the brain

I assume the connection would be that memories traditionally considered to be stored on the brain were in this case stored elsewhere in the body. That seems to be a key Wolfean conceit and one presumably necessary for the alzabo effect, unless the animal always eats the brain before it mimics its dead prey. However, there may be other ways to explain this behavior in planaria.

2013-07-14 Andy Robertson replying to Jerry Friedman #

Argues that the worm-eating experiment cited for the alzabo elixir is flawed, because worms fed chopped worms learned faster from a better diet rather than from absorbed memories. Responds to Jerry Friedman's planaria remark by reporting secondhand that the control worms had an inferior diet, so nutrition, not eaten memory, explains the faster learning.
disputes · theories: Worm-eating memory experiment was methodologically flawed

I heard tell it wus a duff experiment. THe worms who were fed chopped worms were on an ideal worm diet and learned faster for that reason. The controls had an inferior diet.

2013-07-14 António Pedro Marques replying to Gerry Quinn #

Argues that planarian worms have too little neural structure to tell us much about how complex animals' bodies work, undercutting the idea that their memory-regrowing ability makes Wolfe's alzabo elixir plausible. Responds to Gerry Quinn's forwarded news article about planarians regrowing memories after decapitation, offering a brief scientific objection rather than a reading of the novel.
disputes

Planarians can hardly be said to have brains. And they aren't worms like the ones we find in our yards. They barely have any internal structure. I don't think anything very useful can be learned from them in what concerns the way the bodies of more complex animals work.
