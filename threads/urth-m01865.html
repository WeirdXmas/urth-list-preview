Why so much synchronicity?

Urth list · 12 messages · 2006-08-19 to 2006-08-20

2006urthThe Book of the New Sunjunk06Tristan DavenportAdam ThorntonBob MillerJeff WilsonDon DoggettDan'l Danehy-OakesTony EllisJesper Svedberg
The thread: Readers asked why Severian keeps meeting relatives and former acquaintances across The Book of the New Sun, and whether the coincidences are heavy-handed contrivance. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes offered Dickens-style plotting and Divine Providence as explanations, while junk06 proposed time manipulation disturbing lives and Bob Miller and don doggett linked the coincidences to Severian's selective first-person memoir. Tony Ellis and Bob Miller argued real life is full of improbable events, Tristan Davenport framed coincidence as a workshop-approved convention, and Jeff Wilson argued Nessus is underpopulated so the meetings are not really synchronicity. No consensus emerged; the thread ended with competing explanations side by side.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Wolfe borrows Dickens's coincidence and the coincidences are divine providence guiding Severian
  • junk06: coincidences may be heavy-handed, caused by time manipulation pulling people into its eddies
  • don doggett: first-person memoir selects meetings and signals meaning indirectly
  • Bob Miller: real life is equally improbable and Wolfe would use such coincidence; Divine Providence heavy-handed
  • Tony Ellis: Wolfe uses coincidence deliberately because life is improbable and he enjoys flouting convention
  • Jeff Wilson: Nessus is underpopulated and characters have logical postings, so these are not real synchronicity

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Stories within stories, Unreliable narration

Offers two explanations for the many coincidences in the series: Wolfe admires Dickens, whose coincidence-driven plots readers accept, and the coincidences are signs of divine providence guiding Severian toward his destiny. Responds to junk06's complaint that the coincidences look like ham-handed authorial contrivance, citing the Dickens example of Oliver's grandfather.
proposes · author-and-biography · theology-and-religion · theories: Coincidences are signs of divine providence guiding Severian; Wolfe admires Dickens's coincidence-driven plotting

I have two.

1. I'm pretty sure Wolfe is an admirer of Dickens. Dickens's plots are utterly coincidence-driven. (E.g., the fellow who catches Oliver, but not the Dodger, turns out to be his grandfather...) This doesn't bother his fans.

2. These are all signs of Divine Providence (or the guidance of the Hier*s, aqastors, etc.) to make sure that Severian fulfills that destiny (of which he already had some presentiment)

2006-08-19 junk06 #

Asks why Severian keeps running into relatives and former acquaintances across the New Sun books, citing examples such as the library encounters, the grave-digger, and the childhood friend met at sea. Frames the coincidences as possibly heavy-handed and invites explanations, having assumed the common genealogy theories are true.
questions · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic

I'm rereading (started with BOTNS this time, then back to Shadow).

I've read many theories on Severian's genealogy. Assuming all (or even most) of those theories are true, why does Severian end up meeting practically every single relative he has? He almost can't walk down a street without running into one.

It's not just relatives. Even as the narrator keeps mentioning how incomprehensibly populated Nessus is, he keeps running into people he knows. He even runs into people he knows out in the wilderness sometimes. Synchronicity abounds.

Meets man in library, meets same man years later racing down a street. Meets man digging a grave, meets same man years later in a boat. Has childhood friend, meets him again years later in the middle of a vast ocean.
Meets man in library, meets him years later in another library across the country.
etc etc etc for probably a dozen more examples

Why? It smells of ham handedness, as someone mentioned recently. Anyone have theories as to why so many coincidences fill the series?

2006-08-19 Don Doggett replying to Jesper Svedberg #

Argues that the many coincidences in The Book of the New Sun are explained by Severian's first-person memoir, which records only selected meetings and so signals something indirect. Responds to Jesper Svedberg's thread asking why coincidence fills the series, a question that had already drawn comparisons to Dickens and Providence.
proposes · author-and-biography · theories: Coincidences reflect Severian's selective recording of his memoir

An even simpler explanation is that the narrative is
written in the first person - It is essentially a
fictional memoir. In "real life" Sev surely meets
plenty of shmoes not worth mentioning. That's why he
doesn't mention them. It's selective recording, and
because Severian is being selective, it is probable
that he (the character, not Wolfe, though Wolfe also
of course) is trying to communicate something
indirectly. What that is is up for what is apparently
eternal debate. :)

Don

2006-08-20 Tristan Davenport replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that Wolfe's use of coincidence follows a genre convention, the workshop rule that coincidence may propel a plot but not resolve it, rather than flouting mainstream practice. Grounds this in a quarter of Wolfe's work and the example of Severian stumbling on Vodalus in the cemetery while still having to choose to eat the autarch's brains.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe follows the rule that coincidence propels but does not resolve plot

It may be that Wolfe is following a genre convention rather than flaunting a mainstream one. One of the pearls of wisdom I obtained from the Clarion writer's workshop was a rule that coincidence can propel a plot, but not resolve one. Having read about a quarter of Wolfe's ouevre, I'd say his writing is pretty consistent with this rule. (e.g. Sev can stumble upon Vodalus in the cemetary, but when he eats the previous autarch's brains, he's gotta want it.)

Then again, I've only read about a quarter, or less, of Wolfe's work. There's plenty of room still for him to flaunt that convention as well.

2006-08-20 junk06 replying to junk06 #

Argues that the frequent coincidences in The Book of the New Sun are a side effect of time manipulation on Severian's behalf, which disturbs time and pulls people into its eddies. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's comparison with Dickens's coincidence-driven plots and his Divine Providence explanation, offering a time-disturbance alternative while agreeing the coincidences create a strange mood.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: Coincidences are side effects of Severian's time manipulation

I would agree it's probably a literary device, and that it definitely gives the mood that something very strange is going on. However, I have a theory along the lines of Divine Providence.

I like to think the coincidences are a side effect of the time manipulation on Severian's behalf that is contrary to the natural flow of time. This produces some sort of disturbance in time, causing people to get caught into the disturbance, like swirling around in the eddies of a river.

Even the hierodules often seem surprised to see him. It's like they are walking merrily through time and and now and then..."Look, it's that guy again!"

2006-08-20 Tony Ellis replying to junk06 #

Argues that Wolfe uses coincidence and synchronicity deliberately because real life is full of improbable events, comparing him to Dickens's use of coincidence. Responds to junk06's complaint that the coincidences seem ham-handed, and suggests Wolfe's own outlook and enjoyment of flouting convention explain the practice.
disputes · author-and-biography · genre-and-craft

And Bob Miller:

Absolutely. Wolfe uses synchronicity because it's realistic.

As Dan'l says, Wolfe is a Dickens fan and Dickens put a lot of coincidence in his novels - but *why* did Dickens do that? Because that was how he saw the real world. A crazy place where unlikely things happened all the time. I suspect that Wolfe, who ended up married to the little girl who once lived next door, has a similar world-view.

In any event, while the modern convention in novel-writing is to shun coincidence like the plague, it really is just that: a convention. And if there's one thing Wolfe enjoys in his writing, it's flaunting those.

The Eata conincidence I'll give you, but the others you likst are hardly that. It makes sense to me for Racho to be stationed near the citadel, for Hildegrin to continue his resurrection business in the corpse-rich area of Nessus, and to meet a library janitor elsewhere in the same library.
