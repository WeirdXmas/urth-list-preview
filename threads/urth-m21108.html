What should I read?

Urth list · 13 messages · 2011-10-12 to 2011-10-13

2011urthThe Book of the Long SunjohnwwoolleyLane HaygoodMilton JacksonMatthew KnightGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffMarc AraminiJames WynnMatthew KnaptonStuart HammAntonin ScriabinDaniel Petersen
The thread: Newcomers asked which Wolfe books to read next, and the thread became a run of recommendations rather than analysis. Lane Haygood urged starting with the Solar Cycle and called The Wizard Knight weaker, mainly because he disliked its protagonist, while Antonin Scriabin and Daniel Petersen defended that book as slow but rewarding. Several posters, including Matthew Knapton, Scriabin, Gerry Quinn and Daniel Petersen, pushed The Fifth Head of Cerberus and Peace as Wolfe's best stand-alone novels, and Petersen and James Wynn also promoted the short story collections. Marc Aramini alone offered thematic analysis, tracing ghost stories and green imagery across the sun series. The thread ended in general agreement on Cerberus and Peace with no real dispute resolved.
  • Lane Haygood: Start with the Solar Cycle; The Wizard Knight is weaker, mainly because of its protagonist Abel
  • Antonin Scriabin: Peace and The Fifth Head of Cerberus are finest stand-alone novels; The Wizard Knight rewards patience
  • Daniel Petersen: Read the short story collections, especially Doctor Death; The Wizard Knight is frustrating but masterful
  • Marc Aramini: Ghost stories and vegetal green imagery form a thematic pattern across the sun series
  • James Wynn: Recommends short story collections and Cerberus; The Wizard Knight defends knightly mores in an unusual fantasy
  • Matthew Knapton: Recommends The Fifth Head of Cerberus and Peace, calling Peace strange and possibly boring

Themes: Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Mirrors, pictures and art

2011-10-12 Lane Haygood replying to Jay #

Argues that the Solar Cycle books come first for newcomers, and that The Wizard Knight is weaker than Wolfe's usual work, mainly because the poster disliked its protagonist Abel. Recommends An Evil Guest, There Are Doors and The Fifth Head of Cerberus as favourites outside the Solar books, while admitting it has not read Pirate Freedom or Home Fires.
answers

New Sun, Long Sun, Short Sun = Solar Cycle.

I personally found "The Wizard Knight" to be below Wolfe's usual quality. Or maybe I just really hated Abel as a protagonist. Either way I don't think they'd make essential Wolfe for me.

I haven't read Pirate Freedom or Home Fires, but An Evil Guest was great.

There Are Doors and the seminal Fifth Head of Cerberus remain two of my favorite non-Solar Wolfe books.

2011-10-12 johnwwoolley #

Asks which Wolfe books to read next after finishing The Book of the New Sun, Soldier of the Mist and early Long Sun, and whether the Short Sun continues Long Sun. Describes his mixed reactions to Urth of the New Sun and The Devil in a Forest and his enthusiasm for Silk and Chenille's story.
questions

Plunging into the middle of the conversation ...

Ages ago, I read "The Book of the New Sun", and loved it to the point that I was handing out copies of the first volume to anyone I could get to agree to try it. Wonderful, spectacular book. Then I read "The Urth of the New Sun" and was much less impressed; ditto with "The Devil in a Forest". Then "Soldier of the Mist", a pretty fine piece of writing and a really ambitious and strange (and rather challenging) project. The other day I started "The Book of the Long Sun", and once again, Wolfe is just blowing me away with his prose and story-telling and characters (Patera Silk, Chenille, Auk, even the bird!); I've nearly reached the end of the second volume, and am completely loving it.

So ... what else of Wolfe's should I make sure to read? I think I'd better go back and reread "New Sun", and the "Short Sun" volumes seem to be a continuation of "Long Sun". (Right?) But what besides or after those?

-- John Woolley

2011-10-13 Marc Aramini replying to Gerry Quinn #

Argues that recurring ghost stories in Peace and vegetal, green imagery across the sun series build a thematic pattern, and that the Hyacinthus story closing Return to the Whorl recalls Silk to his home among the stars and mankind's Green Man future. Cites Shadow, Citadel and the opening of Short Sun, replying to Gerry Quinn.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Hyacinthus story signals Silk's return home; Green Man future is the white fountain's destination; Recurring green and vegetal imagery structures the series; Ghost stories in Peace mirror the narrator's death

Just one more thing I wanted to note.? I always start with "themes" in any explication - the repetitive inclusion of ghost stories in Peace, for example, made a compelling pattern of ghostliness that matched up with the more discrete hints of the narrator's death.? similarly, in the sun series, from the very first two chapters of Shadow Severian thinks that light will engender life in a brush that will run up a tree, and then near the end of Citadel he thinks of the great green emerald forests that will flourish when the new sun comes. ?
In Short Sun, the first chapter talks about those hybrids in the vegetable world.? In the last non-epilogue chapter of RTTW, Silk is touched by the story of Hyacinth(us), whose blood falls and in dying as a man springs up as a flower.? THIS IS THE END OF THE MAIN STORY, it's important n several ways? One of these ways is the obvious one of Hyacinth and her death, recalling Silk to himself.? He was headed to the wrong home, now he can head to his home in the stars.? This lack of ability to distinguish the actual homeward odyssey can be applied to mankind in this case.? We've always known the white fountain would lead to the Green Man future.? The story of Hyacinthus is one in which even though the man dies his blood becomes a flower that lives? This s what has happened.?

2011-10-13 Antonin Scriabin replying to David Stockhoff #

Recommends Peace and The Fifth Head of Cerberus as Wolfe's finest stand-alone novels, on a par with the New Sun books, and suggests The Sorcerer's House as a briefer, less dense read. Replies to David Stockhoff's thread asking what to read, giving personal opinion of the books' quality and entertainment value rather than analysis of their text.
supports

I have enjoyed all of his books that I have read, but his stand-alone novels that most impressed me (like Matthew) were *Peace* and *The Fifth Head of Cerberus*. They are, in my opinion, at the level of the New Sun books; Wolfe at his finest. For a brief, mysterious, and thoroughly entertaining read, *The Sorcerer's House* is also excellent. It is a bit less dense and complicated than those other two I have mentioned.

2011-10-13 Antonin Scriabin replying to Daniel Petersen #

Reports that the poster has read only the first half of The Wizard Knight duology and enjoyed it, finding it slow at first but rewarding by the end of the book. Responds to Daniel Petersen's recommendation of Wolfe's short story collections and of The Wizard Knight against its detractors, defending the book's first volume.
supports

I have only read the first half of the Wizard / Knight duology, but I very much enjoyed it. It was one of those books that got me sufficiently interested (though not enthralled) during the first 200 pages or so, and then rewarded patience with the last half. By the end of the book, I was completely roped into the story. I have a few other books to read, then I will read *The Wizard*. I can understand why people might be turned off by Wizard / Knight, but I for one thought the first book at least was great.

Recommends Wolfe's works to a newcomer, praising the short story collections Innocents Aboard and Strange Travelers, The Fifth Head of Cerberus, Peace and Pirate Freedom, and gives a personal account of which book changed his view of Wolfe. Replies to Antonin Scriabin's comments on The Wizard Knight, saying the reader's patience will be rewarded and that the book is unusual as a fantasy and a defense of knightly mores.

Innocents Aboard is probably my favorite short story collection. Although Strange Travelers contains one of my favorite short stories: Counting Cats in Zanzibar.

Fifth Head of Cerberus was the one that changed my opinion of The Book of the New Sun and of Wolfe as a writer. After I read it, I started telling people he was the best SF writer period...ever.
I always recommend Peace to readers who are more into naturalistic fiction. It tricks them into following Wolfe.

Pirate Freedom made me think it would be fun to be a pirate. In an alternate universe, fantasy stories set at sea are as popular as stories about boy wizards are here. In that universe, Wolfe is a god.

Antonin:
When you get into The Wizard, you will see why some people --one famous female former-lister in particular-- could not finish the book. But as one of the most unusual fantasies, as a fully developed world, as a defense of Knightly mores, it's really something.

I'll chime in on recommending Wolfe's short story collections - I haven't run into one I didn't pretty thoroughly enjoy, but Doctor Death is probably the one that's as consistently rich as New Sun and feels like it prefigures it in many ways. I heartily agree with the recommendation of Cerberus and think that's probably the best next stop. And I must put in a good word for The Wizard-Knight duology against its detractors. It is in some respects rather frustrating or unnerving or annoying or some such thing (which of Wolfe's books aren't?!), but I think it's truly masterful and up there with his greatest stuff - after attempting New Sun and not being sure what I thought, it was Wizard-Knight (and then the collection Innocents Aboard) that got me thorougly mesmerised and hooked and led me in to the entirety of the Solar Cycle and everything else.

-DOJP
