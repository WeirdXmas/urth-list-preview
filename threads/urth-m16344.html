The thread: The thread began as a debate over whether Michael Moorcock's Elric books build real depth or merely borrow elements from Melniboné-as-Númenor to Kafka's torture machine, with Sergei Soloviev arguing borrowing and flattening and David Duffy countering that the Cornelius novels, not the rapidly written Elric stories, are the fairer measure. Craig Brewer defended Moorcock as deliberate pastiche and thought experiment, while Matthew Keeley disputed the Warlord of the Air books as didactic. Jerry Friedman mostly joked about the Cornelius volumes. The thread drifted from Wolfe and Moorcock into a steampunk recommendations exchange, with Brewer asking for works and Duffy suggesting The Difference Engine and Blaylock, ending without agreement on Moorcock.
  • Sergei SOLOVIEV: Moorcock's Elric books borrow rather than build depth; Wolfe uses borrowed material best
  • David Duffy: Judge Moorcock by the Cornelius novels, since Elric was written too fast for depth
  • Craig Brewer: Moorcock's Elric is deliberate pastiche and thought experiment; Cornelius shows his merit
  • Matthew Keeley: Warlord of the Air books are didactic with straw-man societies, though entertaining alternate history
  • Jerry Friedman: Jokingly advises finishing the Cornelius set despite strange passages
  • Craig Brewer: Asked for steampunk recommendations not written to follow a trend

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Stories within stories

2011-08-08 Sergei SOLOVIEV #

Argues that Moorcock's Elric books borrow their elements from other sources rather than building depth, citing Melniboné as Númenor, the labyrinth as Atlantis, and the master torturer as a blend of Kafka's penal-colony machine and Chinese torture. Responds to Jeff's earlier post by clarifying that his dislike targets the authors' writing style rather than the heroes, and extends the comparison to Donaldson and Howard.

Dear Jeff,

I didn' t write that the heroes (or anti-heroes) - Covenant and Elric - were repulsive to me. I wrote
that the books were (their authors writing style). I don't remember to be shocked by the "rape
scene" from Donaldson (I was not a teen), but I remember an intense dislike of how the
book was written.

I glanced at the "Elric". I have to admit that this time (after many years) my dislike
was much less, I was just bored.

I'll try to explain. There is no doubt that Moorcock is quite an erudite. But I don't like
how he uses his erudition. To me it is like a badly done theatric background - the pieces
of this and that squeezed into some rough picture.

It is told that Elrik is in some way antagonist to the heroes of Tolkien. To Conan, that
you mention, as well.

But Tolkien used his knowledge of history, folklore, languages to create depth, what is most impressive in his "Lord of the Rings" is more than 10000 years of history subtly influencing the present.

Howard was young and while his knowledge of history, geography etc is sometimes ridiculous, his books thrive with vital energy, that is completely absent in Moorcock's...

My impression was that Moorcock simply borrows the elements for his story.

- Melnibone looks (from the point of view of history
and background) as Numenor in the "Lord of the Rings".

- Its labirynth is borrowed from the stories about Atlantis.

- Master torturer in Elric story - hybrid of torture device from "In the penal colony" by Kafka:
execution device that carves the sentence of the condemned prisoner on his skin in a script before letting him die (Wiki)
and some stories about Chinese torture.

- The idea that the (future) villain is an Albino is also an n-th reuse of similar ideas (great literary villain - Uriah Heep by Dickens, there were many villains of that type afterwards - recent - Malfoys in Potter stories)

- It seems that he uses some provocative symbolism without any real need (his "pulsing cavern" reminds very much an anatomic description of human or animal colon), I think it is also borrowed from medieval symbolism (bowels of the devil), but used clumsily.

- Gold-plated ships - it is just not practical.

Of course my remarks are subjective, I think it is bad (artless) use of interesting sources - that his sources are more interesting than the result.

All the best

Sergei Soloviev

2011-08-09 Sergei SOLOVIEV replying to Gerry Quinn #

Agrees with Gerry Quinn that Wolfe borrowed the torture machine from Kafka, and argues Wolfe uses it to suggest deep time while Moorcock flattens borrowed material. Compares Gurloes with Banks's Nolieti and Moorcock's Dr. Jest, and cites Arioch's borrowing from Golding, ranking Wolfe's use best.
supports · source-and-allusion · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe deepens borrowed images while Moorcock flattens them

Exactly! And look how Wolfe uses it. In his book it is part of "historical lecture" by Gurloes, so,
it is a token of immense depth of time. It is woven into the subtle fabrics of references
to our own time, like the photograph of lunar landing (or maybe its painted copy) cleaned by
Rudesund, like the Pope's translation of Homer used in Chrasmologic writings and many
others. (Notice also that the machine breaks in the end of Kafka's story, and Gurloes says
it does not work.) The meanings and references play beautifully.

And if we take Moorcock, the use is quite the opposite. He takes a rich deep story and "flattens"
it to suit in some very straightforward way his purposes. So his torturer is just a sadist, and he
borrows the image, using only the idea of extreme cruelty.

It comes to my mind to compare three torturers: Gurloes (complex man who tries to look
simple brute) - by Wolfe; Nolieti in the Inversions by Banks who is a real brute but his
brutishness is described with great skill by Banks, so he has interesting human dimension
(he tries to use the teeth of his victims as his own prostheses) and the main hero Vosill
even tries to negotiate with him because she cannot believe that he may be truly so brutish;
and Dr. Jest of Moorcock borrowed from other contexts and transformed into one-dimensional
sadist. I think the Wolfe's is the best on literary scale and Moorcock's the worst (no talent,
no imagination and even no honest references to the sources).

Another example (I have forgotten to mention it) is that Arioch is borrowed from
"The Lord of the Flies" by Golding (he comes as a fly to Elric) and the whole tradition concerning Beelzebub, and again flattened very much (though at least he is not one-dimensional character).

I still had no time to analyse Donaldson.

Best

Sergei Soloviev

2011-08-09 Matthew Keeley replying to David Duffy #

Agrees with David Duffy's ranking of Moorcock's more complex works but disputes including the Warlord of the Air books among them, arguing they are didactic novels with unconvincing straw-men societies. disputes · genre-and-craft

I mostly agree with this statement, though I'm not sure if I'd include the Warlord of the Air/Nomad of the Time Streams books among Moorcock's "more complex." Moorcock has stated he wrote them as didactic novels; his protagonist moves through a variety of alternate worlds and encounters a number of straw men societies, none of which really convince.

On the other hand, the Warlord books are great if you're ever in the mood for a steampunk alternate history featuring mecha-Stalin vs. the a-bomb...

2011-08-09 Craig Brewer replying to Sergei SOLOVIEV #

Argues that Moorcock's Elric stories are deliberate pastiche and thought experiments rather than plagiarism or conventional epic fantasy, and that his literary merit is better judged by the Cornelius books. Supports this by citing Moorcock's own interview remarks and the premise of Elric as a weak, cultured Conan.
supports

And Moorcock's also said on many occasions that the Elric stories were exercises in a kind of pastiche of reversals. Recognizing elements that are lifted from other works and then used in certain generically deliberate and/or artificial ways isn't so much recognizing plaigiarisim as intention.

The fact that they've become his most famous books is something of a disappointment to him in some of his interviews, too (despite the fact that he keeps writing them...we all gotta eat, I guess). And they do read better when you think of them as quickly written thought experiments rather than "epic fantasy." In the end, he's kind of, "Hey, what if Conan was smart, over-cultured, and weak but had the same adventures?" At a certain point, the premise plays itself out, but I still enjoy the premise for what it was.

And I'm with the others that his literary merit is MUCH better judged according to the Cornelius books. The End of Time series is my personal favorite.

2011-08-10 David Duffy replying to Sergei SOLOVIEV #

Argues that Michael Moorcock's more complex works, such as the Cornelius novels, are a fairer basis for judging his depth than the Elric stories. Responds to Sergei Soloviev's claim that Moorcock flattens rich stories, and contends the Elric books were written too fast to show depth. Offers the Cornelius novels as the better place to start.
disputes · author-and-biography

Moorcock's more complex works are the Cornelius novels, _Byzantium Endures_ series, _Gloriana_, Dancers At the End of Time_, _Behold the Man_, _Warlord of the Air_ and its sequels. The Elric stuff was written at great speed, and it shows (he would write a short novel in three days ;)).

So, I don't think it's quite a fair comparison. The allusiveness and strangeness of the Cornelius novels is a better place to start, if you are looking for depth.

Cheers, David Duffy.
