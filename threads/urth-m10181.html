Fiction, halves, twins

Urth list · 6 messages · 2010-04-27 to 2010-05-03

2010urthThe Book of the New SunGwern BranwenDavid StockhoffMo Holkar / UKGDave Tallman
The thread: Participants asked whether the chapter pairs of Fiction, Halves, Twins map onto Tarot Major Arcana cards and whether Wolfe reads the Tarot high or low. David Stockhoff proposed the pairing scheme and its lycanthropy reading, while Gwern Branwen supplied Wolfe's poem on the Greater Trumps as evidence of Wolfe's Tarot knowledge. Mo Holkar supported the Tarot reading with the sorcery-weapons remark and later proposed the Hierophant as Urban Trelawny, an argument resting on card meanings and the name match rather than the text. Dave Tallman extended the scheme into a character-to-trump matching, and the thread ended with the Hierophant identification unsupported by any passage.
  • David Stockhoff: chapter pairs match Major Arcana cards, with the Star pointing to lycanthropy
  • Gwern Branwen: Wolfe's poem on the Greater Trumps shows he knew the Tarot, so Stockhoff's links need checking
  • Mo Holkar: Emlyn's sorcery weapons suggest the Tarot suits were intended; proposes the Hierophant as Urban Trelawny
  • Dave Tallman: each Trump should match a prominent character or object, testing alignment with chapters

Themes: Stories within stories, Myth and folklore sources, Names and their meanings

2010-04-27 David Stockhoff #

Proposes that the novel's chapter pairs correspond to Tarot Major Arcana cards, matching each pair by shared features such as Shell's advice letters in chapters 13 and 32 and Bax's death challenge in 37. Builds on Tallman's chapter descriptions and asks whether Wolfe reads the Tarot high or low, suggesting classical goddesses like Isis and Venus as likely referents.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Chapter pairs map onto Tarot Major Arcana cards; Wolfe's Tarot follows classical goddess attributions

A few guesses at connections to the Arcana, based entirely on Dave Tallman's chapter descriptions:

15: "Women" and 30: "Freedom!"
15 is to Shell. 30 is from Millie.
Common features: in 15 Bax mentions his two lovers. In 30 Millie hints that she doesn't love George and she wants Bax.

Temperance: Moderation or a compromise is required

13: "Mouthpiece" and 32: "A Request"
13 is from Shell. 32 is from Shell.
Common feature: both are from Shell, offering advice.

Hanged Man: Inaction

8: "The Good Boy" and 37: "The Challenge"
8 is to George. 37 is from George.
Common feature: fights between brothers. In 8 is the introduction of the conflict between Emlyn and Ieuan. In 37 is a death challenge from George to Bax.

Chariot: Conquest, pride

3: "From One Big House to Another" and 42: "A Terrible Mistake" 3 is to Shell. 4 is from Doris.
Common feature: the house. 3 mentions the house to Shell for the first time. In 42 Doris visits the house for the last time.

Priestess: Otherworldliness, assuming the house is not really of this earth

2: "Your Brother" and 43: "Final Report"
2 is to George. 43 is from Madame Orizia
Common feature: opening and closing business arrangements. In 2 Bax writes to George for the first time and asks for money. In 43 Madame Orizia writes to Bax for the last time and asks for money.

Magician: a swindler?

1: "Your Old Cellmate" and 44: "Home!"
Common feature: rags to riches. In 1 Bax has nothing and is trying to get money from George. In 44 Bax gets everything of George's including his identity and his wife.

The Fool: is always a poor young man, an empty vessel to be filled with knowledge or riches

This may not be a handy way to understand the story. Two obvious problems arise: (1) what Tarot interpretation is used? (2) is it applied in a "high" or "low" style? For deep meaning or jokey convenience? You can't tell with Wolfe.

We also don't know whether a card is to be read straight or reversed. In short, you'll need to know both the Tarot and the story well to get much out of it.

However, we know Wolfe knows everything there is to know about classical gods and goddesses. I'd assume he would follow, for example, the High Priestess as Isis and the Empress as Venus/Demeter rather than some new-agey interpretation.

The middle symmetry suggests arranging the chapters in pairs and looking for common features. There are books where this works, for example "The Scarlet Letter" and "Fearful Symmetry" (one of the comics in the "Watchmen" series).

22: "Silver Bullets" and 23: "Silver Bullets"
22 to Millie. 23 to George.
Common feature: title, pistols with silver bullets.

21: "Who Was He?" and 24: "Never Trust"
21 to Shell. 24 from Shell.
Common feature: 24 answers the questions in 21 about girls and Skotos.

20: "A Very Strange House" and 25: "The Hitchhiker"
20 to George. 25 to Orizia
Common feature: In 20 Madame Orizia makes a psychic visit, in 25 this is referred to by Bax.

19: "The Professional" and 26: "Bax Investigates"
19 from Orizia. 26 to Millie.
Common feature: in 19 Madame Orizia offers her services, in 26 Bax tells Millie about the letter.

18: "Lupine" and 27: "Remember the Pistols?"
18 to Millie. 27 to Shell.
Common feature: In 18 is the first mention of Lupine by name. In 27 there is mention of shooting an animal (the werewolf).

17: "A Tramp" and 28: "Cherchez la Femme"
17 to Millie. 28 to George.
Common features: 17 has the first appearance of Toby, the dog. In 28 Bax gets a call from Toby in human form.

16: "Be Careful" and 29: "Help is on the Way"
16 from Millie. 29 from Madame Orizia.
Common features: 16 mentions Madame Orizia for the first time. 29 has her arriving to help.

15: "Women" and 30: "Freedom!"
15 is to Shell. 30 is from Millie.
Common features: in 15 Bax mentions his two lovers. In 30 Millie hints that she doesn't love George and she wants Bax.

14: "Is it a Ghost?" and 31: "Get Out -- And Got Out"
14 is to George. 31 is to Millie.
Common features: 14 mentions Nicholas the Butler and the locked trunk. In 31, the trunk in unlocked and Nicholas emerges.

13: "Mouthpiece" and 32: "A Request"
13 is from Shell. 32 is from Shell.
Common feature: both are from Shell, offering advice.

12: "A Big Deal in Prospect" and 33: "On the Home Front" 12 is to George. 33 is from Millie.
Common feature: 12 has a date with Doris and an invitation to her home. 33 shows Millie is jealous of Doris and the other women.

11: "On Various Women" and 34: "Fox and Wolf"
11 is to George. 34 is to Millie.
Common features: 11 has the first appearance of Winkle in human form. 34 mentions werefoxes and werewolves. The title may also refer to Bax and George respectively.

10: "Tough Question" and 35: "The Duelist"
10 is to Shell. 35 is to Shell.
Common features: In both Bax asks Shell questions and speaks of legal matters.

9: "Sorcery" and 36: "Wherever You Are"
9 is to George. 36 is to George.
Common feature: both have to do with finding things. In 9 the triannulus finds fish, etc. In 36 Bax is looking for George.

8: "The Good Boy" and 37: "The Challenge"
8 is to George. 37 is from George.
Common feature: fights between brothers. In 8 is the introduction of the conflict between Emlyn and Ieuan. In 37 is a death challenge from George to Bax.

7: "Bax Takes a Beating" and 38: "Manjushri"
7 is to George. 38 is to Millie.
Common feature: visions brought about by Winkle. In 7 there is the vision of the dance on the lawn. In 38 is the visit to legendary Japan for advice.

6: "Fresh Air" and 39: "Be Cool"
6 is from Shell. 39 is from Shell.
Common feature: both from Shell with friendly confidences.

5: "So Many Shades of Black" and 40: "Dear John"
Common Feature: dating Doris. 5 has a first letter from Doris making a date with Bax. 40 is Doris breaking up with Bax.

4: "Fisherman's Luck" and 41: "The Riverman"
4 is to George. 41 is to George.
Common feature: Bax using sorcery. In 4 Bax uses sorcery for the first time to get fish and the ring. In 41 he uses it to summon the Riverman and win the battle.

3: "From One Big House to Another" and 42: "A Terrible Mistake" 3 is to Shell. 4 is from Doris.
Common feature: the house. 3 mentions the house to Shell for the first time. In 42 Doris visits the house for the last time.

2: "Your Brother" and 43: "Final Report"
2 is to George. 43 is from Madame Orizia
Common feature: opening and closing business arrangements. In 2 Bax writes to George for the first time and asks for money. In 43 Madame Orizia writes to Bax for the last time and asks for money.

1: "Your Old Cellmate" and 44: "Home!"
Common feature: rags to riches. In 1 Bax has nothing and is trying to get money from George. In 44 Bax gets everything of George's including his identity and his wife.

If the arcana theory is right, these chapter pairs may also be associated with the tarot deck. I don't have time to look at that now, but someone else may give it a try.

2010-04-28 Mo Holkar / UKG replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that Emlyn's remark about sorcery weapons being swords, staffs, wands and cups hints that Wolfe had the Tarot suits in mind. Answers Gwern Branwen's doubt about Tarot connections by citing page 74 and noting that coins also feature in that chapter.
supports · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Tarot suits echoed in sorcery weapons

Another pointer that Wolfe may have had the Tarot in mind when writing this particular book is found on p74 where Emlyn talks about weapons of sorcery:

"Swords, mostly.... A lot are staffs and wands these days. I've heard of cups, too."
(And coins feature in that chapter in their own right.)

2010-04-28 David Stockhoff replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that a Tarot correspondence scheme, drawn from Wolfe's TCITGT, confirms the central point of each chapter pair in the Soldier novels, with the Star card pointing to lycanthropy. Supports the lycanthropy reading by citing Wolfe's Star as Magna Mater of the Old Cult, and notes Gwern Branwen's message about Wolfe's poem on the Greater Trumps.
supports · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Tarot Star card signals lycanthropy in Soldier books; Temperance corresponds to Time in Tarot scheme; Tarot correspondences reveal each chapter's main point

Great!

One thing that a correspondence scheme can do here is confirm the primary "meaning" or point of each chapter---to tell us what to look out for, what is important. Since the Tarot is a system for organizing and transmitting esoteric knowledge, I never doubted that Wolfe was very familiar with it indeed. And the version he presents in TCITGT is helpful.

For example: I wondered about how to connect the Star and werewolves.

But Wolfe says Star = "Magna Mater of the Old Cult." As readers of the Soldier novels know, this means lycanthropy. So Dave's elucidation of lycanthropy as the major plot point or introduction of that chapter pair is confirmed.

I didn't know Temperance could be Time. Watch out for that ...

Proposes matching each Tarot trump to a prominent character or object in Fiction, Halves, Twins, running from the Fool (Bax) through the World, and tests whether the matches align with the chapters where those figures appear. Builds on David Stockhoff's suggestion that a Tarot correspondence scheme could reveal each chapter's main point, using the Star-to-Lupine pairing as an example.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Fiction Halves Twins chapters follow the Tarot trump sequence

Another approach is to associate Tarot symbols with prominent characters and objects and see how far we can go, and how well the matches align with chapters containing them.

0 Fool -- Bax in the beginning
1 Magician -- Madame Orizia
2 High Priestess -- Doris
3 Empress -- Martha Murrey
