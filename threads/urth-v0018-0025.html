Weer's Death, and J.L. Borges

Urth list · 4 messages · 1998-08-25 to 1998-08-28

1998urthThe Book of the New SunC R CulverWilliam H. AnsleyMichael Straight
The thread: Christopher Culver opened by arguing that Peace draws on Borges, especially Weer's labyrinthine house, which he read as existing only in Weer's postmortem imagination, and Mr. Gold's invented books as Borgesian. William Ansley disputed this, holding that Weer's house was real and contained museum rooms replicating important rooms of his life, though not the shifting rooms the book describes. Michael Straight offered a different reading, that the house is an imaginary memory palace, the classical mnemonic device, and reasoned from the house's unstable behavior. Ansley returned to argue that Weer built a real house before he died and that the ghost's house is an imaginary structure grown from it, saying the positions were closer than they seemed. The thread ended with the readings converging rather than resolving into a single answer.
  • CRCulver: Peace is heavily Borgesian; Weer's house may exist only in his postmortem imagination
  • William H. Ansley: Weer's house was real, with museum rooms, but the shifting rooms are not literal
  • Michael Straight: the house is an imaginary memory palace, the classical mnemonic device
  • William H. Ansley: a real house existed; the ghost's house is an imagined version grown from it

Themes: Ghosts, dreams and visions, Memory and forgetting, Books, libraries and writing

1998-08-25 C R Culver #

Argues that Peace draws heavily on Borges, citing the labyrinthine house of Weer as a Borgian element that may exist only in Weer's postmortem imagination, and Mr. Gold's invented books as echoing Borges' fabricated authors. Responds to William Ansley's remark about rereading and his comment that Peace's ideas recur across Wolfe's work, pointing to 'Tlon, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius' as the closest parallel.
proposes · source-and-allusion · genre-and-craft · theories: Weer's house exists only in his postmortem imagination; Peace reworks Borges's fabricated-authors device

<I read the book three times before I realized that Weer was dead.>

That's common with Wolfe's books. I read the BotNS three times before I came to grasp basic plot structure. I'm sure most of the people on the list can testify to similar occurences. <g>

And he continued:
<Another thing that adds to my interest in _Peace_, after reading much of Wolfe's other work, is how many germs of ideas that appear in many of his novels and short stories can be found in _Peace_ (or so it seems to me).>

Peace includes a great deal of Jorge Luis Borges' influence, and seeing it here can illuminate how it was later used in the Book of the New Sun. One Borgian influence I think is particularly strong is the labyrithine symbolism of Weer's house (which I'm assuming is part of his postmortem imagination and doesn't actually exist).
Mr. Gold's writing of books that don't exist reminds me of Borges' tendency to make up books and authors to validate the claims of some of his characters. The story that comes immediately to mind is "Tlon, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius."

1998-08-27 William H. Ansley #

Argues that Weer's house in Peace existed in reality and held museum rooms replicating important rooms of Weer's life, though not the shifting rooms Weer describes. Responds to Christopher R. Culver's assumption that the house is a postmortem imagining, rejecting the book's account of rooms moving and vanishing while accepting the museum-room idea.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Weer's house exists with museum rooms replicating his life

I prefer to believe that the house does/did exist and it did have "museum rooms" replicating important rooms in Weer's life. But, I think the real house certainly wasn't the shifting thing Weer describes in the book, with rooms moving about and appearing and disappearing. Building a house with museum rooms would be a peculiar thing to do, but Weer was a peculiar guy.

William Ansley

1998-08-27 Michael Straight #

Argues that the labyrinthine house in Peace is an imaginary memory palace, the classical mnemonic device, rather than simply a literal Borgesian construct. Responds to Christopher Culver's claim that Weer's house is a postmortem fantasy by granting that Weer might have built one, and reasons from the shifting, unstable nature of the house in the story.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Weer's house is an imaginary memory palace

I don't know. It seems too obviously a reference to the ancient method of enhancing memory by creating an imaginary house or castle in which each room represents a time period or category of knowledge and the objects in each room represent the specific things you are trying to remember.

I guess that doesn't rule out Weer literally building such a house (in fact, the more I think about it, the more I like the idea--but I'd like it more in a story about some character consciously doing it as an extravagant mnemonic device--but then, what is a museum but an extravagant mnemonic device?), but I think the shiftiness implies that at a minimum, the house in the story has imaginary rooms/qualities in addition.

-Rostrum

1998-08-28 William H. Ansley #

Argues that Weer built a real house with museum rooms before he died, and that the ghost's house in Peace is an imaginary structure grown from that original, not wholly invented. Responds to a previous poster's memory-palace reading by saying the two positions are closer than they seem, since the Peace house need not be purely imaginary.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Weer built a real house with museum rooms before death; Ghost's house in Peace is imaginary but derived from a real house

I really don't think we disagree very much here. I think there was a "real" house Weer built before he died that had museum rooms in it. I think the house his ghost inhabits is imaginary and has all sorts of odd properties that no real building could have but had as its starting point in Weer's (ghost's) mind the original house the living Weer built.

I agree that the house described in _Peace_ could be wholly imaginary, I just don't think it *necessarily* is.

(Of course, I mean imaginary within the world of _Peace_ when I use the word above.)

William Ansley
