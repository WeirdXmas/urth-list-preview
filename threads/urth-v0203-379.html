Marble's prophecy

Urth list · 3 messages · 2002-03-18 to 2002-03-21

2002urthJerry FriedmanDan MelizaMarc Aramini
The thread: The thread asked whether Marble's prophecy of the three-horned beast can be trusted despite the narrator's unreliability. maa32 argued it is not discredited, citing Babbie's treatment of Hoof and the narrator apparently being Babbie at the end of On Blue's Waters, and floated polyploid frog and hybrid species images. Jerry Friedman and Dan Meliza rejected the biological basis for those theories, Friedman citing Xenopus laevis limb counts and the lecture slides, and Meliza arguing that development is too complex for a simple chromosome-to-limb link. The exchange ended with the biological speculation largely dismissed and no resolution about the prophecy itself.
  • maa32: Marble's prophecy is not discredited by the narrator's unreliability; Babbie's treatment of Hoof and the ending suggest the narrator is Babbie
  • Jerry Friedman: polyploid animal biology does not support the prophecy theories; the frog and fly images show normal limb counts
  • Dan Meliza: development is too complex for a simple chromosome-to-limb link; Xenopus laevis and genus variation undercut the argument

Themes: Unreliable narration, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Beasts and the animal in us

2002-03-18 Marc Aramini #

Argues that Marble's prophecy of the three-horned beast is not discredited by the narrator's unreliability, citing Babbie's odd treatment of Hoof at the astral scene and the narrator seeming to be Babbie at the end of On Blue's Waters. Responds to someone who dismissed the prophecy by pointing to the narrator's unreliability, and also digresses into a frog polyploidy image and other hybrid species.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Marble's prophecy is not discredited by the narrator; Babbie treats Hoof specially for a reason; Narrator seems to be Babbie at end of On Blue's Waters

Has a prophecy ever failed to have two meanings in Wolfe? Someone pointed out that the beast with three horns is instantly discredited by the narrator. I have two words for you:

UNRELIABLE NARRATOR

The dude doesn't even know who he is for sure, nor the name of his kids with any consistency. Just because he thinks the prophecy has already been fulfilled means nothing - you still have to account for the special treatment that Hoof receives from Babbie at the astral scene in Chapter 17/19 or Return to the whorl. First Babbie is mad at everyone, then when he hears the name Hoof he gets all happy and hugs him and picks him up and comforts him after he gets wounded. Hoof is another UNRELIABLE NARRATOR because he doesn't question why Babbie would treat him that way. And the narrator does seem to be Babbie for a second at the end of On Blue's Waters. You should try to account for this rather than stating that the narrator's discredit to the prophecy is enough.

I have determined that Wolfe never writes with Occam in mind - let's face it, he likes to complicate things. ... and isn't that picture of the frog from my previous post pretty cool? Now that I look at it again, I see the one one the left is 2N, the one on the right is 4N. Note that the front limbs of the frog also have three little digits that should be one (like three psuedo-hands or something) If you click on the arrows at the bottom of that page of the presentation, you can see the whole 31 slides. There are other hybrid species produced through polyploidy, including lizards and flies. The flies also have a doubling of the hind legs that is pretty interesting if you advance a few slides.

Marc Aramini

2002-03-19 Jerry Friedman #

Argues that the Marble's prophecy theories are not supported by the biology of polyploid animals, and corrects a reader's reading of the frog and fly images. Responds to maa32's claim about Occam's razor by saying that the proposed two-planet theory has fewer entities but more explanations, then cites Xenopus laevis anatomy and the fly lecture's slides as evidence that the animals have normal limb counts.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Marble's prophecy involves a two-planet theory with a large female submarine creature

That's a reasonable point (though I think the answer is probably "yes").

...

I think you misunderstood.

Actually, you should be in favor of the literal Occam's razor--that entities shouldn't be multiplied unnecessarily--because in your theory there are fewer entities (two planets instead of four, one large female submarine creature instead of two, etc.). However, I think that your theory involves more explanations than the other.

Thanks for the information about polyploid animals! I learned something already today.

However, on the subject of multiple limbs, I think you're misinterpreting. The tetraploid _Xenopus laevis_ has a big hind foot with a translucent web connecting four toes--not four separate legs. You can see the four toes at
<http://www.ahsc.arizona.edu/uac/iacuc/xenopus/xenopus.shtml>. Note that the web can look transparent or opaque depending on the lighting. As for the front legs, they have four digits in the picture of the diploid _X. tropicalis_ at <http://www.xlaevis.com/>, which also shows that the anatomy of _X. laevis_ and _X. tropicalis_ is much the same. Finally,
<http://www.geocities.com/RainForest/Vines/4966/info.html> has a lot of information about _X. laevis_ from a pet owner's point of view, but nothing about doubled limbs.

The flies in that lecture weren't hybrids or polyploid; they were an example of speciation in progress caused by recent ecological change (the introduction of apples in North America). They didn't have extra limbs, either. I think you must have been interpreting marks on the wings as legs. The left hind leg of the fly in the picture (there's only one picture, repeated in several slides) is visible between the wing and the abdomen. It's just as thin as the other legs, not thick like the wing markings. The right hind leg is hidden or missing.

So all the polyploid animals I've seen so far have normal anatomies, with the normal number of limbs.

Jerry Friedman

2002-03-21 Dan Meliza #

Disputes Marc's suggestion that doubling chromosome number would double limb count, noting that Xenopus laevis is naturally polyploid and that chromosome counts vary widely across the genus. Argues that development is too complex for such a simple chromosome-to-limb link, drawing on biology rather than the text of the book.
disputes · theories: Doubling chromosomes would double limb count

Hi Marc,

I haven't had a chance to look at the pics (qualifying exams, alas), but Xenopus laevis is naturally polyploid (making it difficult to make transgenic animals, for anyone who cares), and N varies greatly between species in the genus (something like 12N for one of those buggers). I think it might be a little spurious to claim that simply doubling the number of chromosomes would double limb count. Development is a lot more complex than that.

best,
dan
