Drotte-Roche mixup (was: Introduction and Breath)

Urth list · 3 messages · 2011-04-16 to 2011-04-17

2011urthThe Book of the New SunAntónio Pedro MarquesJeff WilsonTony Ellis
The thread: The thread asked whether the Drotte/Roche name swap in Severian's opening narrative is a printing error or a sign of faulty memory. Tony Ellis argued it is a writer's or editor's slip, citing the pike sighting that is attributed to Roche and then, two paragraphs later, to Drotte. Jeff Wilson countered that Severian keeps working his office duties while writing, so unstated gaps could let a memory lapse explain the change. António Marques judged an accident unlikely given the mixup's prominence and Wolfe's care in proofreading, and suggested it may be a deliberate device that misleads the reader. The thread ends without agreement, with three explanations left open.
  • Jeff Wilson: the swap may be a memory lapse, since Severian writes between office duties and unstated gaps can separate the names
  • Tony Ellis: a writer's or editor's slip, not memory; the pike sighting contradiction is too close for a memory lapse
  • António Marques: an accident is unlikely given prominence and proofreading care; possibly a deliberate narrative device deceiving the reader

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting

2011-04-16 Tony Ellis #

Argues that the Drotte/Roche swap in Severian's account is a writer's or editor's slip rather than a sign of faulty memory, since name substitution is a common error. Grounds this on the passage where Severian attributes the sighting of pikes to Roche and then, two paragraphs later, to Drotte, noting that a memory lapse would not produce such a close contradiction.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Drotte/Roche swap is authorial error not memory

and Ant?nio Pedro Marques:

A few reasons why I feel this way:
Firstly, because substituting one name for another is an easy mistake for a writer to make, and a hard one for an editor to spot. Mistakes get published all the time.

Secondly, because substituting one word for an associated one is a common error not of memory, but of language. We all do it. You mentally reach for a word, and because your attention is elsewhere, you don't notice that you've taken the one that was lying next to it instead. If a journalist writes that 'the sherrif opened the new sports field' when he meant to write 'the mayor opened the new sports field', it isn't because he remembers a man with a pointy badge and a ten-gallon hat cutting the ribbon.

If Severian consistently recalled that Roche was the one who said he saw the pikes, and then three books later, in a chance remark, Drotte remembered saying it, that would be a good way to show that Severian's memory was suspect. But that doesn't happen. Severian says Roche said it, then almost immediately he says Drotte said it. If that's an error of memory, then Severian has not only remembered an incident in two completely different ways in the space of a couple of paragraphs, but he's forgotten what he *wrote* just two paragraphs ago. That's not imperfect memory. That's serious brain damage.

2011-04-17 António Pedro Marques replying to Tony Ellis #

Argues that the Drotte/Roche name mixup in the opening of The Book of the New Sun is unlikely to be an accidental slip, given its prominence and Wolfe's care in proofreading. Responds to Tony Ellis by conceding memory errors are possible but contending that, even if deliberate, the mixup may be a narrative device that deceives the reader.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Drotte-Roche mixup is deliberate narrative deception

This is the part I find unbelievable. A mistake such as this doesn't happen on such a passage from such an author. First, it'd be much of a coincidence; second, it's the kind of stuff I'm positive he cares more about in his proof-readings; third, it's too conspicuous, I got it outright, dense as I am, and absolutely not on the lookout for such stuff at the time; fourth, it's right in the beginning of the book(s), with all the spotlights on it.

(And from the moment I don't find this believable, there has to be a motive for the mixup, whatever it is.)

That's a good point. But for one it doesn't completely hold. Such memory errors - in 'such' I include the proximity of paragraphs - *are* possible. But more importantly, it may well be a simple language error, yet it makes the point: even if his memory *is* perfect, even if he's not out to fool us, there are still ways in which the narrative may deceive us.

If it's a GW error, well, it looks just like the best of what he usually does.
