2011-05-23 James Wynn replying to Andrew Mason #

Argues that the Red Flower in the Long Sun story is borrowed from Kipling's Jungle Books, where monkeys want Mowgli to teach them man's red flower, and that the flower used by Juno to conceive Mars is unidentified. Supports this with Robert Graves's proposal that it was the whitethorn, a hawthorn linked to miraculous births, and extends Andrew Mason's Mars reading.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Red Flower derives from Jungle Books; Romulus and Mowgli are the same person

The flower used by Juno to conceive Mars is unidentified. Robert Graves posited that it was the "whitethorn" -- a type of European hawthorn with medicinal uses that Graves asserted was associated with miraculous births. http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crataegus_monogyna

The Red Flower is a direct lifting from one of the most famous stories from The Jungle Books -- one referenced in the Disney version: The story where the monkeys want Mowgli to teach them to make "man's red flower" (then Kaa shows up and eats them all). Of course, the compilers of the Story of Frog might well have seen it as a confirmation that Romulus and Mowgli were "actually" the same person. Certainly, a clue like that would have attracted Robert Graves.

J

2011-05-23 Lee Berman replying to Andrew Mason #

Argues that the gods and demons of Urth have a watery, fishy origin, linking Abaia, Fomalhaut, Jurupari, undines and kelpies, and that this ties to the name Fish paired with Frog. Grounds the claim in the giant fish from Scylla's lake, the fishy bond between The Mother and Seawrack, and Severian seeing a school of ship-sized fish after the flood, which he reads as the pelagic argosy of undines.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Urth's gods and demons have a watery fishy origin; Severian's fish are the undines in the argosy; Juturna was chosen to seduce Severian

Ah well, at the risk of being repetitive, I think there is a volume of evidence which points to a watery, fishy origin for the gods/demons of Urth, references including Abaia, Fomalhaut, Jurupari, undines, kelpies, etc. I don't know if that idea came first or the idea of using "Fish" as a pairing for Frog, but I think they are related. Wolfe provides some bolstering for this connection in that giant fish from Scylla's lake and the very fishy relationship between The Mother and Seawrack.

I was just reading the section where Severian is swimming around in the ocean after the flood and he sees shadows of a school of fish as big as ships. Those are some awfully big fishes. I am thinking he is really seeing the "pelagic argosy" (open ocean flotilla). Meaning they are the undines. Not yet ship-sized, Juturna is probably the smallest of them (not counting Idas) and thus she was picked to seduce Severian.

2011-05-23 James Wynn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Frog from The Book of the New Sun is most likely Ymar, a royal orphan with a twin, and builds the case on name meanings, star constellations, and a chain of links to Erebus and Tzadkiel. Grounds the case in the Frog story, Ymar's conversation with Severian, Master Ash's remark on Erebus, and the Ogre in the Student and His Son story, while answering Lee Berman's question about the connection.
proposes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Frog is Ymar; Ymar is royal stock with a twin; Erebus is the Ogre; Erebus is dead

Well, it means that Ymar is of some sort of royal stock. That like Severian, he has a twin (probably fraternal). Since he is being raised by the Torturers it is clear that he has been "orphaned" which does not necessarily mean his parents are dead. But there is nothing I've detected in his conversation with Severian or in the story of Frog to say how he got there. If you're looking for the true identity of Frog, Ymar is an obvious choice. I'm open to others, but you HAVE to like the way Ymar means "king" and so does the genus for frogs.

Don't think that connection has evaded my notice. And the constellation Dorado (swordfish) is nearby the star Rana (frog) in the Eridanus (stream) constellation. Since Wolfe reuses ideas, a swordfish strikes me as a useful place to start. There are of course other possibilities. But this is the way profiling works. Gerry thinks I just make stuff up on the fly. I wish it were that easy. In order to solve a puzzle, you must first allow that there *is* a puzzle.

Tzakiel as the shepherds? Isn't that exactly their role regarding humanity? (for whatever purpose)

There's his name for starters, which means the Darkness of the Underworld, that's awfully chthonic.
Then there's the story of the Student and His Son. Wolfe has said in The Castle of the Otter that Nix is the consort of Erebus "which makes him the step-father of Princess Noctua" [from memory]. Well, the consort of Nix in the story is the Ogre, and (using the commutative property) Erebus is the Ogre. Of course the Ogre dies in that story. Finally, there's the fact the Ascians are called "slaves of Erebus", but his base is supposedly in the South. It seems to me that he is a bit inactive. There's no assault on the Commonwealth from Antarctica.

The pirates of the south still honor Erebus. Still, when someone speaks of pirate "ship of Erebus", they might only be talking about the region where he once ruled.

One thing that speaks against this view is Master Ash who says "If Erebus and Abaia and the rest
enter the field themselves, it will be a new struggle. If and when." I would guess they would "enter the field" in the same way the Storm King entered the field against the US Navy. But just because Erebus has died, does not mean he cannot be a power anymore. The great powers in Wolfe's novel are not debilitated by death. Severian, Mr Million, Weer, Silk, Typhon. So, Erebus' current state is ambiguous but, yeah, I think he's dead.

J.

2011-05-23 Andrew Mason #

Explains why the character Fish is named as Frog's twin, suggesting the name was chosen to suit 'Frog', and says the Red Flower story is a historic legend about Mars's birth. Responds to Gerry Quinn's list of Jungle Book and Romulus and Remus parallels, adding that Spring Wind fits Mars better than Hercules.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Fish was named to suit the name Frog; Red Flower legend is the birth of Mars; Spring Wind fits Mars more than Hercules

Elements from the Jungle Book include:

The name 'Frog' (that's what 'Mowgli' means)
The tiger (smilodon) as the threat to the child.
The figures of the Black Killer (Bagheera, the Panther) and the Naked One (Baloo, the Bear - the resemblance is less exact in his case, but the role he plays is very similar)
The Red Flower as a name for fire
A woodcutter as Frog's father before his adoption by the wolves (in the original his biological father: in this version, since it has to be fused with the Romulus/Remus legend, his first adoptive father).

All the Romulus and Remus references you mentioned are clearly there as well. It's an amalgam of two stories about [a] child[ren] brought up by wolves. It's entirely plausible that such a story would develop - much less weird than the combination of the Monitor and the Minotaur. There is also a bit derived from American history (Squanto), a glancing reference to Moses, and quite likely other things as well.

So, why Fish? Well, if they are trying to combine one version of the story in which the hero has a twin, and another in which he is called Frog, they need to find a name for his twin which fits 'Frog'. 'Fish' looks suitable.. That may be all there is to it, though for all I know it is also a reference to another legend we haven't spotted yet. That, at least, is all we need to say to explain how the story developed; it's very probably meant to have resonances with other references to fish in various parts of the cycle.
.

If I remember rightly, the Red Flower story is actually a historic legend about the birth of Mars. (Virtually everything that is said about Spring Wind fits Mars. Once he has dropped out of the story things become more complex.)
