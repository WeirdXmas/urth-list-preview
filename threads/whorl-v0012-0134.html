The thread: Readers asked Ian Smith's question about the final Remora scene in The Return to the Whorl, especially the Hyacinth passage, and debated who narrates it. Ian Smith argued that the 'It' is the azoth and that Horn's and Silk's memories are mixing, and Mark Millman agreed to a point while correcting a reference to Nettle and Hyacinth. Adam Stephanides argued the figure in Silk's body is Silk carrying Horn's memories, since Silk could only accept Hyacinth's death through that passage, and David DiGiacomo pushed further, claiming Horn's spirit is transferred as the Neighbor's words suggest. Spectacled Bear disputed the whole reading, holding that the narrator is Horn rather than Silk. The thread ends in unresolved disagreement over whether memory or spirit transfers, with no consensus reached.
  • Ian Smith: the 'It' is the azoth; Horn's and Silk's memories are mixing
  • Mark Millman: accepts the memory mixing and corrects the Nettle/Hyacinth reference
  • Adam Stephanides: the figure is Silk carrying Horn's memories
  • David DiGiacomo: Horn's spirit, not just memories, is transferred into Silk's body
  • Spectacled Bear: the narrator is Horn, not Silk, and Silk uses the Mother's communication method

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2001-02-06 Ian Smith #

Asks for an explanation of the final Remora scene in The Return to the Whorl, especially the Hyacinth passage, and then collects others' readings. Argues that the 'It' is the azoth and that Horn's and Silk's memories are mixing, so Nettle may have sailed on to Pajorocou; also suggests Severian takes Horn/Silk's spirit for Malrubius' ghost.
questions · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Horn and Silk's memories mix in Return to the Whorl; Nettle sailed back to Pajorocou with the others; Severian thinks Horn/Silk is Malrubius' ghost

Just finished RTTWand heartily confused at the ending. Will someone who is more able please alleviate my stupidity by explaining exactly what happened in that last scene with Remora, esp. what the 'Hyacinth' passage from the writings indicated?

Having read the posts, I also have a couple more pennies-worth of my own to stick in:

From Nacre's post:

"I would never have used it while

For the most part, I agree with David DiGiacomo's
answers. For the question above, though, I'd suggest
that it's another example of Horn's and Silk's mem-
ories mixing. "It" is the azoth, if I recall correctly (I don't have the book here to check); he says "Nettle"
but means, I think, Hyacinth. Note, if you will, that
this error is the opposite of the ones he was making
at the beginning of OBW; at that time, Hyacinth's
name often appeared when he meant Nettle, but by
this point, the substitutions have entirely reversed
themselves."

I checked back, and it's the method of communicating with the Mother which the narrator "would never have used while Nettle was alive". At the time of reading I presumed he was speaking in the past tense because he was convinced he was about to die (or at least 'cease to exist') and so would never get to use it. However, presumably Silk subsequently did, as Seawrack then appears pretty quickly afterward.

And from cephalothorax:
"> "I would never have used it while Nettle was alive",

No and No."

I understood Nettle DID sail with the rest back to Pajorocou, which is why Nettle is "making her own"[goodbyes] whilst Horn/Silk is talking to Daisy [p411] and why Daisy hopes "he and his eerie young woman, Nettle the old sibyl, and their bird [are] on course upon a greater sea" [p412]. Of course, as I've already admitted I don't understand the end, I could just be plain wrong...

and another from cephelothorax:

"Persnickety: Are we really meant to believe that
Severian, who writes about Master Ash, and the Green
Man, and being a white fountain hundreds of years
before he's born is going to consider "Horn" too much
of a stretch for his readers to believe because he's
got the love-vibe and walks through walls?"

On this I'm no more certain, but I got the distinct impression that the apprentice Severian was under the impression that Horn / Silk's spirit was actually Malrubius' ghost (hence his comment to Gurloes about Oreb), which of course does get mentioned frequently throughout TBOTNS( & he certainly pairs Malrubius with Triskele a fair bit). Whether Wolfe has managed the phenomenal task of tying the ends up, we won't know until someone has the energy to re-read TBOTNS looking for them. Surely such a thing is impossible, but I'm trying not to make the mistake of under-estimating Wolfe...

Ian

2001-02-07 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that the character called Horn in Silk's body is really Silk carrying Horn's memories, because Silk could not accept that Hyacinth was dead until the Hyacinth passage let him. Answers Ian Smith's confused question about the final Remora scene by offering this reading, and says the poster was surprised and will reread the three volumes.
answers · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn in Silk's body is Silk with Horn's memories

The person we have been thinking of as Horn in Silk's body is actually Silk with Horn's memories. Silk could not admit to himself that he was Silk because that would mean admitting that Hyacinth was dead. The Hyacinth passage enabled Silk to accept this.

I have to admit I was caught completely by surprise. Now, of course, I'll have to reread all three volumes in this light.

--Adam

2001-02-08 David DiGiacomo #

Argues that the figure in Silk's body is Horn, with his spirit rather than only his memories transferred, as the Neighbor's words suggest. Adds to Adam Stephanides' memory-transfer theory by quoting the Neighbor's line "I can send your spirit into someone else" and calling Fava's afterlife an earlier clue.
supports · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn's spirit inhabits Silk's body; Silk's body holds Horn's spirit, not memories

I would just add to this that the Neighbor told Horn "I can send your spirit into someone else" - spirit, not memories.

In retrospect, Fava's afterlife was a good clue.

2001-02-08 Peter Westlake #

Argues that the narrator of Return to the Whorl is Horn, not Silk, so the narrator never uses the Mother's communication method, and that Silk is the one who uses it. Supports this by pointing to Horn's expectation of Remora's return as an executioner and to Silk's talk with Daisy about having killed Hoof and Hide's father.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn is the narrator of Return to the Whorl

...
...

Yes - it's definitely Horn speaking at the time, and he never does use it. It's Silk who does that. I don't know exactly what happens to Horn when Silk recovers himself, but it's pretty terminal: he anticipates Remora's return as that of an executioner, and Silk talks to Daisy about having more or less killed Hoof and Hide's father.

Spectacled Bear.
