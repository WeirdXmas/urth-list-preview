The thread: A newcomer, Oliver Wakefield, asked how to fix text wrapping and who the list's members are, whether SF fans or general readers, and where they live, and Wolfe's popularity in the US versus the UK. Members answered mostly with personal self-descriptions, reading habits and locations (mostly US, with some UK, Australian and Canadian members), while line-wrapping advice was given by Roy Lackey. The thread then drifted into chat about Moorcock, Mieville, Patrick O'Brian, a Gojira book joke and California geography. Late in the thread Steven and Chris Laybourn raised Wolfe questions, including the Long Sun and Short Sun styles and online interviews, and dcooperv and Matthew Keeley answered them briefly. It ended with no real debate about the text, mostly social drift.
  • Oliver Wakefield: Wolfe is little known and his UK covers misrepresent him as pulpy fantasy
  • Roy C. Lackey: most members are American and Wolfe has never been a big seller
  • David Duffy: Australian libraries stock every new Wolfe novel, Wolfe is widely read there
  • Adam Thornton: Moorcock carries Peake's tradition against Tolkien's, with Harrison and Mieville as heirs
  • Russ Allbery: judge Mieville by The Scar and Perdido Street Station, not Iron Council
  • Steven: asks what Wolfe intended with the differing Long Sun and Short Sun styles and first-person choice

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Myth and folklore sources

Hi Oliver. I'm from Texas. I've always gotten the impression that most of the people who post here are from the US, but that's certainly not a scientific sampling. Argues that Moorcock champions Peake's tradition against Tolkien's in British fantasy, and that Moorcock's protégés Harrison and Mieville carry on that line. Responds to Matthew Keeley's dismissal of Moorcock by naming Peake's Gormenghast books and the Harrison and Mieville novels as examples, offering praise and a mild reservation about Mieville.
disputes · author-and-biography · theories: Moorcock champions Peake over Tolkien in British fantasy

There is that.

Although one could argue that "Rape and Forgiveness" is absolutely in the Mervyn Peake tradition.

The thing I think is useful to remember about Moorcock is that he represents an alternative theme in British fantasy; effectively, he's championing the cause of Peake over Tolkien.
