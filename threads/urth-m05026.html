The Will Of God

The thread: The thread asked whether characters in The Book of the New Sun can know the will of God, and whether the Hierogrammates or Abaia perceive or resist it. David Stockhoff argued divine will is inaccessible and that readers cannot verify miracles, while John Watkins doubted the Hierogrammates know God's will. brunians held that every event falls under God's will, and that multiple timelines are real through personal experience. Jeff Wilson proposed superior witness through shared fragments and shared memories, which Stockhoff doubted. Dave Tallman argued that if all timelines exist, every event becomes God's will, which undermines the concept. The thread drifted into drugs, dreams and joking tangents, and ended without agreement.
  • David Stockhoff: divine will is inaccessible to humans and the question is whether actors believe they do good or evil
  • John Watkins: the Hierogrammates do not know God's will and may not sincerely hold their piety
  • brunians: every event falls under God's will, and multiple timelines are real through personal experience
  • Jeff Wilson: the Hierogrammates' shared witness and memory give them knowledge of God's will
  • Dave Tallman: if every possible timeline exists, every event would be God's will, contradicting divine will
  • Craig Brewer: multiple timelines matter only if one can actually move between them

Themes: Time loops and causality, Gods, angels and the Increate, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

2008-12-07 Craig Brewer replying to brunians #

Argues that the idea of multiple timelines matters only if one can actually move between them, otherwise alternate timelines are practically meaningless as metaphysics. Frames this as a limit on the speculative value of parallel worlds, distinct from their usefulness within fiction, responding to brunians' point about events becoming something that had not occurred.
disputes · genre-and-craft

The one thing that's bothered me about multiple timelines is that it only makes any difference if you can actually move between them. That's great for stories where that happens, but even if it is true, if "I" only exist and ever will exist in one timeline, then all the rest might as well not exist.

In other words, the idea of multiple timelines only really becomes speculatively interesting if time travel is a real possibility. Otherwise, the answer to "what if there are multiple timelines?" is, for all pratical purposes, "It doesn't matter."

Of course, within speculative fiction, all kinds of great stuff can come from the idea. But when it becomes an actual metaphysical speculation, I just don't really know what to do with it.

2008-12-07 Josh Geller replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that every event, including the actions of Abaia and his lords, falls under the will of the Increate, since God either wills or permits whatever occurs, so one can only recognize that will after the fact. Responds to David Stockhoff's question about whether the Hierogrammates and Abaia perceive the Increate's will, reframing it through a definitional argument about 'God' and 'will' rather than the characters' knowledge.
disputes · theology-and-religion

Individual entities may or may not know the will of God. This does not stop them from doing the will of God. Nothing happens save by the will of God.

God, will, occurred, did not occur: these are terms.

'God' means the (presumed) omniscient, omnipotent ruler of the world. You may or may not believe that such a thing exists. This is immaterial: that is the definition of the term.

Will is a deliberate act.

I am going to assume that everyone here knows what it means to say that something did occurred or that something may have occurred but did not in fact occur.

Assuming that there is an omniscient, omnipotent ruler of the world, everything that occurs is in accordance with said ruler's will: either He wills it to occur, or he chooses not to prevent it.

So once something has occurred, you can look back and say "that was the Will of God". Before it occurs, you don't know.

2008-12-07 David Stockhoff replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that Stockhoff's claim that God's will is either active or permitted is too simple, and that in a novel the question of what occurred is only settled by the text. Responds to a quoted post, saying an event can occur and later be revised within alternative timelines, which makes occurred versus did-not-occur a weak tool for The Book of the New Sun.
disputes · theology-and-religion · chronology-and-plot-logic

Ah, but you said, "That's it." As in, it's that simple. Now you're saying it's not that simple: "either He wills it to occur, or he chooses not to prevent it."

This seems like a huge distinction to me. Creating a big enough space to fit a four-volume novel or three, maybe.

Anyway, we can't ever know which is which, but we can speculate. Especially since the actual topic here is not God's actual will, but a mere novel depicting such events.

(For the same reason, I'd say "occur" vs "not occur" is pretty well useless in analyzing TbotNS. We have only "Severian said it occurred."

And even within that category, can something occur and then become something that has not occurred? I'd say so. Maybe not in TBotNS, but certainly in Wolfe and any time you are dealing with alternative timelines. I mean something more than "I thought it occurred, but then I found out it didn't.")
