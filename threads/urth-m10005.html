Sorcerer's House: First Impressions *spoilage*

Urth list · 13 messages · 2010-04-05 to 2010-04-12

2010urthJosh GellerDave LeblingMo Holkar / UKGCraig BrewerGwern BranwenJames WynnJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-OakesJonathan GoodwinRob Thornton
The thread: Readers discussed The Sorcerer's House, with Dan'l Danehy-Oakes finding its mysteries largely resolved, especially Bax's parentage, and Craig Brewer quoting John Clute's praise of it as a single-sitting tall tale. The thread shifted to Robert Thornton's claim, endorsed by Jeff Wilson, that a wolf is a key to Wolfe's books, which Neil Gaiman echoed in a Goodreads review. Dave Lebling and Jeff Wilson then debated who first deduced the identity of Number Five in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, with Mo Holkar disputing the timing from the WolfeWiki Cave Canem chronology. The priority question was left unresolved, with Holkar asking whether anyone had read Clute's Strokes.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: The Sorcerer's House is clearer than Wolfe's usual work, with mysteries largely resolved
  • Craig Brewer: Clute's single-sitting tall-tale reading matches other readers' reactions
  • Jeff Wilson: Thornton's wolf-key theory fits Gaiman's review; priority for it may belong to Borski
  • Dave Lebling: He decoded the narrator's identity around 1972-73 and the other segments took rereadings
  • Mo Holkar: Gaiman's deduction of Number Five's name must postdate Strokes and predate Lexicon Urthus
  • Robert Thornton: Gaiman's review says a wolf can always be found in a Wolfe book

Themes: Unreliable narration, Doubles, twins and clones, Hidden identities and parentage

2010-04-05 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Reports that the poster finished The Sorcerer's House and found its mysteries largely resolved by the end, especially Bax's birth parentage, which makes the rest seem straightforward. Gives this as a first impression written before reading any list discussion, noting the book's clarity is relative to Wolfe's usual difficulty.
informs

I'm writing this up before reading any of the stuff that's come over the list, which I've kept isolated in a folder of its own.

I finished TSH yesterday and it seemed to me remarkably free of "Huh?"s. By which I mean, by the end of the book I think Wolfe cleared up most if not all of the mysteries pretty, uh, clearly. By the time we find out who Bax's birth-parents are, the rest seems pretty straighforward.

Straighforward for Wolfe, that is...

2010-04-05 Craig Brewer replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Shares a quoted passage from John Clute's review of The Sorcerer's House in NYRSF, noting that Clute's reaction resembles the reactions of other readers. Quotes Clute's praise of the novel as a single-sitting tall tale, centering on love of telling and the bond between the characters.
informs

John Clute's review just appeared in NYRSF (alongside yours of _The Long Man_, so this is probably not news to you in particular), and his reaction was remarkably similar!

Since I just read it a few minutes ago, I have it at hand. His summary: "...I believe in Baxter Dunn... Because I wanted a Wolfe novel to read in a single sitting without escaping us utterly, and that by virtue of that single sitting love might be conveyed: Wolfe's love of telling, the love of the main characters of _House_ for each other, the love felt by the reader for a book whose author may, for once in his life, have told a tall tale around the campfire. I believed every word of it."

2010-04-08 Rob Thornton replying to Jonathan Goodwin #

Informs the list that Neil Gaiman posted a Goodreads review of Sorcerer's House, quoting his claim that a wolf can always be found in a Wolfe book as a key to the text. Shares the quotation as a pointer to the review rather than offering an argument of its own.
informs

Hi folks,

I am a member of Goodreads, a site where readers and authors can share their experiences with books. Each member can post a rating and a small review of books that they have read/are reading.

Neil Gaiman is also a member and he posted a brief review of Sorcerer's House at: http://www.goodreads.com/book/show/6845909-the-sorcerer-s-house

I will quote the most interesting part of the review here:

"As a side note, I have a mad theory that you can always find a Wolf in a Gene Wolfe book, and it will always be the key, or a key, to the text. This book does nothing to disprove my theory."

Rob

2010-04-09 Dave Lebling replying to James Wynn #

Recounts how the poster first decoded the narrator's identity in The Fifth Head of Cerberus around 1972-73, pointing to the library scene where the narrator seeks his father's books near works by Vinge, Wilhelm, Woolf and Bertram Wolfe. Responds to James Wynn's remark crediting Robert Borski, noting that the other two segments took many rereadings to work out.
informs · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: The narrator of The Fifth Head of Cerberus is Wolfe's father's son via library clues

This thread makes me wish I had published that (somewhere?) back when I first figured it out, which was the first time I read 5HC in Damon Knight's Orbit 10, in 1972 or '73. Too bad there was barely even an Arpanet then, much less the Urth list. I think SF-Lovers was later, but I'm not sure.

The name of the narrator was pretty obvious from the library scene alone; the narrator looking for his father's books near works by "Winge" (Vernor Vinge), "some German" (Kate Wilhelm), Virginia Woolf, and Bertram Wolfe. Not terribly subtle. Figuring out the other two segments was much harder; that took many rereadings many years later. I was completely bewildered by them.

-- Dave Lebling, aka vizcacha

2010-04-09 Mo Holkar / UKG replying to Jeff Wilson #

Questions when Neil Gaiman deduced Number Five's real name in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, arguing the WolfeWiki Cave Canem intro dates it after Strokes (1988) but before Lexicon Urthus (1994). Responds to Jeff Wilson by quoting the intro's sequence of books, reading it as showing the name was deduced after Strokes and before Lexicon Urthus.
disputes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Gaiman deduced Number Five's name after reading Strokes

I'm not sure about that last:

"Once upon a time, a not-so-young man read a book called THE FIFTH HEAD OF CERBERUS ... Then along came a book called STROKES by John Clute, where he learned FIFTH HEAD had a somewhat more sophisticated structure and that it was even possible to deduce Number Five's real name, followed still later by Michael Andre-Driussi's LEXICON URTHUS..." http://www.wolfewiki.com/pmwiki/pmwiki.php?n=CaveCanem.Intro

Suggests to me that he deduced the name after reading Strokes (pub 1988), but before reading LU (pub 1994).

best,

Mo
