Wolves

Urth list · 5 messages · 2014-04-11 to 2014-04-11

2014urthThe Book of the Long SunMarc AraminiPiotr SzczęsnyJeff WilsonAntonin ScriabinAndrew Mason
The thread: The thread asked whether wolves are a hidden pattern running through all of Gene Wolfe's fiction, as Neil Gaiman had suggested. Andrew Mason opened by proposing a catalogue of wolves across the works, arguing Captain Serval in Long Sun is one, and asking for more finds. Antonin Scriabin and Piotr Szczęsny added further examples, including Rex von Madadh, Lupine, and a pack in The Land Across, with Jeff Wilson linking the alzabo in Cerberus to a furry pack predator. Marc Aramini disputed the premise, arguing the animal symbols appear only sometimes and that Wolfe's obsession with names better explains them. The thread ended without resolution, with supporters listing examples and the skeptic holding his ground.
  • Andrew Mason: wolves are hidden across Wolfe's fiction, and Captain Serval in Long Sun is one
  • Marc Aramini: animal symbols appear only sometimes; Wolfe's interest in names and symbols better explains them
  • Jeff Wilson: the figurative reading is the key to Wolfe's mysteries, and the alzabo fits a furry pack predator
  • Antonin Scriabin: added werewolf examples Rex von Madadh and Lupine from memory
  • Piotr Szczęsny: The Land Across contains open wolves in a forest pack

Themes: Beasts and the animal in us, Names and their meanings, Myth and folklore sources

2014-04-11 Marc Aramini replying to Andrew Mason #

Disputes the claim that every Wolfe story contains a wolf, arguing the animal symbols appear only sometimes and that Wolfe's obsession with names and symbols better explains them. Grounds this in an essay in Castle of Days on famous writers' names, the howling in "The Mountains Like Mice," and the Long Sun naming scheme tied to Wolfe's own name and his wife Rosemary.
disputes · author-and-biography · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolves in every Wolfe story is overstated; Wolfe's name influences the Long Sun naming scheme

Well "each" is such a all inclusive term. Sometimes they are there, sometimes they aren't. I think a more apt rationale for this is he is obsessed with names, representations, symbols... There is an essay in castle of days where he goes on about what several famous writers' names mean. With such an obvious authorial symbol and a symbolist writer they creep in, but they aren't in everything.

I think the first time I noticed it in my survey of the short fiction was the howling in the background of "the mountains like mice" but as I said it isn't in every story.

The long sun naming scheme is certainly influenced by the fact that he is named after an animal and his wife Rosemary a plant/seasoning

2014-04-11 Jeff Wilson replying to Andrew Mason #

Recalls that Wolfe said finding the figurative Gene Wolfe inside a story is the first step to solving its mysteries, and applies this to The Fifth Head of Cerberus. Argues the alzabo fits as a genetically altered furry pack predator rather than as the eat-by-eat family devourer Neil Gaiman proposed.
supports · puzzle-solving · theories: Each Wolfe story contains a figurative Gene Wolfe; The alzabo is a genetically tampered pack predator

IIRC, he stated more strongly in the old GEnie days, that his first step in solving the mysteries of a Wolfe story is to find the figurative if not literal Gene Wolfe *in* the story. This certainly worked for 5HC, though I didn't particularly think the alzabo passed muster as a "gene wolf" in the sense Neil nominated it (it eats the family member by member) but as genetically tampered furry pack predator (its pack is on the inside), sure.

2014-04-11 Andrew Mason #

Proposes tracking wolves hidden across Wolfe's fiction, citing Neil Gaiman's idea that each story contains one, with examples from Cerberus, New Sun, Soldier, The Sorcerer's House and Long Sun. Argues that Captain Serval in Long Sun is a wolf, citing the etymology of 'serval' from 'lobo cerval' and his round chin and moustache, and asks whether others have found more.
proposes · source-and-allusion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Captain Serval is a wolf in disguise; Each Wolfe story contains a hidden wolf

Neil Gaiman, I think, proposed the theory that in each of Wolfe's stories there is a wolf. I've been thinking about how this works out. In some cases, of course, the wolves are quite open, while in others they are hidden.

_Cerberus_ has an open Wolf (the name of the Shadow Child when just one is left) and, by general agreement, a hidden Wolfe.

_New Sun_ has the story of the boy called Frog, brought up by wolves.

_Soldier_ has a known werewolf, and possibly hidden ones as well.

_The Sorcerer's House_ also has a known werewolf.

_The Wizard Knight _ has nothing explicit so far as I can see. Could Able's miraculous dog really be a wolf?

In _Long Sun_ I think I may have found one. Captain Serval (Hyacinth's ex-lover/estranged husband, depending who you believe) is named, I think, only once in the main text - we know his name mostly from the glossaries - and is generally called just 'the captain'. Now, the serval is actually a feline animal, but its name derives from 'lobo cerval', meaning 'deer-like wolf'. Strikingly, he has a round chin and a moustache.

I have not found any in Short Sun, but I will keep on looking.

Has anyone found any others?
