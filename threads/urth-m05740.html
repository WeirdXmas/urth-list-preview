What's So Great About Ushas?

Urth list · 5 messages · 2008-07-10 to 2008-07-10

2008urthThe Book of the New SunO'Donnell, Tim (BOSI)Jeff WilsonPaul B
The thread: Jeff Wilson and Paul B debated whether the Hierogrammates of the Urth books are benevolent, with Wilson reading them as grim benefactors whose alien, time-travelling outlook skews their morality, comparing them to the Star Trek 'Empath' episode. Paul B argued they cannot be good guys because they could save Urth's population but choose not to, and that they are not resource-constrained, so only a utilitarian ethic would excuse them. Wilson pressed how they could evacuate people yet disclaim responsibility, linking it to Israel. Tim O'Donnell argued they are not benevolent and intend to condition humanity toward Hierodom through a hidden flood plan with Severian as a social engineering tool, while offering a contrary view that the Hieros are imperfect beings aiming to become like God. The thread ended in disagreement with no resolution.
  • Jeff Wilson: Hierogrammates are grim benefactors whose alien morality is skewed, like the Empath aliens testing McCoy
  • Paul B: Hierogrammates cannot be good since they could save Urth but choose not to; they are not resource-constrained, so only utilitarianism excuses them
  • Tim O'Donnell: Hierogrammates are not benevolent; they plan a hidden flood to condition humanity and use Severian as a social engineering tool
  • Jeff Wilson: if the Hierogrammates evacuated Urth they stay responsible and must aid humanity elsewhere, as with the problem of Israel

Themes: Gods, angels and the Increate, Power, rulers and politics, Free will and choice

2008-07-10 Jeff Wilson replying to Paul B #

Argues that the Hierogrammates in Wolfe's Urth books are grim benefactors whose alien, time-travelling nature gives them a skewed moral outlook, and draws a parallel to the Star Trek episode "Empath" where aliens torture McCoy to test a healer. Grounds this in their long-lived, fluid individualism of merging and splitting parts of a greater whole, and offers a C.S. Lewis analogy of adopting a wounded orphan.
proposes · genre-and-craft · close-textual-reading · theories: Hierogrammates are grim benefactors with skewed morality

I'm stuck by the parallel to the original Trek episode, "Empath", where the crusty, mean aliens torture McCoy within a whisker of death to see if the mute healing empath woman can/will cure him at the cost of her own life, before deciding to use their limited resources to save the her planet instead of others in danger.

Remember that the Hierogrammates are long-lived aliens from another dimension with rather fluid individualism, as at least some of them merge and split off intelligent parts of the greater whole. This and the whole time travel issues can give them such a skew outlook or moral and ethical values that they can be grim benefactors while still pushing humanity's evil buttons early and often. C.S. Lewis might compare them to parents adopting a wounded war orphan from some distant land and forcing them to undergo painful medical procedures without explanation because they don't yet share a common language, lest the child die before one or the other can learn.

Argues that the Hierogrammates cannot be good guys because they can save Urth's population but choose not to, making any outcome morally suspect. Responds to Tim O'Donnell's suggestion that the Hieros could have flooded the world and then posed as saviors, and clarifies that the Hieros are an ascended race the Hierogrammates need as their creators.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Hierogrammates cannot be good guys

The fundamental moral facts in this case are that 1.) we know they can save the population of Urth and 2.) they choose not to. This makes any outcome morally suspect according to most ethical frameworks. As long as 1.) and 2.) are facts, the Hierogrammates cannot be good guys.

Incidentally, if I remember correctly as far as terminology goes the Hieros are an ascended race that the Hierogrammates (aliens) need as their own creators.

2008-07-10 Paul B replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the Hierogrammates in The Urth of the New Sun are not resource-constrained like Star Trek's aliens in the Empath episode, so their choices cannot be excused by scarcity. Grounds this in their stellar engineering, time travel and evacuation of Urth, and holds that only a utilitarian ethic would call their actions good.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Hierogrammates are not resource-constrained

It seems like you're challenging point 1.)
However, remember that the Hierogrammates have performed stellar engineering, and have the ability to time travel to boot. They are not likely to be resource constrained, and they are shown to be able to evacuate Urth as further proof. The examples you give are only interesting in a constrained scenario.

It is the contention of many, and I'd bet the author to be one of them, that "ethics" is not a strictly human concept. The only ethical system according to which the thing the Hierogrammates are doing is a good thing is utilitarianism - which has more flaws than most. If they wanted to be moral in any other sense, they'd have to try harder.

2008-07-10 O'Donnell, Tim (BOSI) #

Argues that the Hierogrammates are not benevolent and intend to condition humanity toward becoming Hieros, with Severian serving as a mystical figure and social engineering tool around a hidden flood plan. Responds to a prior claim by pressing the objection that they could have staged the flood as natural disaster, and offers a contrary view that the Hieros are imperfect beings aiming to become like God.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Hierogrammates condition humanity to become Hieros; Severian is a social engineering tool; Severian's book is a mystical testament to the flood

Sure, I'll take a quick stab at this, being of the aforementioned "Hierogrammates are not as good as they seem" persuasion. They admit to wanting to condition humanity to become the Hieros. This conditioning can come in all sorts of forms, and the spiritual is certainly not to be discounted given how secular Urth is. My contention is that they need a mystical account of what they've done to Urth for it to matter, like a certain other flood, for which they recruit the mostly unwitting Severian to act as a mystic and write The Book of the New Sun as a mystical testament to what otherwise would be a pretty secular and unpleasant occurrence.

Don't forget that Urth isn't the only human-inhabited planet, and as the Anchorite mentions if they wanted to save the population there were less murderous ways to do it - planetary evacuation in Ragnarok scenario. I believe this suggests that they instead wanted to do something else to the population, and Severian's mystical roles as Apu Punchau, the Conciliator, and the Sleeper may well have something to do with it. I see him as a social engineering tool.

Curious about others' opinions though, myself.

Paul

I guess my problem with that is - why tell the humans that the Hieros are going to wipe out the planet? Just cause the flood anyway and then tell the survivors and those repopulating the Earth that it was a natural disaster. Then you could instil in the people the idea that the Hieros were the ultimate saviours of man by helping them post-flood. I am not a Catholic and have no particular axe to grind but any other explanation of the Hieros seems unnecessary to me.

The Hieros are a higher order of imperfect vessels attempting to become like God. It doesn't mean that their solution was perfect, but that in no way means they are evil.
