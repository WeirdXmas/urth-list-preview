1999-12-17 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Moorcock's "Behold the Man" is less anti-religious than much of his other work and more nuanced than a first reading suggests, and that an author stays alive as an interpretive force. Responds to Alex David Groce's claim that knowing Moorcock's themes makes the story simple, arguing readers will keep using authors' past statements, and citing Homer, McLuhan and John 8:32.
disputes · theories: "Behold the Man" is less anti-religious than Moorcock

mantis says:

I tend to think of "Behold the Man" as being less anti-religious than a lot of Moorcock that seems less directly critical of religion. Certainly not positive, per se, but more nuanced than a first reading indicates.

In general, the author is alive and kicking until the day people cease to use information about what the author has written/said/claimed to desire/etc. in the past to help them shape their conception of anything he writes/says afterwards. Since we automatically do this with other people, and unless we all become Latros are probably going to keep doing so, I suspect the author will be in good shape for many years to come. Certainly the "author" of publishing/etc. is a "new" thing, and different with the changing media of the ages (insert random mandatory Marshall McLuhan observation) but the creating force behind a text/story is a very old concept--it's not as if post-Gutenberg thinkers made up Homer.
