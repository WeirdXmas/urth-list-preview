Did the Library Survive?

Urth list · 3 messages · 2003-07-10 to 2003-07-11

2003urthThe Book of the New SunMatthew MalthouseChris PJames Jordan
The thread: The thread asked whether the Library in The Book of the New Sun survived Ushas's flood. James Jordan proposed it survived, since it is transcendental like Borges's library and physically protected underground, pointing to the copy Severian left for later generations and the parallel copy in heaven. Matthew Malthouse disputed this, arguing the flood drowned the library's books in the mundane world even if the library is transcendental. Chris offered a symbolic reading in which Severian's placing of a book is an author-like release into the world, grounded in the Borgesian infinity of the Library and a scriptural image. The thread ends with the survival question unresolved and the exchange shifting toward symbolism.
  • James Jordan: the Library survived the flood, protected underground and transcendental like Borges's library
  • matthew.malthouse: the flood drowned the library's books in the mundane world, even if the library is transcendental
  • Chris: Severian's placing of a book is a symbolic release, like an author sending a work into the world

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Stories within stories, The dying sun and the cosmos

2003-07-10 James Jordan #

Argues that the Library in The Book of the New Sun may have survived the Ushas Flood, since it is transcendental like Borges's library and physically protected underground. Grounds this in Severian placing one copy of his book there for later generations, and in the parallel copy he left in the heavens, asking what Wolfe means by the same book on earth and in heaven.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · close-textual-reading · theories: The Library survived the Ushas Flood underground; Severian's book in the Library is meant for Ushas generations

It's been assumed that the Ushas Flood destroyed the Library, but I wonder. As Don noted, it's Borges's library, and is transcendental. Also, it's physically protected, arguably, being underground.
Sev. put one copy of his book there. I think this was for the Ushas generations to read, not for his own to read. I think he assumed that the Library (symbolically in another way the Bible, or Bible of Bibles) would survive.
Sev. put the other copy in the heavens. Wolfe seems to be doing something here, beyond explaining how he got his copy to translate. The same book in heaven and on earth? Or what?
What think ye?

Nutria

2003-07-11 Matthew Malthouse #

Argues that Ushas's flood in the Urth of the New Sun era drowned the library in the mundane world, leaving its books soggy and submerged, even if the library itself may be transcendental. Responds to James Jordan's suggestion that the library is physically protected underground and echoes Don's point that it resembles Borges's library.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: the library was destroyed by Ushas's flood

If it's transcendental who knows. But in the mundane world Nessus drowned. The library's under water and full of soggy vellum, paper, papyrus etc.

Matthew

2003-07-11 Chris P #

Argues that Severian's placing of a book in the Library is a symbolic act of release, like an author sending a work into the world without knowing whether it will be read. Grounds this in the Library's infinite, Borgesian nature, Severian's self-rescue in The Urth of the New Sun, and a remembered scriptural image of God placing the Torah in heaven and earth.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Severian's book in the Library is symbolic release; Severian saves himself by releasing his book

That's what I meant by the "philosophical" argument for the Library's survival. It's hard to say, given the nature of the Library, whether he ever expected it to be read at all - when you place one book among an infinite number of other books it's not much different from launching it off into space/the corridors of time. I feel that perhaps either Wolfe (or Severian - his subtlety as a writer is perhaps underrated when one considers him simply a dissembler) is here giving a nod to the leap of faith every writer takes when writing a book. It is a sentimental thing suited to the end of such a work, a symbolic ritual of release where the author frees his creation with no way of knowing if his meaning will ever come home in a reader, or where it may go that he never intended.

It is probably also notable that Severian literally saves himself by this act of release, in the continuation of his journey that is UotNS.

Nutria's comment about heaven and earth rings a bell, but though I spent some time hunting for a reference I was unable to come up with one. I could swear I recall having read somewhere that, in completion of his act of creation, God placed the Torah in the heavens and in the earth. Of course, it is not presented in quite as literal a sense as this, but who's to say that Severian is being purely literal either.
