The thread: Readers debated whether the violet eyes shared by Thecla and the paracoita, and the misspelled 'forebearance' in The Shadow of the Torturer, hint at Thecla's paternity or are just poetic or typographical quirks. Proposers such as b sharp, mournings glory and Roy C. Lackey built theories on Gunnie, Purn and Hethor and on Appian as a possible father, while Andy Robertson and Rex Racer argued for ordinary explanations, and the spelling dispute ended with Rex Racer and mournings glory unconvinced by each other's dictionary sources. A parallel thread on a Wolfe wiki reached agreement to proceed, with Ranjit Bhatnagar offering hosting and maru dubshinki offering help after May.
  • b sharp: violet eyes and Sword dream link Thecla and the paracoita, perhaps via the kaibit; Appian may be her father
  • Andy Robertson: violet eyes just poetically mean dark blue, no hidden connection
  • mournings glory: violet imagery foreshadows Thecla's death; Hethor and Purn may be one man; 'forebearance' might hint at ancestry
  • Rex Racer: 'forebearance' is a plain variant spelling with no hidden parentage meaning
  • Roy C. Lackey: disputes that Hethor uses Wellerisms and that Lune crewed the Quasar before Jonas
  • Ranjit Bhatnagar: offers to host a Wolfe wiki on urth.net if others share the maintenance

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Names and their meanings, Fathers, mothers and children

2006-05-01 Jeff Wilson replying to b sharp #

Argues that the khaibit, a clone-shadow of each Exultant child, is harvested for growth factors, which explains the Exultants' height and suggests other cosmetic changes and spare organs. Builds on the Thecla quote about eyes made larger by childhood poisons and on Mantiss's account of the khaibit, extending the idea of engineered Exultant bodies.
proposes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Khaibits are harvested for growth factors and organs

As Mantiss has it, each exultant child has a clone (the khaibit, egpytian word for shadow) from which certain items are harvested, notably growth factors. Given the Exultants' natural height, this results in a towering Exultant and a Khaibit of roughly normal height. Givne this precedent, doubltess any number of other cosmetic adjustments could be made to the Exultants, and the khaibits possibly serve as sources of spare vital organs as well.

2006-05-01 b sharp #

Asks what connection exists between Thecla's large violet eyes in The Book of the New Sun and the purple-irised, large-pupiled eyes of Hethor's paracoita, both first appearing in Shadow. Notes the Sword dream describing exultant women's eyes enlarged by childhood poisons and suggests possible links to the kaibit process.
questions · close-textual-reading · theories: Thecla and the paracoita share a hidden connection

Wolfe seems to use physical descriptions of his characters in somewhat mysterious ways. I have a few issues I'd like to discuss regarding this. First, in BotNS, Thecla is first described as having "great violet eyes, lids shaded with blue". Her large, violet eyes are noted numerous times later in the text (though they are once called "deep blue" when comparing them to Master Ash's eyes; perhaps this is more evidence of Severian's unreliablity?)

In Sword, in a mushroom induced dream, it is implied that Thecla's (and other exultant women's)eyes have been made to grow bigger by "the application of minute amounts of certain poisons in childhood". Now there is another quasi-character who is described as having eyes with great pupils as dark as wells, irises purple as summer flowers. It is Hethor's paracoita.

I don't know of any other characters who are described as having very large violet or purple eyes, so it is difficult to call the similarity a coincidence, especially since both descriptions first appear in Shadow. But what connection can there be between these two? I have some vague thoughts involving the kaibit process but I'm interested in any ideas others might have.

2006-05-02 mournings glory replying to b sharp #

Argues that violet and purple imagery in The Shadow of the Torturer foreshadows Thecla's death, linking the necropolis flowers and belladonna to courtesans and poison. Proposes that Gunnie in The Urth of the New Sun is Hethor's imaginary girlfriend, citing her cow-like eyes and the name Burgundafora as possibly purple-tinged, replying to b sharp's post.
proposes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Gunnie is Hethor's imaginary girlfriend; Violet imagery foreshadows Thecla's death

From: "b sharp"

Much of the early violet imagery in Shadow is associated with flowers in the necropolis. I.e., "The necropolis has never seemed a city of death to me; I know its purple roses (which other people think so hideous) shelter hundreds of small animals and birds." "Spring came, and with it the purple-striped and white-dotted lilies of the necropolis." "The lilies faded as lilies do, and the dark death roses came into bloom. I cut them and carried them to Thecla, nigrescent purple flecked with scarlet." Hence the chief use of violet/purple may be to foreshadow Thecla's imminent death.

Belladonna will do this and was often used by Venetian courtesans to enhance their desirability. Interestingly the first six letters in scopolagna -- which is how at one time Hethor refers to his lost love -- can also refer to a class of compounds extracted from belladona.

Isn't it implicit in URTH that Gunnie is actually Hethor's imaginary girlfriend? Her eyes are described "as large, as dark and as soft as any cow's," which doesn't necessarily rule out a darker shade of violet. Ditto for her real name -- Burgundafora -- I've always associated burgundy with a sort of wine-dark red shading into purple.

Then again maybe you're onto something. So let's have it, mr "c" note.

mourning's glory (whose namesake, of course, also comes in violet and purple-blue)

2006-05-02 mournings glory replying to don doggett #

Corrects Don Doggett's claim that several Wolfe characters have violet eyes by quoting the text on Noctua, Katharine, Valeria, Apheta and the House Absolute child. Argues that Thecla is the only one described with violet eyes and that Katharine's dark eyes make her son's brown eyes consistent, while Apheta's deep blue and the child's midnight eyes come closest.
disputes · close-textual-reading

Mr. Doggett:

Either you're misremembering, have a variant text, or I'm looking in all the wrong places.

Noctua is "luminous of eye" "and the light that is in them is not of Urth" -- "she, whose eyes held starlight."

Katharine, meanwhile, is "dark of complexion, dark of eye" -- like her son, who has brown eyes. (Genetically, there is no way a violet-eyed mother and a blue-eyed Ouen could have a child with brown eyes.)

As for Valeria, "She had dark, curling hair, like Thecla's, but her eyes were dark too. Thecla's were violet." Here Severian deliberately contrasts the two women's eye color. (Perhaps, in Valeria's paraphrase of "I am all the daughters of my father's house, And all the brothers too," you are confusing her with Twelfth Night's Viola -- whose name indeed suggests violet.)

Two others, however, come a little closer to violet, if not all the way there.

Apheta's eyes are "large and luminous and of the deep, deep blue of her sea, unfaded by age." While the little girl Sev encounters in the antechamber of the House Absolute has "sparkling midnight eyes."

So it seems b sharp's original assertion is still correct.

2006-05-02 Andy Robertson replying to b sharp #

Argues that the violet eyes of Thecla and the Autarch's paramours are simply a poetic way of saying dark blue, with no hidden kaibit connection needed. Answers b sharp's question about the shared violet-eyed descriptions in Shadow by pointing to the connotations of blue eyes and to the dying Urth.
answers · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Violet eyes are a poetic way of saying dark blue

"Violet eyes" is just a poetic way of saying dark blue eyes.

Blue eyes are not found among animals, only among humans.

But "blue-eyed" also has poetic connotations of outdoorsy, healthy, sunburned, blonde, natural, happy, which Wolfe does not want to bring up in connection with the paramours of the Autarch, who should be surpassingly beautiful but sick, like the dying Urth.

This isn't complex, but in this case I **think** it's the whole story.

Wolfe has a wonderful way of implying much in a few words, and if you pick those words apart you won't always find a conscious construction under them.

I believe it was Mr. Andre-Driussi who argued earlier that Hethor and Purn might be younger and older versions of the same individual. (I've given up ever trying to find anything in the archives.)

1) Purn wonders if Lune is "big and white" -- which may mean he crewed aboard the Quasar before the moon was forested, possibly making him a contemporary of Jonas.

2006-05-06 mournings glory replying to b sharp #

Asks whether the misspelling "forebearance" in a Master Gurloes note in The Shadow of the Torturer is a hidden clue to Thecla's paternity or merely a typo. Grounds the question in the word's distinction from "forbear" and Wolfe's consistent usage in the other Urth books, and also asks about typos in The Wizard Knight and Short Sun editions.
questions · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Forebearance misspelling hints at Thecla's paternity

I came across what is either an interesting clue in this regard, a typo, or sloppy copy-editing, depending on your druthers.

In Chapter VI of _Shadow_ Severian is reading a note from Master Gurloes:

"'By the will of a court we have in our keeping the exulted person of the Chatelaine Thecla; and by its further will we would furnish to the Chatelaine Thecla in her confinement such comforts as lie not beyond reason and prudence. That she may while away the moments until her time with us is come - or rather, as she has instructed me to say, until the heart of the Autarch, whose forebearance knows not walls nor seas, is softened toward her, as she prays she asks that you, consonant with your office, provide her with certain books...'"

Now what is the oddest word in all of the above paragraph? It's "forebearance" with an -e, which relates to *ancestral* forebearers, not to "the action or habit of forbearing." In the other four books of the Urth cycle, Wolfe also always uses the e- version of 'forebears' to mean ancestors and the non-e version 'forbear' to mean, "to bear with, have patience with, put up with, tolerate."

So is this indeed a secret clue as to Thecla's paternity? Or simply a typo/copyediting mistake? (Speaking of which, has the Garsec/Garvaon mistake been corrected in the trade paperback edition of _Wizard Knight_? Sad to say, but the very nice SFBC edition of _Book of the Short Sun_, despite being printed on much higher quality paper, perpetuates all the typos of the original edition, which is surely the most error-ridden of any Wolfe book, primary or secondary.)

Also, if not a typo, however would such wordplay survive translation into another language?

Me sSe eg

Disputes Rex Racer's claim that 'forebearance' is an acceptable variant of 'forbearance', arguing the word could also mean ancestry and that the Oxford English Dictionary lists only the standard spelling. Grounds the objection in the OED's entry for forbearance, which cites usage as early as 1593, and asks for an alternate reputable source.
disputes · source-and-allusion

But you will allow that forebearance can also mean pertaining to one's forebears, right? And while you may be correct about the usage of forebearance for forbearance in the personal correspondence of yesteryear, you will not find the variant spelling listed anywhere at all in Wolfe's beloved dictionary of choice, the O.E.D. -- which seems curious to me since it generally lists all known variants, even when obsolete or rare. 2006-05-06 Rex Racer replying to mournings glory #

Argues that "forebearance" with an e is simply a variant spelling of "forbearance" with no link to parentage or forebears, so reading a hidden meaning into it is overreach. Supports the claim by citing common usage, several dictionaries, and the poster's own business-letter use of the word, while rejecting the claim that it is an old-fashioned correspondence usage.
disputes · close-textual-reading

I have common usage, and a couple of dictionaries at hand here that have forebearance listed as a variant of forbearance and make no reference to it having to do with parentage. I have seen it with an e and without, and have used it with an e or with out. I think too much is being read into the use of a variant spelling. It isn't something from the the personal correspondence of yesteryear, either, as I am not yet 40 and used it in a business context. :-)
