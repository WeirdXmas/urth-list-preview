Gene Wolfe Covers

Urth list · 5 messages · 2013-01-30 to 2013-01-30

2013urthThe Book of the New SunStephen HoyDavid StockhoffAntonin Scriabin
The thread: A brief thread started when Stephen Hoy asked why the French translation of The Claw of the Conciliator became 'La Griffe du demi-dieu' rather than keeping 'conciliateur', noting that 'demi-dieu' suggests demigods like Hercules but loses the redemptive intermediary sense. Antonin Scriabin shared a gallery of foreign covers for The Book of the New Sun, and David Stockhoff praised some of them while asking who the sword-wielding woman on one cover was. Scriabin answered that the figure was borrowed from the cover of Matthew Sturges's Midwinter, which explains the cover's weak link to The Shadow of the Torturer. The thread ended there, with the cover question answered and Hoy's translation question left without reply.
  • Stephen Hoy: 'demi-dieu' loses the redemptive intermediary sense of 'conciliateur'
  • David Stockhoff: some foreign covers capture Wolfe's literary side best; asks about the sword-wielding woman
  • Antonin Scriabin: shares the cover gallery and traces the sword figure to Midwinter's cover

2013-01-30 Stephen Hoy replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Questions why the French translation of The Claw of the Conciliator is titled 'La Griffe du demi-dieu' rather than keeping 'conciliateur'. Suggests 'demi-dieu' evokes demigods like Hercules but loses the sense of a redemptive intermediary that 'conciliator' carries.
questions · close-textual-reading

just noticed Claw of the Conciliator translated into French as "La Griffe du demi-dieu"...at first glance, seems an intrusive choice by the translator. Why 'demi-dieu' over 'conciliateur'? ?I suppose the former may have the merit of invoking demi-gods like Hercules, but I think it skirts the idea of a redemptive intermediary that conciliator bears with it.

2013-01-30 David Stockhoff replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Praises a set of foreign covers for The Book of the New Sun, singling out a mountain scene and a monk-like Severian and calling one cover the truest to Wolfe's literary side. Responds to Antonin Scriabin's shared link by describing the covers' looks and asking who the woman with the sword is.

The Lake of Birds is great for suggesting the opposite of S&S---it looks almost like a modern cover for a Victorian novel, and in a sense it may be the truest to Wolfe's literary side.

Who would be the woman with the sword?

2013-01-30 Antonin Scriabin replying to David Stockhoff #

Reports that the sword-wielding figure on one Wolfe cover was taken from another novel's cover, Midwinter by Matthew Sturges, which the poster traced after searching. Argues this explains why that cover bears little relation to The Shadow of the Torturer, answering David Stockhoff's question about who the woman with the sword is.
informs

I was confused by the one with the sword-toting elf as well. It look really familiar (the artwork is very similar to that of the *Mistborn *series),
and after some digging I found that the cover was just taken from this book<http://www.amazon.com/Midwinter-Matthew-Sturges/dp/1591027349>. This explains the disconnect between the cover and, well, virtually everything about *The Shadow of the Torturer*.
