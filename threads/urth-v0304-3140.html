The thread: A thread that began with a Shadow of the Torturer quotation and a request for a book like Gene Wolfe's drifted into recommendations of other authors. Steve Case asked for a little-known writer for a gift certificate; Malco suggested Alasdair Gray's Lanark with no explanation. Timothy Reilly separately argued Nabokov's prose is cold and that Humbert's regret is a smokescreen, and Jonathan Laidlow joked about pulp fiction. The main substantive exchange concerned Iain M. Banks: jismulkstis praised entry points and criticized Feersum Endjinn's phonetic style, matthew.malthouse argued for publication order, and Thomas Bitterman contrasted Severian with Zakalwe. The thread ended without resolution, drifting from Wolfe to Banks and Nabokov.
  • Timothy Reilly: Nabokov's prose is cold and buries feeling; Humbert's regret is a smokescreen
  • Thomas Bitterman: Severian is buffeted by events while Zakalwe acts to earn redemption
  • jismulkstis: Banks is a good entry point, though Feersum Endjinn's phonetic writing hinders reading
  • matthew.malthouse: Culture novels should be read in publication order
  • Malco: recommends Alasdair Gray's Lanark
  • Jonathan Laidlow: jokes about pulp fiction and his place on the list

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Beasts and the animal in us, Free will and choice

2003-04-17 Timothy Reilly #

Says Nabokov's prose reveals a proud, cold personality that buries human feeling, despite admiration from Wolfe, Martin Amis and others, and agrees with Kingsley Amis that Humbert's regret in Lolita is a smokescreen. Grounds this in the Amis argument recounted in Martin Amis's memoir Experience and in the poster's own reading of Lolita.
supports · theories: Humbert's final regret is a smokescreen

Re Nabokov, I also greatly regret that I do not enjoy his prose as much as I'd like, given he is so admired by Wolfe, Martin Amis and other writers I greatly admire. Lolita I finished, but have had false starts on others (I may try again with Pale Fire now).

The problem for me with Nabokov is that the personality revealed by the prose always sounds like such a wanker, if you'll all pardon the phrase. A natural aristocrat so proud of his verbal skills and convinced of his unquestionable superiority that any genuine human feeling seems buried. Kingsley and Martin Amis had an argument along these lines about Lolita (recounted in the latter's autobiographical Experience), with the former essentially saying that Humbert's ultimate passage of regret near the end of the novel was just a smokescreen to make it appear Nabokov really cares, when he doesn't. I confess I agree.

Tim

2003-04-21 jismulkstis #

Recommends Iain M. Banks novels (Excession, Look to Windward) as entry points and warns that Feersum Endjinn's phonetic writing interferes with reading, comparing it to Pig's dialect in Return to the Whorl. Draws a stylistic contrast with Wolfe, noting Wolfe would never have a character use crude insults like the ones in Banks.
informs · genre-and-craft

In reply to my mail, Dave Rolsky <[email]> recommends
Feersum Endjinn for those who wish to try Iain M. Banks.

A word of warning: about a quarter of that book is written phonetically (Feerum Endjinn = Fearsome Engine). I found that this interfered with my enjoyment, just as having to decode Pig's dialog in Return to the Whorl did. There's a stylistic similarity for you.

I recommend Excession or Look to Windward for an intro to Banks. Use of Weapons is also very solid, but I thought the ending came somewhat out of the blue. Excession is thoughtful space opera with the best twist on the Climactic Space Battle I've ever seen. Look to Windward is about, among other things, why one should bother staying alive.

I can illustrate one stylistic difference between Banks and Wolfe by noting that I doubt Mr. Wolfe would ever have one of his characters refer to another as a "tentacled scumbag fuckwit".

Muskrat

> I've been following this discussion with some interest, because I do like

>

> think, is "These Are the Jokes" (which is brilliant).
>

> including "The Cabin on the Coast", "In the House of Gingerbread", "When I

2003-04-25 Thomas Bitterman #

Compares Severian of The Book of the New Sun with Zakalwe from Iain M. Banks's Use of Weapons, arguing that Severian is buffeted by events while Zakalwe acts to earn redemption. Grounds the contrast in Severian's events (guildhouse expulsion, Agia, becoming Autarch) and in the Culture's view that human action is effective.
proposes · author-and-biography · close-textual-reading

An interesting comparison could be made between Severian and Zakalwe. They're both men who've done terrible things in their past and are journeying toward some form of redemption. The major difference, and this goes to a major difference between the authors, is the agency of the characters. Spoilers ahead.

Severian is constantly buffeted by events: he's kicked out of the guildhouse, Agia pushes him around, he gets caught up in the war, backs into being the Autarch, and so on. His main decisions are moral: how to react, what to fight for and against. He's neither a pilgrim nor a penitent - personal action is ineffective as a moral instrument.

Zakalwe is a man of action. Action, in the form of what he understands as good works, meant for self-atonement and to earn the forgiveness of others. The Culture is a world where human action is efficacious - although Zakalwe is not forgiven (at the time the novel depicts) his attempt to earn forgiveness is not portrayed negatively, nor is it implied that he is laboring in vain.

Tom
