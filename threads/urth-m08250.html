Calde of the Long Sun and Learning to Read Wolfe

The thread: Readers debated what the title calde means and whether the office carries theocratic weight in Calde of the Long Sun. Paul B and Craig Brewer argued from the Spanish root alcalde (mayor) that it is a secular office, while Son of Witz floated a bishop-like reading and then accepted the mayor analogy. A second strand asked whether Pas or Silk engineered the 'Silk for Calde' evacuation, with Paul B demanding conclusive evidence and John Watkins arguing Pas's plan could run after his apparent death. Paul B's insistence on evidence kept the Pas question unresolved, and the thread ended without consensus on the instigator or on Silk's parentage.
  • Paul B: calde means mayor, the office is secular, and conclusive evidence is needed for claims about who instigated the evacuation
  • Craig Brewer: the calde holds no theocratic office and the mayor is the nearest analogue
  • Son of Witz: the calde may be a bishop or mayor figure beneath a Pope-like Prolocutor, though the charter could lend it theocratic weight
  • John Watkins: Pas's plan may run after his death, and Silk may have been engineered to lead an exodus
  • Dave Tallman: the Outsider, not Pas, inspired Silk's enlightenment and the rumors about him
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: a list belief holds that Pas meant the Silk embryo to serve as his own body

Themes: Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Gods, angels and the Increate, Power, rulers and politics

2009-01-13 Ken Rutsky #

Reports that the poster finished Calde of the Long Sun and feels more dazzled by it than by The Book of the New Sun, crediting a growing ability to read Wolfe's oblique asides and Bible references. Gives as evidence the revelation of Blood's father in the last pages of the volume and a plan to reread the earlier books.
informs · close-textual-reading

Just finished book 3 of the Long Sun. I am dazzled by this work, more than I was with the New Sun books.

I suppose some of it has to do with learning how to "read" Wolfe, which I believe I'm just starting to do. Now, I'm not a dullard; my degree is in English and my favorite writers include Borges, late-period Samuel Delany, Nabokov.a lot of "lit snob" stuff. However, I've little background in studying the Bible, which I'm realizing is key to Wolfe's work, and is something I'm rectifying on my own. In addition, I am getting better at spotting massive revelations about the book's characters and the Whorl itself in casual, almost throwaway asides and oblique references; in particular, I was blown away by learning who Blood's father is in the last pages of this volume. Having had these insights into his style, I'll have to go back and re-read New Sun in the future.

On to volume four! I can't wait to comb through the list archives to read past discussions on this series. I've been very impressed with what I've read here so far.

2009-01-13 Michael Straight replying to Ken Rutsky #

Argues that Gene Wolfe's Calde of the Long Sun rewards readers who follow his digressions, since the apparently unrelated conversations he steers toward are the real story rather than distractions. Responds to Ken Rutsky's post about learning how to read Wolfe, offering a general reading strategy with no textual citations.
supports

For me, one of the most important keys to enjoying Wolfe's work is to let him take you where he wants to go. He's constantly doing this bait-and-switch, leading you up to some exciting event, making you want to see it, then taking you aside to listen instead to some conversation that is (at least at first glance) completely unrelated. You chafe, feeling like you're missing the real story. But the key is - you are not. That thing you're missing is a distraction, a magician's trick. This conversation here is the real story. Don't miss it.

- Rostrum
