The leatherskin

Urth list · 7 messages · 2003-01-09 to 2003-01-10

2003urthThe Book of the Long SunWilliam H. AnsleyJames WynnJoe CilluffoDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: The thread asked how many legs the leatherskin in On Blue's Waters and The Book of the Long Sun has, and whether its limbs are legs or flippers. James Wynn initially argued for six legs with the tail in the water, and William Ansley objected that Horn could call fins legs and asked for a usage example. cilluff1 argued for more than six submerged legs based on the narration's wording, while Dan'l Danehy-Oakes proposed a crustacean identity, perhaps a giant softshell crab. Wynn later shifted to clawed hands drawn from Pausanias, linked the creature to the manticore, and finally proposed jointed forward arms with tail propulsion. No consensus was reached; the thread drifted from counting legs to new identifications.
  • James Wynn: six legs, tail dangling in the water, later clawed hands from Pausanias and jointed forward arms
  • William Ansley: Horn may call fins legs, so the leg count is open to doubt
  • cilluff1: more than six submerged legs, based on the wording of Wolfe's narration
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the leatherskin is a crustacean, perhaps a giant softshell crab

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Beasts and the animal in us, Names and their meanings

2003-01-09 James Wynn #

Argues that the leatherskin in On Blue's Waters has six legs, not twelve, and that its tail dangles in the water, which explains why only half its weight rests on the starboard gunwale. Responds to William Ansley's earlier claim that the creature has twelve legs, quoting the passage about six massive legs and half its weight on the gunwale.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Leatherskin has six pectoral flippers and a tail dangling in the water

William Ansley heroically proffers:
Way back on October 18, 1999 I argued that the leatherskin has twelve legs and no one has bothered arguing with my conclusions yet. What I said then: The leatherskin, one of the largest I have ever seen, stood with six massive legs and half its weight on the starboard gunwale, over which silver water cascaded. [OBW (hc), p. 62]
If this creature has only six legs and all of them are on the starboard gunwale (rather a tricky balancing act in the first place), then how can the gunwale be only bearing half its weight?

Crush blunders forward:
Ok. I think I have an understanding of what a leatherskin looks like.

The leatherskin is giant dugong with six pectoral flippers and a manticore's jaws.

The reason "half its weight" was on the starboard gunwale was because its massive tail-end was dangling in the water.

See the following link for my full explanation:
http://www.urth.org/whorlmap/leatherskin.htm

--- Crush

2003-01-09 William H. Ansley #

Disputes James Wynn's objection to the claim that the leatherskin in On Blue's Waters has twelve legs, arguing that Horn may call fins or flippers "legs" and demanding a quote showing a former Whorl denizen using the term for fish. Responds to the quoted passage describing six massive legs and a gunwale, and to Wynn's reply that the other six limbs are flippers.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: The leatherskin has twelve legs

Oh happy day! Finally, someone is arguing with my conclusions!

If I am to buy your argument, I have to believe that Horn doesn't know the difference between flippers and legs. Maybe he doesn't. Maybe he has never seen any creature with fins or flippers on the Whorl, so he calls all such appendages "legs." But, until you can show me a quote where Horn or some other former Whorl denizen talks about fish legs, I will remain doubtful.
--

William Ansley

2003-01-10 James Wynn #

Argues that the leatherskin in The Book of the Long Sun has clawed hands rather than flippers, drawing on Pausanias' description of a triton with hands, fingers and murex-like nails. Responds to William Ansley's doubt that Horn would call fins 'legs' by citing Pausanias as the source of the creature and suggesting it had no true arms.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Leatherskin derives from Pausanias' triton with clawed hands

William Ansley responded to Crush:
Oh happy day! Finally, someone is arguing with my conclusions!

If I am to buy your argument, I have to believe that Horn doesn't know the difference between flippers and legs. Maybe he doesn't. Maybe he has never seen any creature with fins or flippers on the Whorl, so he calls all such appendages "legs." But, until you can show me a quote where Horn or some other former Whorl denizen talks about fish legs, I will remain doubtful.

Crush takes another whack at it:
Hmmm, I wouldn't want you to remain in doubt, so I'll make a feeble attempt to calm your disquiet.

My first inclination was to say, "William, William, you're troubling yourself over nothing. Flippers **are** legs for an aquatic mammal."

But then I remembered that Pausanias' "Description of Greece" is the inspiration for the leatherskin (I'm quite certain of this). Pausanias said the triton has "hands, fingers, and nails like the shells of the murex." So it is reasonable to assume the leatherskin would too.

So I must amend my description, The leatherskin does not have flippers. It has clawed "hands." Considering the leatherskin's apparent clumsiness in getting on the boat, I'm guessing it did not have "arms". The point is that it did NOT have true flippers.

-- Crush

2003-01-10 James Wynn #

Proposes revising the reading of the leatherskin's legs in On Blue's Waters: the foremost claws sit on jointed arms that extend forward, and the creature is propelled by its tail rather than its claws. Grounds this on Horn's sighting, with claws scrabbling at the port gunwale before the head appears, and keeps Pausanias as the original inspiration despite the change.
proposes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Leatherskin propelled by its tail, claws are jointed arms; Pausanias remains the leatherskin's original inspiration

One thing I've reconsidered:
The morphology of the "legs" seems to differ from the dugong's flippers in that the foremost claws (at least) are attached to jointed "arms" that permit them to be extended in front of the animal to some degree. This is clear because Horn's first view of the creature was its claws "scrabbling" for the gunwale with the head not in site.
"Looking behind me, I saw great, coarse claws, each as thick as the handle of our ax, scrabbling for a hold on the port gunwale and rowling its wood like so many gouges. A moment later the head appeared and shot toward me." On Blues Waters, chapter 2
But it did not use its claws to impel itself at Horn because (I would guess) if its claws had such strength, it would not have had so much trouble getting on the boat. "The head shot toward" Horn because the leatherskin was impelled itself with its tail.

I don't think this undermines Pausanias as the original inspiration.

--Crush
