The thread: Mr Thalassocrat shared Borges's Funes the Memorious, a man with perfect memory, and asked whether Wolfe modeled Severian on it or whether Severian might instead have hyperthymesia, citing a Wikipedia definition. Lee Berman argued that Borges and Wolfe present Funes and Severian as precursors to Nietzsche's superman, too limited to recognize it, with Zarathustra and Apollonian-Dionysian themes recurring in The Book of the New Sun. António Pedro Marques held that Borges had already anticipated nearly every idea in Wolfe, which Wolfe applies practically, though from general impressions rather than close textual evidence. Jerry Friedman then offered a naming reading, that Funes echoes the Spanish funesto, meaning ill-fated, and the thread ends there without any response to it.

2011-01-24 Lee Berman replying to Mr Thalassocrat #

Argues that Borges and Wolfe cast Funes and Severian as precursors to Nietzsche's superman, too limited to recognize what they are, and that Nietzsche's Zarathustra and Apollonian-Dionysian dichotomy recur throughout The Book of the New Sun. Builds the case on the word dithyramb and passages in Borges's story, plus the Zoroaster gnostic link and 2001's use of Also Sprach Zarathustra. Responds to Mr Thalassocrat's quotation from Borges's Funes the Memorious.

Yes, I agree. Good find!. And I think it goes beyond the memory thing. The term "dithyramb" is used. And this from the third paragraph:

These are direct references to Nietzsche and it would appear that Borges/Wolfe view Funes/Severian as precursor to the ?bermensch, the superman, but a little too provincial/dumb to realize what he is.

Zarathustra is Nietzsche's term for Zoroaster, the gnostic religious figure with a name meaning something like "pure star". Nietzsche saw humanity as a transitional form between the apes and the future supermen. No coincidence that the main theme of the movie 2001-A Space Odyssey is Also Sprach Zarathustra.

Then we have Nietzsche's dichotomy between Apollonian (paternal, male, light, Judeo-Christian, science/order based) vs. Dionysian (maternal, androgynous, dark, pagan/mystic, individual experience based). This theme seems repeated over and over in BotNS.

I don't know if the lines of influence of ancient gnostic religion and philosophy are in series or parallel through Nietzsche and Borges to Wolfe but they are definitely there. I have not previously seen Nietzsche noted as a major influence on Wolfe but perhaps it should be considered.

2011-01-24 António Pedro Marques replying to Mr Thalassocrat #

Argues that Borges had already presented nearly every idea found in Wolfe, and that Wolfe applies Borges's philosophical ideas in practical form, like an engineer. Responds to Mr Thalassocrat's remark after reading Borges's Funes the Memorious, which prompted a question about Severian's memory. The post offers its view from general impressions of Borges's work rather than close textual evidence.
proposes · author-and-biography · theories: Borges presented nearly all of Wolfe's ideas first; Wolfe applies Borges's ideas in practical form

Mr Thalassocrat wrote (24-01-2011 07:36):

Heh. Borges excelled in that. He got some idea and he wrote a story with the right amount of substance to illustrate it - differing from other authors in that there's barely no scenery. Almost his entire work is like that. Read almost all of it a long ago... and sadly haven't gotten the time to reread, though it's itching. My impression is that it's got everything there. There are few, if any, ideas, in Wolfe or anyone else, which Borges didn't present. Borges of course got them from a myriad of sources, directly or indirectly. There's even a Borges story where some guy from the future, which has a life span of centuries, mentions he's read only a couple of books in his life, as what is important is rereading. GW responds to Borges in a way I haven't seen anywhere else; where Borges could be considered the philosopher, GW is the engineer, finding practical application for the other's ideas. (Again, neither Borges came uo with them on his own, nor is GW some kind of imitator: their roles are quite different, complement each other and neither is subordinate - GW didn't necessarily get his ideas from Borges, and had there been no Borges, he could have built his work, or another of comparable value, all the same.)

2011-01-24 thalassocrat #

Shares a Borges story about a man with perfect memory and suggests Wolfe may have drawn on it when building Severian. Proposes that Severian may instead have hyperthymesia, citing a Wikipedia definition of the condition with its two features: heavy dwelling on the personal past and extraordinary recall of specific events.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Severian suffers from hyperthymesia

A Borges story I hadn't read before, about a man with perfect memory: http://evans-experientialism.freewebspace.com/borges.htm

Nice to ponder on what use Wolfe might have made of it in constructing Sev.

But perhaps Sev suffered from hyperthymesia, even though it seems to have been defined as a condition only recently:
http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperthymesia

"1) the person spends an abnormally large amount of time thinking about his or her personal past, and 2) the person has an extraordinary capacity to recall specific events from his or her personal past."
