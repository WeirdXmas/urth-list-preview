Ouen and Catherine

Urth list · 5 messages · 2010-12-21 to 2010-12-22

2010urthThe Book of the New SunJames WynnLee BermanAndrew Mason
The thread: Readers debated whether Ouen's link to Severian is a deliberate clue in The Book of the New Sun or a mistake. James Wynn argued Ouen closely resembles Severian and that Erebus may be dead, citing Tussah's son Silk and the Nox myth. Andrew Mason called the Severian link a mistake rather than a trail, and answered Wynn's Dorcas-as-mother reading with Severian's twinhood, his virgin-priestess mother, and time journeys within Briah. Lee Berman proposed reading unresolved paternity through myth, comparing Severian to Hercules and Jesus and Joseph, and later argued that mythic figures like Erebus and Typhon are immortal and their deaths do not count. Wynn rejected that claim as too strong, since Wolfe himself marked the mythological references. The thread ended in disagreement with no resolution.
  • James Wynn: Ouen closely resembles Severian and is linked to him in a skewed way; Erebus may be dead
  • Andrew Mason: Severian-Ouen link is a mistake, not a deliberate trail; parallels need not match in every detail
  • Lee Berman: unresolved paternity should be read through myth, with Severian as a divine-impersonation birth like Hercules
  • Lee Berman: mythic gods and monsters like Erebus and Typhon are immortal and their deaths do not count as real

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Myth and folklore sources, Fathers, mothers and children

disputes · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Ouen is associated with Severian in a skewed way; Erebus may be dead, surviving as a name or ghost; The Student's fall describes Typhon's death

Well, not necessarily. In fact, I disagree. Ouen is similar to Severian in appearance. Very very similar. However, no one AFAIK actually SAYS he's Severian's father. This is a paradox and one I would like spelled out.

Look, I say that TUSSAH calling SILK his son is of the skewed kind, but explanation of most people would seem to put it in your second category. You can say he was his "adopted" son, but Tussah was not married to Silk's mother, did not formally adopt him, and if Silk ever met him, he doesn't remember it. He's arguably less Tussah's son than Mucor is Blood's daughter. Yet Tussah called him "his son", even though (as was true for Horn with Hoof, Hide, and Krait) "he was not the son of his body". Note that it was not necessary for Tussah to name a son as his successor and he left no method for anyone to ascertain that Silk was the one he was talking about anyway. He was merely making a statement of fact, as he saw it. In Horn's discussion with Silk on the floater, he tries to broach that riddle and Silk brushes it aside.

What I'm getting at is that perhaps Ouen's relationship is of this peculiar skewed kind, but we are missing something key about their true relationship. I think Ouen is associated with Severian, I just suspect he's not associated in exactly the way we're led to believe.

2010-12-21 Lee Berman replying to Andrew Mason #

Proposes that when the main text leaves Ouen's paternity unresolved, readers should look to myth, arguing that Severian resembles a Greek hero such as Hercules in a divine-impersonation birth. Draws parallels to a Tussah and Silk scenario in the Whorl and to Jesus and Joseph, where an adoptive father lends his looks to a divine son, in reply to James Wynn's paradox about Ouen.
proposes · myth-and-structure · allegory-and-typology · theories: Ouen is Severian's father by myth pattern; Severian parallels Hercules and Jesus

I think, when the main text fails us in some way, we are meant to delve to a different level of interpretation. I think mythological may serve here.

I find it virtually undeniable that Severian and his life resemble those of a generic Greek hero, incest curse included. I may be alone in sensing an especially close kinship of Severian to Hercules. Hercules was conceived via Zeus posing as Alcmene's husband Amphitryon (he was born a twin since the the real husband also impregnated her on the same night). So, who does Hercules resemble, Zeus? impossible. So it must be Amphitryon. Perhaps this theme at least indirectly suggests a solution to James' quandary or paradox.

If something similar happened in the Whorl, with Tussah and Silk, (Tussah being a kind of silk/worm) perhaps it happened also on Urth. And a similar situation with Jesus and Joseph. Did Jesus resemble Joseph? I gather there is some controversy over whether Jesus was descended from the House of David through his mother's or "father's" line. But there is more than a suggestion that Jesus physically resembled Joseph

Well, I don't have clear cut conclusions regarding this but the theme of an adoptive father giving his resemblance to an adopted but divine son seems highly relevant to me. Severian reminds me of Hercules and Jesus more than any other legendary figures.

2010-12-21 Lee Berman replying to Andrew Mason #

Argues that the gods and monsters of The Book of the New Sun, such as Erebus and Typhon, should be read as immortal mythic figures standing for dead human rulers, so their apparent deaths do not count as real deaths. Grounds this in the idea that legends keep such figures alive and in the claim that Wolfe makes them immortal like a sponge, reforming after being broken into pieces, which the poster links to the Whorl's electronic gods. Responds to Andrew Mason and James Wynn's readings of 'The Student and his Son' and Typhon's death.
proposes · myth-and-structure · allegory-and-typology · theories: gods and monsters are metaphors for dead human rulers; gods and monsters are immortal like legends; Whorl's electronic gods reform like biological organisms

I think it is a mistake to take the text too literally about death when it comes to mythologically named characters. Again I find it helpful to frame these characters in mythological terms when the main text fails to explain them to us. What I mean is that the gods and monsters in BotNS should be thought of as immortal.

Let's look at immortality from a science and SF point of view. Let's assume all gods and monsters are ancient literary metaphors for various human rulers. The human rulers have died, but as long as their mythological legends are told, they live on. They are immortal as long as some one believes in the stories of gods and monsters.

In the legends, the gods and monsters can be defeated, even vanquished, but they are immortal; they don't really "die" as humans do. They are trapped under rocks or chained up somewhere or placed in the sky as constellations etc. So if we think we see Erebus or Typhon or some other such named character in BotNS die, know that they aren't really dead. Not in the way we define human death. They can be resurrected under the right conditions because they are immortal in the way legends always are.

Now in the Sun series, Gene Wolfe has seen fit to take gods and monsters and imply they have a fleshly existence on Urth. How does he deal with the immortality issue? He almost explicitly makes them immortal in the way of a sponge. How can you actually kill a giant organism when it can and has broken into many pieces which can reform?

This is what Wolfe is trying to tell us with The Whorl. What happens electronically to the gods there is what happens biologically on Urth. They break into pieces and can reform.

(there is more to this theme which I won't get into here. But I offer assurance that some natural objections to this view can be addressed)

2010-12-22 James Wynn replying to Lee Berman #

Disputes Lee Berman's claim that the text should not be read literally about death for mythologically named characters, arguing that it is too strong to say an obviously referenced mythological figure shares a name with a monster. Grounds the objection in Wolfe having identified the mythological reference himself, and responds to Berman's post in the Ouen and Catherine thread.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Mythologically named character shares name with a monster

For me, the implication is too strong that an obviously referenced mythological character (which Wolfe has identified) has the same name as one of the monsters.
