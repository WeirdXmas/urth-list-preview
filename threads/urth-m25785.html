Severian's Mausoleum

Urth list · 9 messages · 2014-11-21 to 2014-11-24

2014urthThe Book of the New SunRobert PirkolaAntónio Pedro MarquesJeff WilsonNorwood, Frederick Hudson
The thread: Robert Pirkola asked what "faithless door" means in the Severian's mausoleum passage and traced it to William Gifford's 1852 poem Epitaph, where a blast bursts the door open. Frederick Hudson Norwood supported the echo with the poem's lines, then offered a personification reading in which the door is faithless because it failed to stay closed as Severian trusted it would. Pirkola pressed on whether the phrase is Wolfe's coinage and how so heavy a door could have been opened at all. Norwood floated the unlatched door as a possible deliberate clue. Jeffery Wilson later argued for personification, comparing the door to a faithless servant and linking the phrase to Severian's broken vows at the tale's start and end. The thread moved from source-hunting toward a shared personification reading, though the mechanics of the door were left unresolved.
  • Robert Pirkola: traced the phrase to Gifford's Epitaph and asked whether it is Wolfe's coinage and how the heavy door could be opened
  • Frederick Hudson Norwood: read the door as an echo of the Gifford poem and then as a personified, faithless door that failed Severian
  • Jeffery Wilson: personification like a faithless servant, tied to Severian's broken vows at the start and end of the tale

2014-11-21 Norwood, Frederick Hudson replying to Robert Pirkola #

Argues that the "faithless door" in Severian's mausoleum, which remains eternally ajar, echoes the door in the Gifford-attributed poem Epitaph that a wintry blast bursts open. Supports this by quoting the poem's lines and linking the door's failure to remain closed to Severian's story.
supports · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading

Just as in the poem the door is faithless for letting in the cold wind, Severian's door is faithless for failing to remain closed.

Rick Norwood

I was struck by the term "faithless door" in the passage describing Severian's mausoleum " . . . it was the smallness of the room, the thick walls of masonry, and the single, narrow window with its one bar, together with the faithless door (so massively heavy) that remained eternally ajar." (*Shadow and Claw*, pg. 17).

I do not know, and still do not know, what a faithless door is but while trying to find out, I came across this poem attributed to William Gifford, called *Epitaph* (1852), which has interesting resonances with Severian's story:

EPITAPH.
Where ragged nettles mark the rising ground,
And pois'nous night-shade breathes infection round,
Bill Brazen rots. In the good patriarch's phrase,
"Evil and few were his unhallow'd days:"
Yet in these few and evil the rank knave
Choused of a head-stone his poor father's grave;
Abused his mother; grudged his children bread,
And coffin'd them in wig boxes when dead;

Bullied his sister; kicked his wife to th' door;
Belied the parish books, and starved the poor.
Till grown too bad for this bad town, kind Heaven
Suffer'd the miscreant westward to be driven;
Where three long years in solitary state
He dragg'd the drunken hours through scorn and hate;
Till as he lay one night devoid of rest,
And conscience woke the worm within his breast,
A wint'ry blast, with hoarse, tremendous roar,
Rush'd through the gallery, burst the faithless door,
Approach'd him, touch'd-"Christ Jesus! save," he cried,
"A wretch! a hateful wretch!"-shook, groan'd, and died.
Now buried here, the scorn that dogg'd his way
Through life, still scents, and opens on his clay.
Stranger! this scene demands an awful pause:
A vicious world takes arms in virtue's cause:
Vice cowers beneath the shame she boasts to brave,
And finds chastisement on this side the grave.

2014-11-24 Jeff Wilson replying to Robert Pirkola #

Argues that the phrase "faithless door" in Severian's mausoleum is personification, comparable to a faithless servant who fails to serve as intended, since the door stays stuck open and cannot keep the interior safe. Grounds this in an archaic-phrasing parallel with a poem about a door that fails to keep out an intruder and in the placement of the phrase near the start and end of the tale, where Severian confesses his broken vows.
proposes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: faithless door is personification like faithless servant

I believe it is personification, and a faithless door will not serve its intended purpose like a faithless servant does not serve as intended. The mausoleum door is stuck open so it cannot keep safe the interior, likewise the door in the poem fails to keep out the person that bursts in. I suppose a door that fails to open when necessary would be equally faithless.

It's a quaint and archaic turn of phrase like many in the BOTNS, and perhaps it is no coincidence that it appears near the beginning and ends of the tale(s), which are also the times when Sev discusses his own faithlessness, as in the numerous vows he has broken.
