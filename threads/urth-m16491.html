two Siyufs?

Urth list · 11 messages · 2011-12-05 to 2011-12-07

2011urthJerry FriedmanLee BermanGerry QuinnLarry MillerDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: Readers asked whether Siyuf appears twice in the Long Sun saga, as a chem body like the Ayuntamiento's, and whether Larry Miller should reread Short Sun or start the Latro books. Gerry Quinn confirmed the double Siyuf, suggesting Potto had a copy made, which he found implausible, and asked where the councillors' chem bodies came from; Miller speculated about Talus factories or bought black mechanics without citing text. On the Latro question, Dan'l Danehy-Oakes said further Latro books are unlikely and called The Wizard Knight complex yet more explicit. Jerry Friedman argued it is an anti-Connecticut Yankee book where Able's modern knowledge never helps, and Lee Berman agreed, adding a Christ-over-technology reading. The thread ended in agreement on the Wizard Knight contrast rather than resolving the Siyuf question.
  • Gerry Quinn: Siyuf is two people, possibly a copy made by Potto, though implausible
  • Larry Miller: councillors' chem bodies may come from Talus or bought black mechanics; leans toward starting the Latro books
  • Lee Berman: Siyuf may be doubled but Long Sun is a slog; Latro books are central, with Christ as our real advantage
  • Jerry Friedman: The Wizard Knight is an anti-Connecticut Yankee book, suited to sharp YA readers
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: further Latro books unlikely; The Wizard Knight is complex yet explicit

2011-12-06 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Larry Miller #

Answers Larry Miller's questions about whether Soldier of Sidon is the last Latro book and whether The Wizard Knight matches Wolfe's usual depth. Says further Latro books are unlikely given weak early sales, and that The Wizard Knight is complex yet more explicit, with Wolfe's usual tricks pitched at younger readers and taken more seriously than a Connecticut Yankee premise.
answers · genre-and-craft

We may only hope, but I doubt there will be any more. If the books had been more successful when the first two came out there would probably have been several.

It's pretty deep and complex, but spells things out a little more. There are all the usual Lupine tricks -- lacunae, misdirection, etc. -- but on a level that can be handled by a YA reader.

It really isn't a "Connecticut Yankee" premise; it's taken much more seriously than that.

2011-12-06 Jerry Friedman replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that The Wizard Knight is the anti-Connecticut Yankee: Able's modern knowledge, skills and values never help him in the medieval-ish world, which the poster thinks Wolfe intended. Grounds this in the absence of any such help in the book, notes it suits a sharp YA reader, and compares the dialogue favourably with the Short Sun books.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: The Wizard Knight is the anti-Connecticut Yankee

...

An exceptional YA reader, I'd say.? People who could have followed TWK in their mid teens are undoubtedly overrepresented here.

In fact, I'd say it's the anti-Connecticut-Yankee.? I don't remember any way in which Able's modern knowledge, skills, or values help him in the medieval-ish world (but will be happy to be corrected).? I imagine Wolfe was making that point intentionally.

I've mentioned my problems with it before, and others here have said they don't like it because they don't like Able.? On the plus side, Wolfe seems to have solved his problem with weird dialogue that one of the worst features of the /Short Sun/ is.? H'if yer see what Jerry means, bucky.

On the whole, I liked it.? I don't know whether I liked it or the /Soldier/ books better.

Jerry Friedman

2011-12-07 Lee Berman replying to Larry Miller #

Supports Larry Miller's point, quoting Jerry Friedman, that Able's modern knowledge does not help him in the medieval-ish world, an anti-Connecticut Yankee contrast Wolfe intends. Cites Wolfe's interviews to argue that ancient peoples understood the world as well as we do, so our real advantage is Christ rather than advanced technology.
supports · theology-and-religion · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe intends ancient peoples to be as intelligent as moderns; Christ, not technology, is the modern advantage

I agree. Judging from interviews, this may be one of the major points Wolfe hopes to convey with his work- that ancient peoples were just as intelligent and capable of understanding the world as we are. If we have an advantage over them, it is not so much that we have advanced technology but that we have Christ.
