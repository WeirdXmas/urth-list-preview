The thread: Two posters disputed fan-written stories that fill gaps in Wolfe's Fifth Head of Cerberus novellas. Alice Turner argued such invented stories replace Wolfe's text, mocked the dispute with humour, and pointed to a Dorothy Parker verse as a source for Wolfe's lines. Tony Ellis argued that readers writing their own stories into gaps find connections never intended, citing Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead, and questioned whether the Parker verse was what Wolfe meant. Ranjit Bhatnagar supported Ellis, adding Urth of the New Sun and Dan Simmons's Endymion books as examples of continuations that can be made to look planned. The thread ends without resolution; the posts stay split between Turner's objection and the defence of gap-filling.
  • Alice Turner: gap-filling fan fiction replaces Wolfe's text and is a mock-worthy distraction
  • Tony Ellis: readers' gap-filling finds unintended connections, as in Stoppard
  • Ranjit Bhatnagar: continuations like Urth of the New Sun and Simmons's Endymion can be made to look planned

Themes: Stories within stories, Books, libraries and writing, Humour in Wolfe

1998-06-24 Alice Turner #

Argues that the disputes between two posters about the Fifth Head of Cerberus novellas are mere invented fan fiction that replaces Wolfe's text, and mocks them with humour. Points to a Dorothy Parker verse, "And I am Marie of Rumania," as a light-verse source for Wolfe's own lines and asks readers to check it.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Wolfe alludes to Dorothy Parker's Marie of Rumania verse

With due respect, Sarge, sir, this becomes a ridiculous argument, similar to that of science-meister scolex. The two of you are replacing Wolfe's text with text of your own, yours involving an elaborate background for Marsch and his for Aunt Jeanine. There a word for that, and it isn't analysis, it's fan fiction (okay, two words).

Mud-colored. And I must warn you, sir, that fraternization between ranks is inappropriate behavior.

I think the poem everyone "almost" remembers is the one by Dorothy Parker which ends "And I am Marie of Rumania." Alas, I can't find it in my two light verse collections which both lean heavily on dead Brits, but it's not bad. It puts in verse that fairly standard expression of scorn that goes more or less" "Well, if he's a successful you-name-it, I'm the Queen of England!" (Funnier from a male, and goes back at least to Gilbert & Sullivan.) Maybe one of us has a more American light verse collection, or a Parker collection, and can look it up. It's pretty well known. Wolfe is, I think, just doing his own verse in the tradition.

1998-06-25 Tony Ellis #

Argues that readers who write their own stories into gaps in Wolfe's text find connections that were never intended, using Stoppard's Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead as an example. Responds to Alga's objection by quoting a Dorothy Parker verse found in a book of quotations and doubting it is what Wolfe meant.
disputes · source-and-allusion

Absolutely. Once you start writing your own story into the gaps in someone else's, it's really not that hard to find connections suggesting that it was there all along. "Rosencrantz and Guildstern Are Dead" is a classic example of what I mean.

This live Brit has found it in his book of quotations, but unless this is just an extract I'm not convinced it's what Wolfe had in mind:

Comment

Oh, life is a glorious cycle of song,
A medley of extemporanea;
And love is a thing that can never go wrong; And I am Marie of Rumania.

1998-06-25 Ranjit Bhatnagar #

Responds to Tony Ellis's remark about writing one's own story into gaps in another's work by offering further examples, namely Urth of the New Sun and Dan Simmons's Endymion books, which the poster says rewrite Hyperion and Fall of Hyperion. Grounds the point in the claim that such sequels or continuations can be made to look as if they were always planned.
