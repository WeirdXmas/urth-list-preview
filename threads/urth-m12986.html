The Politics of Gene Wolfe

Urth list · 25 messages · 2010-07-03 to 2010-07-13

2010urthLee BermanJosh GellerAdam ThorntonDavid StockhoffStuart HammJeff WilsonRob Thornton
The thread: Lee Berman opened the thread by arguing the Second Amendment's militia clause limits the right to bear arms, and Jeff Wilson and David Stockhoff pressed debates over whether 'the right of the people' is collective or individual and whether militias were town watches or Indian-war forces. Wilson leaned toward an individual reading and cited Ruby Ridge and lynching, while Stockhoff argued for a collective, militia-based reading. Brunians then introduced blunt libertarian claims and disputed Stockhoff's account of the Founders' fears about armed slaves. The debate stalled into personal sparring and never returned to Wolfe's books, and the late posts from Robert Thornton and brunians complained about exactly that drift.
  • Lee Berman: Second Amendment limits arms to regulated militias
  • Jeff Wilson: 'the people' and 'free State' support an individual right and anti-fascist defense
  • David Stockhoff: the Amendment protects a collective, militia-based right, not self-defense
  • brunians: arming defense is for free men, and the past was not peaceful
  • Robert Thornton: list should stick to Wolfe, not personal belief

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Justice, torture and mercy, War and soldiers

2010-07-03 Lee Berman #

Argues that the Second Amendment was meant to allow regulated militias rather than personal self-defense, and asks why the text names only militias. Replies to an unnamed poster's rebuttal, citing Ruby Ridge and Waco and admitting personal bias, then closes by declining further discussion and joking about A.E. Van Vogt.
chats

Those are reasonable arguments. I have nothing against them. It just appears to me that the purpose of the Second Amendment was to allow regulated militias to function. This has little to do with how the Second Amendment is usually invoked today. Why didn't the Founding Fathers mention self-defense, hunting and defense from fascist governments in that amendment? Why only militias? And are there now legal militias which rely on self-owned weapons? In all militias I know of,you must use a government owned weapon, yes? A militia armed solely with self-owned weapons would tend to get the Ruby Ridge/Waco treatment, I think.

I have never felt a need for personal ownership of guns so I'm sure that fact biases my opinion of the Second Amendment, just as those who enjoy the ownership of guns would be similarly biased in the opposite direction. It is an interesting topic but not one I will waste more of this board's space on. I can only invoke A.E. Van Vogt so many times ;-).

2010-07-04 Jeff Wilson replying to David Stockhoff #

Questions whether the Second Amendment's phrase "the right of the people" means a collective rather than individual right, pointing out that the Fourth Amendment uses identical wording for persons, houses and papers. Argues that "person" in the founders' idiom meant the individual's physical presence, and that Wolfe, being historically informed, would read the Constitution as a document of its time.
disputes · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion

I'm not sure how much of this is humor or rhetoric.

I'm not sure how you distinguish between persons and People. The 4th amendment, introduced at the same time on the same piece of paper, uses the identical wording "the right of the people". Does this mean that persons, houses, papers, and effects are also held under government regulation as a collective rather than an individual right? Of course, in the idiom of the time, "person" had a much stronger connotation than today of the individual's objective physical presence, their physical body and the artifacts and space immediately proximate to it. This would make it a poorer term of reference for establishing abstract, inalienable rights that endure even if the "person" had business physically far afield.

I'm pretty sure that Wolfe would agree that the People is made of individuals, and while a state can be a sovereign person and not their subjects, a literally democratic state is made of individual persons. Given his prodigious, endlessly elaborate convolutions of notional languages and expressions historic and posthistoric, one can expect him to be more than sufficiently informed to take the Constitution as it was intended to be read in its time, as a document of its time.

2010-07-04 David Stockhoff replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the Second Amendment's phrase 'the People' refers to an organized militia built on long-gun ownership, so individual handgun rights are hard to derive from it. Responds to David Stockhoff's question about whether 'the right of the people' in the Fourth Amendment likewise means collective rights, offering a militia-based reading of the text rather than engaging Wolfe's books.
disputes

Below.

Neither. Just the facts.
I agree that it is a tricky question and one central to any serious debate about the 2A. But the People plainly are thought to defend themselves with an organized militia based on universal individual long-gun ownership, because that's what a militia is. (I suppose a militia armed with pistols could be effective if mounted, but hardly otherwise. And such could keep order against minor disruptions, just as cops with .38s can. But there is probably no precedent for an all-handgun militia, because generally handguns have been more controlled than rifles. With good reason.)

Any reading regarding individuals acting outside of an organized group, or regarding either pistols or machine guns, or defense against muggers and burglars, has to connect with this basic idea or fail to ground itself in the 2A. Thus, it's hard to see how it spells out my right to carry a concealed pocket revolver in a club at 2:00 AM in a city whose residents fear handguns enough to collectively decide to ban them, let alone my right to have several machine gun nests positioned around my house. But it certainly lets me defend my property on my own as I see fit, and that's where I think most Americans do overwhelmingly agree. It's just too bad that handguns kill more handgun owners and their children than they do burglars.

Although one could argue that absent any police force or guard at all, either of these "rights" may suddenly become useful. And that's what SF is good for---exploring such situations. Zombies, for example. Don't take that argument to the SC, though.

2010-07-04 Jeff Wilson replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that slow police response today stems from perverse incentives, not only limited force, and calls this a systems failure that Wolfe's characters remark on. Replies to David Stockhoff by agreeing on the earlier fascism point, then citing historical lynching and mob justice before and after the Revolutionary War as context.
disputes

I'd agree on texbook fascism then, but lynching and armed race violence go back to biblical times. There was also no shortage of mob justice and score-settling under the pretext of political differences before during and after the Revolutionary War.

That's just what they did not have; there was nothing like the modern, full-time municipal police force for the great majority of people then. The context is different today, thank god, but one still cannot count on immediate police response in general, chiefly because they have finite resources, to make demands on their time is easy, convenient, and subsidized, and there are signficant legal, financial, and political consequences to the police for responding and acting in way later judged improper, but essentially none for their failure to respond in a timely fashion or all.

To maintain thread relevance, this is the sort of lamentable systems failure from perverse incentives so many of Wolfe's characters remark upon.

2010-07-04 David Stockhoff replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that mob violence and race violence are ancient but worsened in America around 1900, and that eighteenth-century militias were more about Indian raids than town watches. Responds to Jeff Wilson's quoted remark on fascism, lynching and the Revolutionary-era justice, offering historical context rather than any reading of Wolfe's fiction.
disputes

Half the history of the human race is the history of mob violence. But American race violence worsened through the turn of the 20th century. That's not to say it wasn't an issue earlier. Protecting your town against attackers from another town, as happened in the most notorious riots, would certainly call for a militia, but in the late 1700s, Indian "raids" were probably more on peoples' minds.

The towns had watches; the 2A was probably not primarily meant to support them.
