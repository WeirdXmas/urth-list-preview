The thread: Michael Straight asked for a plot reminder of the novella Forlesen and wondered whether Gene Wolfe invented its title word. Derek Bell and Craig Christensen supplied plot recaps, with Bell comparing the bureaucratic authority to Kafka and the situation to Heidegger's thrownness and Christensen comparing it to Brazil. Christensen quoted an 1913 Webster's entry for the archaic word "forlese" meaning to lose utterly, and Alice Turner traced it to Old Frisian and Beowulf, tying it to forlorn and ruin. The thread ended with the word's meaning established and no interpretation of Wolfe's use offered.

1998-06-24 Derek Bell #

Summarizes the novella Forlesen for a reader who has forgotten it, describing a man who wakes in a house, meets a wife, consults life manuals, and goes to work in a short life. Compares its bureaucratic authority to Kafka and the protagonist's situation to Heidegger's thrownness of being.
informs · genre-and-craft

It's about a guy who wakes up in a house, meets a woman who is his wife, glances through the manuals for his life, goes to work and his whole life is short, over before he can take control of it. It's been described as Kafkaesque, which is appropriate, though the authority in question is the one that supplies the "manuals" (for want of a better word) and makes the rules. Oh, the main characters' job seems particularly strange and pointless, with (other) people showing great enthusiasm for something that isn't quite clear. (It's either a course in motivation, a course in managment, a game or all three.)

It reminds me of Heideggers' "throwness of being".

Derek

1998-06-25 Craig Christensen #

Summarizes the plot of the novella Forlesen for a reader who has forgotten it: a man wakes with no memory, is hurried through a drab workday by his wife and coworkers, and slowly recovers fragments of his past. Responds to Michael's request for a brief reminder, offering a plot recap, a reading of the name as forlorn, and a comparison to the film Brazil.
informs · close-textual-reading · theories: Forlesen is dislocated from a real past

Forlesen is one of my favorite Wolfe stories. The main character's, in fact most or the characters' lives, seem to be condensed into one long, drab, pointless day. Forlesen wakes one morning a blank slate. Some memories slowly return. He recalls that people cover themselves with clothing. He remembers language. Before he can remember more his wife begins to rush him off to work. She points out a stack of manuals which explain his responsibilities. He only has time to scan them.

His wife can barely contain her terror. She woke before him and had time to read through her manuals, one of which told her that if her husband doesn't go to work, she and the children will starve. After she finally pressures him to leave, he hears the door lock firmly behind him.

I believe that 'Forlesen' is a name invented by Wolfe to invoke the idea of someone forlorn or lost. I had the feeling that Forlesen was dislocated from a real past elsewhere. He seems to know that life isn't supposed to be like this. I thought that if he had more time to reflect he might remember more of his real past, but he is hurried from one station in his life to the next by his wife and coworkers. These people, themselves, seem to be as lost and befuddled as he is. They are obviously bluffing their way along and after Forlesen begins to imitate them his 'progress' at work becomes streamlined.

There is more to the story and there a couple of subplots. On the surface, it seemed to be a sketch of life in the industrial workplace. It reminded me of the movie 'Brazil'. The part that confused me the most was during Forlesen's drive to work. Can anyone tell me what he saw holding up the elevated road? I particularly enjoyed the scene with the patrolman here.

1998-06-25 Alice Turner #

Explains that the word Forlesen is an old term rather than an invention, tracing it to Old Frisian and Beowulf and linking its sense to forlorn, with connotations of loss, perdition, ruin and destruction. Responds to Rostrum's question about whether Wolfe made the word up, reporting an OED citation as evidence without quoting it.
informs · source-and-allusion

Rostrum,

Don't be disillusioned! It's a very old word, with an impressive (and apt) OED citation. which I won't quote. It's related to "forlorn" with implications of loss, perdition, ruin, shame, destruction. It goes back to Old Frisian, and to Beowulf. I'm impressed!

-alga-
