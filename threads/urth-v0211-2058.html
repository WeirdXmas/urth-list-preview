Pas's Two Heads

Urth list · 4 messages · 2002-11-08 to 2002-11-08

2002urthThe Book of the New SunThe Book of the Long SunDan'l Danehy-OakesRoy C. LackeyJames Jordan
The thread: Readers debated why Pas in The Book of the Long Sun has two heads, and whether the answer lies in the scanning of Typhon's brain or in deliberate symbolism. James Jordan argued the two heads may be a divine image drawn from Janus and the double-headed Russian eagle, and that the second head may be Piaton's, while noting the text is insufficient to settle it. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes held that the scan captured only one brain, so the form must be a self-image or chosen image Typhon made, perhaps linked to Piaton, and that Typhon scanned himself before launch. Roy Lackey backed the scanning objection, arguing Typhon's congenital blindness limits what the scan holds and that Piaton was superfluous. Jordan then disputed the monstrous reading, using Roman religion and the Whorl-as-Roman-empire idea. The thread stayed at impasse, with the disagreement left open.
  • James Jordan: two heads a deliberate divine image, Janus and Russian eagle, second head possibly Piaton's
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: scan had one brain, so the two-headed form is Typhon's chosen self-image, perhaps linked to Piaton
  • Roy C. Lackey: Typhon's blindness limits the scan, Piaton superfluous, Severian reached for sword rather than worship
  • James Jordan (disputing): two-headed gods were not monstrous in Roman religion, Whorl mirrors Rome

Themes: Gods, angels and the Increate, Robots, androids and artificial life, Myth and folklore sources

2002-11-08 James Jordan #

Proposes that Pas's two heads may reflect a deliberate two-headed divine image, citing Roman Janus and the double-headed Russian eagle, and that the second head may be Piaton's rather than Typhon's. Responds to Roy's claim about Typhon's scanning and grafting, concluding the information is insufficient to decide the question.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Pas's second head is Piaton's; Pas's two heads reflect a deliberate divine image

Maybe. Maybe not. Janus of Rome was two-headed, and thereby ruled the past and the future. Typhon may well have wanted a two-headed image for Pas, so that he was visibly greater than other gods. Think also of the double-headed eagle of Russia, and similar images. We don't know that Pas's two heads looked like Typhon and Piaton. They may have both looked like Typhon. Of course, on the other hand, the reason Pas is not as bad as Typhon may be because the second head is Piaton's, and Piaton's nature has become highly influential in Pas. I don't think we have the information to decide about this.

Nutria

2002-11-08 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Pas's two heads in The Book of the Long Sun are not explained by the Gods of Mainframe's appearance being copied from scanned bodies, since the scan captured only one brain. Reasons that the form is a self-image or chosen image Typhon made, perhaps linked to the Piaton body, and that Typhon would have scanned himself just before the Whorl's launch.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Pas's two heads are a self-image chosen by Typhon; Typhon scanned himself just before the Whorl launched

There is nothing in the books which seems to me to suggest that the appearance of the Gods of Mainframe is necessarily based upon the appearance of the bodies in which they resode when they were scanned -- it seems to be more a matter of either self-image or a chosen image. (Does Tartaros manifest visibly?)

The "scan" seems to be a sort of brain-snapshot, or perhaps soul-snapshot. So even if it was taken when Typhon was
grafted onto Piaton, it would only be a scan of the one
brain.

It is not at all clear to me why Pas has two heads, but it seems clear that this is a choice Typhon made, either
consciously or un-. It might have some reference to the
Piaton body; it seems likely to me that he would want to scan himself at the last possible moment before the launch of the _Whorl_, to preserve as much of his self as possible.

--Blattid

2002-11-08 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Typhon's blindness from birth limits what his scan in Mainframe can contain, so Tartaros cannot see, and that Piaton was superfluous and unlikely to be scanned. Supports the point by citing what Typhon told Auk and Severian's reaction on first seeing the two-headed Typhon, which was to reach for his sword rather than worship him.
supports · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Tartaros cannot see because Typhon was born blind; Piaton was unlikely to have been scanned

No, he doesn't.

Having been blind from birth, the scan of Tartaros is limited by the biological abilities of the brain he was born with. Knowledge he acquired after birth becomes a part of his personality, or whatever you want to call it that gets scanned, but he can't learn to see, not even digitally, because the requisite areas of his brain were never developed, and no amount of computer-enhanced tinkering can change that. At least that is the essence of what he told Auk.

I agree that Piaton's brain was unlikely to have been scanned. The only reason Typhon kept Piaton around at all was to handle biological functions that Typhon's brain hadn't yet learned to assume. Typhon had no body to worry about in Mainframe, so Piaton was entirely superfluous.

As regards two-heads-being-better-than-one for inspiring or enhancing religious awe, as Nutria suggests, I must have been born lacking that gene. <g> A two-headed man is a freak, like a two-headed calf; the idea of worshiping such a thing would never cross my mind. Sev's thoughts on first seeing Typhon were:

"A man, larger than I and far broader of shoulder, stepped from between the foot of a cataphract, and it was as though one of the monstrous constellations of the night sky had fallen to Urth and clothed itself in the flesh of humankind. For the man had two heads, like an ogre in some forgotten tale in _The Wonders of Urth and Sky_.
"Instinctively, I put my hand on the sword hilt at my shoulder."

Sev's gut reaction was not to fall to his knees, but to grab a weapon. And the more Sev found out about him, the less he liked him. Sev may have feared the power of the monster, but that didn't stop him from killing it, having first refused, specifically and emphatically, to worship it.

-Roy

2002-11-08 James Jordan #

Argues that Typhon's two heads should be read through Roman religion, since two-headed gods such as Janus were not seen as monstrous there, and that Wolfe's Whorl mirrors the Roman empire. Responds to a post that called a two-headed man a freak, offering the Janus comparison as a counter to Severian's ogre-like impression of Typhon.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Typhon's two heads reflect Roman Janus; Whorl mainframe gods parallel Roman gods

But then, why would Typhon make himself look like an ogre? My point was that two-headed gods are not unknown, especially in Rome, and Rome is a big deal for Wolfe. In the symbolism of the Whorl novels, the mainframe gods are like the Roman ones, the Whorl like the Roman empire, and Viron like Israel at the time of Jesus. So a two-headed Roman god like Janus makes sense. The Romans did not view Janus as an ogre, however Severian might have looked upon him.

FWIW

JBJordan
