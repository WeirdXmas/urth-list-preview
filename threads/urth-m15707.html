The thread: The thread began with Lee Berman's claim that the Shadow Children in The Long Sun are a native shape-changing people of Ste. Anne rather than degenerate colonists, a reading Gerry Quinn endorsed for The Fifth Head of Cerberus as the straightforward interpretation. The larger debate concerned the name of the home world in Wolfe's fiction: David Stockhoff argued that Philly is a literary allusion rather than a real place, while Jeff Wilson and Lee Berman argued that the Country of Friends refers to Texas, citing its state motto and Feather Tigers. Mo Holkar offered fay-myth and African analogues, and Mr Thalassocrat floated a half-remembered Greek etymology for Philly. The thread drifted into speculation and ended without resolution, with one brief aside about the inhumi.
  • Lee Berman: Shadow Children are a native shape-changing people of Ste. Anne, not degenerate colonists
  • Gerry Quinn: Shadow Children are degenerate human discoverers who grew confused about their nature
  • David Stockhoff: Philly is a literary allusion rather than a real place name
  • Jeff Wilson: Country of Friends refers to Texas, a colonized land
  • Mo Holkar: home worlds may be fay-myth analogues, with Africa as a land of wonders
  • Mr Thalassocrat: Philly may derive from Greek philai meaning friends, offered as a half-remembered aside

Themes: Colonization and the native, Myth and folklore sources, Names and their meanings

As Old Wise One speaks to Sandwalker in the pit:

"When we came, we looked as you do now--...You come from a race of shape-changers-- like those we called werewolves in our old home. But then there are doubts. The abos are all of human form and behavior. It is the Shadow Children who are weird looking and seem indeterminate in their number and solidity. You never know how many of them there are or or whether they will be tangible or ethereal.

2010-09-06 Jeff Wilson replying to Lee Berman #

Proposes that the phrase "Country of Friends" refers to Texas, whose name is said to come from an indigenous word for friends, and reads the place as a colonized land. Responds to Lee Berman's list of possible Earthly homelands by citing Wolfe's Texas boyhood, the Texas space program, and Coronado's El Dorado quest, where he called the buffalo-filled land useless.
proposes · source-and-allusion · author-and-biography · theories: Country of Friends refers to Texas

The "Country of Friends" is almost certainly a reference to Texas, named after the the indigenous word for "friends". Texas is quadruply appropriate as the boyhood home state of Gene Wolfe, as an assimilated colony, as the center of the American space program, and as part of the area where Coronado encountered similar deception and duplicity in his quest for El Dorado.

It's also worth knowing that Coronado wrote that he considered the land to be useless, while standing amidst the undepleted buffalo herds, shortly to be "changed into" a new way of life for the plains indians and then into wealth for the colonizing Europeans.

2010-09-06 Mo Holkar / UKG replying to Lee Berman #

Proposes that Africa's classical image as a land of wonders fits the fantastical homelands suggested for the Shadow Children, which may be analogues of fay myths. Responds to Lee Berman's remark on Africa and Poictesme by citing Anderson's The Queen of Air and Darkness and recommending Cabell's Jurgen as an out-of-copyright read.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Shadow Children are pre-human fay-analogues; Galaxy-wide settlement of fay-analogues before humans

In Classical times Africa was seen as a land of mythic wonders, hence "ex Africa semper aliquid novi". So I think this fits into the fantastical pattern of the other suggested homelands.

I take all this to be flagging the Shadow Children up as analogous to the various fay myths that humans have cherished on Earth. Perhaps Wolfe is hinting at a galaxy-wide settlement of fay-analogues before humans came along -- like in Poul Anderson's 'The Queen of Air and Darkness', if I'm remembering that correctly.

Sad to reflect that Cabell is now fairly described as "somewhat obscure". He was hugely influential on a wide range of SF & F authors, and I think much of his work is still very worthwhile and interesting. Lots of it can be read on Project Gutenberg, as it's out of copyright -- I recommend starting with Jurgen:
http://www.gutenberg.org/etext/8771

Mo
