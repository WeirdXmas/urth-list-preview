Points out a quotation from a McCaffery interview in which Wolfe says that letting someone hit you does nothing to stop violence, reading it as an explicit rejection of a memorable passage in an influential book. Takes the quote as a sign of Wolfe's own stance against a passive, non-resisting response to violence, framed as a contrast with the source text he is reacting to.

I was reading the recently-posted Larry McCaffery interview, in which the mystery of 5HC is "given away" (http://www.depauw.edu/sfs/ interviews/wolfe46interview.htm)

There's the following quotation in it too:

"The fact that you stand there and let someone hit you in the face doesn't do anything to eliminate violence (it may even contribute to further violence) ?which is one of the underlying themes in The Book of the New Sun."

That seems a very explicit repudiation of one of the more memorable passages in a book that's generally considered to be a pretty huge influence on Wolfe's work generally and BotNS in particular.

Adam

2006-09-07 Matthew King replying to Adam Thornton #

Argues that Wolfe's remark about turning the other cheek should not be read as repudiating Matthew 5:39, since that instruction was written for a narrow group in a specific context. Supports this by quoting Malina and Rohrbaugh's social-science commentary on honor and shame, which reads the antitheses as a way out of feuding in a public, honor-based world.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Matthew 5:39 is context-bound, not universal

I take this to be a reference to Matthew 5:39 (and parallel passages), the famous "turn the other cheek" instruction.

Wolfe's statement above could be seen as a repudiation of Mt 5:39, if you assume that the scope of the instruction is universal, that it applies to all men at all times. I think this is a dangerous assumption to make about a passage in a document that was written for a specific and narrow group of men. GW has shown in his work a deep consideration for narrative context, and I think it unlikely that he would give the New Testament documents any less thought.

I've been reading Malina and Rohrbaugh's _Social-Science Commentary on the Synoptics_ lately, in which they apply the sociological research of the Context Group to Matthew, Mark, and Luke. Valuable reading, though I don't find it wholly convincing. Here's a bit of their take on Matthew 5:39

---
What the scenes described in these antitheses offer is a way out of the honor-shame impasse that requires taking satisfaction. . . . [They] all describe having one's entitlements infringed on in a humiliating way: being struck on the right cheek by a backhand slap is an insult, as humiliating as being successfully sued in court or being forced to carry military gear for a mile. All such humiliating behavior required defense of one's honor. The advice given is to put up with the dishonor and cede what one was entitled to! The key to imagining the scenarios here is to realize that all of them presume an audience. In the Mediterranean world no one fights in public without others intervening to break it up. . . . Should someone be publicly insulted, the bystanders are sure to intervene. The real question raised by the image here is whether an insulted person should seek to defend his own honor or let another person defend him. Allowing others to come to one's defense enables one to be reconciled later with the one who dishonored and not proceed to a demand for satisfaction and feuding.
---

2006-09-08 Joel and Rachel Wilhelm replying to Adam Thornton #

Argues that Severian's violent acts in The Urth of the New Sun do not contradict Christian teaching, since the Church holds that people are saved by God's grace rather than moral perfection. Responds to Adam Thornton's quoted claim that Severian's arrest scene and killing of the prison governess are disturbing for a Christian reader, citing the biblical example of David as a flawed figure used by the Divine.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Severian is a flawed figure used by the Divine

The central message of the Church is that man is saved by grace from God - not due to moral perfection. Wolfe has deeply flawed characters who nevertheless are used by the Divine - not unlike Biblical characters (David for example). I see no contradiction.

Joel W.

2006-09-09 Daniel D Jones replying to thalassocrat #

Argues that Severian should not be read as a direct parallel to Jesus, since he is a messiah figure with many other themes and is not a perfect model as Christ was. Responds to Adam Thornton's quotation from the McCaffery interview about nonviolence by saying critiques of Christ's actions drawn from Severian's conduct miss the mark.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Severian is a messiah figure but not a Christ parallel

I think seeing Sev as a direct parallel to Jesus is a mistake. Certainly, he is in some sense a Messiah figure but I think that's he's both more and less. More, in that there are a great many other themes and issues being addressed than solely a Messiah, and less in that Sev certainly isn't in any sense a perfect representative as Christ was supposed to be. If you're a Christian, you're supposed to use Christ's actions as a model on which to base your own. It's become a trite phrase but that's exactly what WWJD means. I don't think Wolfe ever intended anyone to ask WWSD, so trying to draw critiques of Christ's actions from Sev's actions is, I think, missing the mark.
