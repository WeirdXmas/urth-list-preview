Oh That Hy!

Whorl list · 4 messages · 1998-11-02 to 1998-11-02

1998whorlThe Book of the Long SunAlice TurnerAlex David GroceMichael Straight
The thread: Readers debated whether Hy in The Book of the Long Sun is a flawed bride in an allegory of the church and Christ, with Mary Magdalene imagery attached. Alice Turner first called Hy a worldly, beloved bride and hoped Wolfe would not leave the familiar cast behind, then later pressed a Christ-like reading of Silk through Dostoevsky and critics. Michael Straight agreed that Hy is a blatant parallel to Christ and that the bride figure is also troubled, while Alex David Groce linked Hyacinth's marriage to the allegory, floated her as a Trivigaunti spy leader in Short Sun, and offered an Eliot Waste Land echo he admitted might be coincidence. The thread ended without resolution, with the allegorical reading broadly accepted but its details left open.
  • Alice Turner: Hy is a devious, untrustworthy figure who will matter later, and the church-bride allegory reading is a bit much
  • Michael Straight: Hy is a flawed bride of Christ paralleling Silk's lame foot, death, and resurrection
  • Alex David Groce: Hyacinth's marriage fits the flawed bride allegory and she may head the Trivigaunti spies; Waste Land echo may be coincidence
  • Alice Turner: a New York Review of Books letter supports reading Silk as a Christ-like figure, citing Dostoevsky and Leys

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Women, sex and gender

1998-11-02 Alice Turner #

Argues that Hy is a devious, untrustworthy character who will be important later, and hopes Wolfe does not skip decades ahead and leave the familiar cast behind. Responds to Alice Turner's reading of Hy as the worldly, loved bride in an allegory of the church and Mary Magdalene imagery, calling that interpretation a bit much.
disputes · allegory-and-typology

My goodness, that seems a bit much. My own opinion simply is that Hy is No Better Than She Should Be and also Big Trouble, and that we haven't heard the last of her--she's much too devious and useful a character to abandon (alga crosses her cilia and hopes).

This is so (both the change and the murkiness). That's why I'm hoping very much that Wolfe doesn't simply pitch us forward by decades, leaving our old friends in the past. That would be sadistic.

1998-11-02 Michael Straight #

Agrees that Hy in The Book of the Long Sun reads as a flawed bride of Christ, a blatant allegorical parallel alongside Silk's lame foot and his death and resurrection. Responds to Alice Turner's reading of Hy as the worldly beloved tied to Mary Magdalene imagery and the church-as-bride allegory, while adding that the bride figure is also deeply troubled.
supports · allegory-and-typology · theories: Hy is a flawed bride of Christ

I don't know. Even I who reject a lot of the allegorical interpretations that get passed around on this list thought that the "flawed bride of Christ" stuff was as blatant as Silk having a lame foot, his "death" and resurrection.

Of course, I think the Bride of Christ is also Big Trouble and maybe even No Better Than She Should Be.

-Rostrum

1998-11-02 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Hyacinth's marriage to Silk fits the allegory of the flawed bride of Christ, and that Hy may head the Trivigaunti spies in Short Sun. Also suggests her name echoes the Hyacinth girl in Eliot's The Waste Land, linking to Eliot's troubled marriage, while admitting the link may be coincidence.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · source-and-allusion · theories: Hy as head of the Trivigaunti spies; Hyacinth named after Eliot's Hyacinth girl

Which seems to be a fair description of the church at various times, so it works very well on both the self-sacrificing God-like love and the "silly nice priest falls for treacherous but hot lady..." As to the second Enlightenment after the honeymoon, it seems to imply that the Outsider approves of Silk wedding Hy (by the way, who else thinks that ONLY Wolfe would have a massive four-volume series and put a double wedding involving the four major characters offstage!). Thus giving the allegory a big boost.
I'm still betting on Hy as head of the Trivigaunti spies, as well. Although how important (if it's true) that will be in Short Sun (assuming she shows up) I don't know.
Another thing, that just struck me today--I suspect this is pure coincidence, but doesn't Hyacinth actually work fairly well as being named after the Hyacinth girl in "The Waste Land?" Thus tying to Vivian Eliot, another marriage that was lots of trouble (at least Silk can be sure Hy won't be sleeping with Bertrand Russell, unless things get very odd...) --

1998-11-02 Alice Turner #

Argues that a New York Review of Books letter supports the theological reading of Silk as a Christ-like figure, by quoting Dostoevsky's remarks on a positively beautiful man and Christ as Don Quixote. Grounds this in Leys's essay on Quixote and Lev Loseff's linking of Myshkin to Christ Ridiculous, and draws the parallel to Silk.
supports · theology-and-religion · source-and-allusion · theories: Silk is a Christ-like figure; Silk is a Christ Ridiculous figure like Myshkin

A letter I read today in the New York Review of Books can be seen as support for the theological reading of Silk. It responds to an essay by Simon Leys entitled "The Imitation of Our Lord Don Quixote" by quoting a letter from Doestoevsky to his niece about the novel he is writing:

"The main idea of the novel is to present a positively beautiful man. This is the most difficult subject in the world, especially as it is now....There is only one positively beautiful man in the world, Christ, and the phenomenon of this limitlessly, infinitely beautiful person is an infinite miracle in itself....I'd only mention that of all the beautiful individuals in Christian literature, one stands out as the most perfect, Don Quixote...Wherever compassion toward ridiculed and ingenious beauty is presented, the reader's sympathy is aroused. The mystery of humor lies in the excitation of compassion."

The novel D. was working on was, of course, -The Idiot-, which features Prince Myshkin (as this letter-writer, Lev Loseff, states) as his own variation on "Christ as Don Quixote" or "Christ Ridiculous." Leys responds generously and warmly, calling the association between Quixote and Myshkin "apposite and illuminating."

As I hope this post might be.

-alga
