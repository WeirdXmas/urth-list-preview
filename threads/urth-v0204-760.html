Human JPEGs

Urth list · 6 messages · 2002-04-23 to 2002-04-24

2002urthThe Book of the Long SunAndy RobertsonJason IngramNigel PriceDan MelizaMichael Straight
The thread: Jason Ingram asked why uploaded minds in the Long Sun books cannot be copied into several machines at once and why data leaves the mainframe during downloading, proposing a soul-like one-copy limit. Nigel Price answered that personality transfers are lossy compressions, which he called a "human JPEG", and argued Scylla moved into Oreb this way. Dan Meliza offered a hologram analogy in which gods upload fragments of themselves that recombine, applying it to Oreb as an interference pattern. Michael Straight then broadened the thread to a general claim that people explain the unknown through what they build, without reference to a specific passage. The thread moved from a textual question to general theory rather than reaching agreement on the Whorl mechanics.
  • Jason Ingram: one-copy limit like a soul, so minds should not duplicate across machines
  • Nigel Price: transfers are lossy compression (human JPEG), Scylla moved into Oreb
  • Dan Meliza: gods upload fragments that recombine, like holograms; Oreb is an interference pattern
  • Michael Straight: people understand what they make best and explain the unknown through it

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Ghosts, dreams and visions, Names and their meanings

2002-04-23 Jason Ingram #

Asks why uploaded minds in the Long Sun books cannot be copied into several machines at once, and why the data always leaves the mainframe during downloading. Proposes a soul-like one-copy limit, notes that Typhon and Silk keep their identities, and contrasts this with Severian's serial versions and the Short Sun's bilocation.
questions · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Uploaded minds limited to one copy by analogy to soul

One question that has perplexed me is why multiple copies couldn't have been 'downloaded' at the same time, and why all the 'data' seems to have left mainframe whenever downloading occurs. I can think of reasons why the narrative might demand this, but I'm not sure what the logic is.

One possibility: there is a 'one copy per person' limit because of a rough analogy to a soul (not identity, since Typhon and others seem to retain their personality even after uploading; and Silk remains 'in' Silk, presumably, even as Pig contains a downloaded Silk. The timing of the last part might warrant some scrutiny, however).

If it's just data, why not have three or four or four hundred versions at the same time? Perhaps Typhon wouldn't have trusted himself; maybe it's hypothetically possible, and an innate desire to think ourselves unique prevented duplication; but I seem to remember hints that the data were irreplacable . . . no backups possible.

Strange, considering Severian--or perhaps a purposeful contrast. Serial versions of the new sun, single duplicates in the long sun, bilocation and identity sharing in the short sun.

Of course, maybe I've gotten my facts wrong again.

2002-04-23 Nigel Price #

Proposes that personality copies transferred between windows, people and night choughs in the Whorl are compressed, using a lossy algorithm that keeps a convincing likeness, which he calls a "human JPEG" rather than a bitmap. Builds the idea on Dan Meliza's earlier speculation and a playful pun on "two byte word" as Lupine wordplay, arguing Scylla transferred herself into Oreb this way.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Scylla's personality was lossy-compressed into Oreb like a JPEG

All I wanted to do originally with this thread was point out the possibility of Lupine wordplay around the idea of "two byte word"/"two word bites"/"two word bird", but then I was foolish enough to put down that idle bit of nonsense about the 48K Spectrum.

Having failed to learn my lesson, I have to confess that I too, like Dan, have been whimsically speculating to myself whether, in the Whorl, the copies of personalities which are transferred between windows and people and windows and night choughs might not in fact be radically compressed in some way, or use some sort of wonderfully super-advanced lossy algorithm which gives such an accurate approximation of a personality that you really don't miss the bits that aren't there. In other words, Scylla didn't unload a "bitmap" of herself into Oreb, it was more a sort of "human JPEG".

Only speaking metaphorically of course.

Nigel

2002-04-23 Dan Meliza #

Proposes that the gods upload fragments of themselves into people and animals, which can later recombine into a full personality, using the hologram analogy: fragments show the same image at lower fidelity. Applies this to Oreb, described as an interference pattern between Scylla and Oreb proper in a bird's limited vocabulary, and to Scylla's varying admirability depending on who she occupies.
proposes · theology-and-religion · genre-and-craft · theories: Gods upload fragments of themselves into people and animals; Oreb is an interference pattern of Scylla and Oreb

The other possibility, I suppose, is that the gods upload (download, whatever) fragments of themselves into people and animals which can later be recombined into a full personality. In fact, fragments of holograms display the same image as the full hologram but at a lower fidelity. So any intelligence can serve as a substrate for personality transfer but in proportion to the size of the brain. What we see in Oreb is actually the interference pattern between Scylla and Oreb proper expressed in the limited vocabulary of a two-bite bird. As Nutria points out this is a fine metaphor for human interaction, a tidy attack on modernism's atomic individual; and it goes a long ways toward explaining why Scylla's personality is more or less admirable depending on who she's occupying.

never one to capitalize after a colon,
dan

2002-04-24 Michael Straight #

Argues that people understand the things they make better than any natural phenomenon, and so explain what they do not understand in terms of what they build. Responds to Andy Robertson's claim that humans understand themselves through machine parables by extending it, with no reference to Wolfe's books or a specific passage.
supports

I don't know of any natural phenomena we understand as well as the things we make ourselves. Naturally, we try to explain what we don't understand in terms of what we do.

-Rostrum
