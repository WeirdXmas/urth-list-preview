The thread: Readers on the TWK questions thread debated the origins and logic of Wolfe's giants in The Wizard Knight: why giant men and women live apart, why men prefer human women, and what mythic sources lie behind them. James Wynn read the separation and the men's attraction to humans through Norse myth, the Ymir maggot-born men, and an elephant analogy, and tied it to honor. Andy Robertson disputed that Norse sources support any separation of the sexes, while Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued the book draws on Celtic and Mediterranean material as well as Norse. Nathan Spears offered an emasculation explanation and harlekin linked the giantesses to undines and the parasites to Kulili and Ymir. Stilskin's Tolkien comparison got little follow-up. The thread ended without resolution, drifting between sources and in-world explanations.
  • James Wynn: separation and human attraction reflect honor and the Ymir maggot-born myth
  • Andy Robertson: Norse sources show no sexual separation among giants, so the Ymir link is weak
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the book draws on Celtic and Mediterranean material as well as Norse, including Kulilli
  • Nathan Spears: males feel emasculated by larger women, and Utgard's southern location explains it
  • harlekin: giantesses are linked to undines and deep-space origins, and parasites tie to Kulili and Ymir
  • stilskin: giants and Abel echo Tolkien's Ents, Entwives, and Gandalf-Aragorn

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Robots, androids and artificial life, Beasts and the animal in us

2005-01-04 James Wynn #

Argues that Idnn's marriage to King Gilling and Able's refusals show Wolfe's theme of honor as giving up what one wants for others, and explains the giants' separation of the sexes by analogy to elephants. Responds to James Wynn's remarks on Idnn, the giants' living arrangements, and the Angrborn men's attraction to human women, citing the Ymir myth of maggot-born men.
supports · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Idnn's marriage is an act of honor; Angrborn men desire humans because they are not giants; Angrborn customs parallel elephant herds

And that was Wolfe's point right? That honor and courage are more than just carrying a weapon against overwhelming odds or protecting your reputation -- although that might be part of the duties of honor too. It is about giving up what you would really want (including potentially a long life) for the benefit of others. When Able refuses to carry a sword when he knows it will go against him in being taken seriously as a knight, that is honor. When he rebuffs sex with willing women for his love for Disiri, that is honor. And when Idnn (although terrified) takes on the mantle of the duties of being King Gilling's wife -- guarding his life and well-being when it would be better for her if he died -- that is honor and that is why Valfather comes to visit her.

Male and female giants live apart just as male and female elephants live apart.

They accept her as her queen as a matter of honor. Furthermore, I don't think you can map human relations to the giants. The mothers don't love their sons. In fact, they are presumably contemptible to them. The male giants are attracted to human women because they ARE NOT giants.

It is taken from the myth of the death of Ymir. Men were formed from the maggots that formed on his corpse. So when Giants die their corpses are covered with creepy-crawlies.

~ Crush

2005-01-04 Nathan Spears replying to James Wynn #

Offers an explanation for why female giants live apart from males and why males prefer human women in The Wizard Knight, suggesting males feel emasculated by larger, stronger women. Grounds this in Utgard lying in the south of the giant kingdom where the north would be more desirable, and in the lack of grotesque description of giantesses compared with giants.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Angrborn males feel emasculated by larger women

I got the impression it was because the males felt emasculated by their women, who are larger and stronger than they are, and perhaps because the giantesses don't want them around. Notice that Utgard is in the southern part of the kingdom of the giants; for a species where colder is better, the northern land would be the most desirable.

I also noted that Wolfe didn't go out of his way to paint the giantesses as inhuman and grotesque the way he did the giants, and that Able refers to one as if she were attractive; I wonder if they don't look vastly different from the giants as well.

2005-01-04 harlekin replying to James Wynn #

Supports this by noting that Don observed Wolfe does not paint the giantesses as inhuman or grotesque as he does the giants, and that Able calls one attractive, suggesting they may look quite different.

2005-01-04 Andy Robertson replying to James Wynn #

Disputes James Wynn's claim that the separation of male and female giants in The Wizard Knight comes from the myth of Ymir's death, arguing that Norse sources show gods and giants cohabiting and giantesses attractive to gods. Notes the separation idea is absent from that tradition and says the source is unknown until he recalls it as hartshorn.
disputes · source-and-allusion

Odd, this. It is not from the Norse, unlike nearly very thing else in TWK.

There are plenty of stories of cohabitation between the gods and the giants, and the giantesses are usually seen as sexually attractive to the gods: but the idea that the giants and giantesses are separate species does not exist in that culture-stream. I don't know what its source is.

Ah, yes.
