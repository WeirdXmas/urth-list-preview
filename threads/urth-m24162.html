silk, the dancing toy, gods in the tunnels

Urth list · 21 messages · 2013-08-24 to 2013-08-27

2013urthThe Book of the Long SunThe Book of the New SunCraig BrewerAntónio Pedro MarquesGerry QuinnMarc AraminiDavid StockhoffFred KiescheDavid DuffyDaniel Petersen
The thread: Marc Aramini argued for a literal, coded reading of Wolfe: Silk is controlled by his mother Kypris working through the dancing toy, Hyacinth and Mamelta, the tunnel gods are real, and names and phrases like 'the smallest of those dead' carry hidden meaning. Gerry Quinn rejected that as a cipher reading that leaves characters no life, treating the gods as blasphemous wordplay and 'dead' as possibly 'left for dead.' Daniel Otto Jack Petersen backed Aramini on the Outsider's role, while António Pedro Marques found the pun childish and Silk unreliable. David Stockhoff and Aramini then turned to free will, comparing Severian and Silk. The thread drifted from the Whorl's gods into Aramini's wider complaints about academia and New Wave criticism, with no agreement reached.
  • Marc Aramini: symbols are literal and coded; Kypris controls Silk through the toy, Hyacinth and Mamelta; tunnel gods are real
  • Gerry Quinn: figures embody Woman, the tunnel gods are a blasphemous joke, and literal coding leaves characters no life
  • Daniel Otto Jack Petersen: clockwork imagery does not fix Silk; the Outsider frees him into responsive agency
  • António Pedro Marques: the dog-god pun is too childish to bear weight, and Silk's distrust of the Outsider is a pulp-style reveal
  • David Stockhoff: Severian is clockwork who becomes a god, and Silk may be freer in Mainframe than in his body
  • entonio: Wolfe may treat characters as vehicles for the telling more than readers would like, though without evidence

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Free will and choice

2013-08-24 Marc Aramini #

Argues that Wolfe's symbols are literal, so Silk is controlled by his mother Kypris, who works through the dancing toy, Hyacinth, Mamelta and the enlightenment voice, serving the Outsider. Grounds this in the Marsh/Marsch spelling, the gods in the tunnels, Silk's suicidal thoughts, and Mamelta being swallowed by the fish, which he reads as Kypris subsumed by something greater.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Kypris controls Silk through his mother's dancing toy; Silk's enlightenment is predetermined and serves the Outsider; Mamelta swallowed by fish symbolizes Kypris subsumed

By now everyone know I actually think Wolfe creates symbols and "metaphors" that are actually extremely literal.? Thus, Shadow Children riding Marshmen is the same as Shadow Children riding a man named Marsch (his name is misspelled in my copy as Marsh in the title "A Story by John V Marsh", which I feel might be intentional even if changed in later editions by copy editors). ?
I've always felt that Silk felt particularly manipulated, and that this prompted his suicidal thoughts.? Kypris is equated with mother, Hyacinth, and Mamelta in his dreams.? I want to talk about the dancing toy that his mother makes move for him - I think this is a symbol of Silk, and that "mother" calls the shots.? When he is enlightened, that female dove voice he hears has always resonated, in my mind, with Kypris, and when mamelta is awakened I have always posited that her claim, "We will be lovers" is not from Mucor (who was insistent that no sex with Silk would occur in their first meeting) but actually from somewhere else. ?
It is Mother who makes the little toy dance, it is Kypris who calls Silk to join with her and Typhon in mainframe, it is Kypris in Hyacinth that compels Silk to love a prostitute at first, and I think it is Kypris' voice in the enlightenment (which can have a physical cause and still be serving the Outsider, just as the castle in the sky is a tent full of hot air in New Sun - it has a physical cause, even though Crane's explanation seems absurd, training us NOT to look for it).? ?
I think that the rather odd naming of the dogs in the tunnels as gods is indicative of exactly what it implies literally: the gods are in the tunnels - the sleepers behind the seals of Pas.? Before his probably suicide attempt, Silk indicates that he is aware of his origins now and that he learned them in the time down below.? While we can say this is just a statement about humanity and the whorl, I think it is personal - he realizes that the things which control him (Mother Kypris) are using him like that dancing toy, that his enlightenment might be spurious and predetermined in that gnostic universe (how quickly the chalk of Silk for Calde goes up after his enlightenment - I've always felt this overdetermined beginning is part of that "clock work figure" of Silk and the whorl.? He realizes that he has very little freedom and it is all set up.? However, Mamelta is swallowed by the giant fish, and I think this too is a symbol - that Kypris is subsumed by something far greater than herself, so that the englightenment, which Silk misinterprets at least once and possibly twice, serves the Outsider in the final analysis even if Pas and Kypris, the male and female voice, set it up as the spark that would start their little clockwork plan in motion, like the dancing toy controlled by Mother.

2013-08-24 Daniel Petersen replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that the clockwork imagery around Silk, such as the dancing toy and Mamelta's swallowing, does not fix him in a predetermined role, and that the Outsider frees him into responsive agency. Agrees with Marc Aramini's reading of the Outsider's role, citing Kypris being subsumed by something greater and gods ontologically smaller than the Outsider setting Silk's plan.
supports · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Outsider liberates Silk from predeterminism; Silk's clockwork role is not definitive; Mamelta's swallowing symbolizes Kypris subsumed by Outsider

'He realizes that he has very little freedom and it is all set up. However, Mamelta is swallowed by the giant fish, and I think this too is a symbol - that Kypris is subsumed by something far greater than herself, so that the englightenment, which Silk misinterprets at least once and possibly twice, serves the Outsider in the final analysis even if Pas and Kypris, the male and female voice, set it up as the spark that would start their little clockwork plan in motion, like the dancing toy controlled by Mother.'

I think I agree with this. I don't think that Silk being shown in various ways to be a 'clockwork figure' is meant to convey that he definitely is and always will be. I think it indicates that *some* forces have that in mind for him as part of their master plan, but that the Outsider actually liberates Silk out of this simplistic pre-determinism into a responsive sort of agency as part of a 'greater plan' than the schemes of gods ontologically 'smaller' than the Outsider.

2013-08-27 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that Severian's remark about language being perspicuous, with one word meaning one thing, signals that the literal wording of Book of the New Sun passages carries a hidden coded message, using the line 'he was the smallest of those dead' about Triskele. Asks whether Severian and his dog are therefore ciphers, grounding the claim in the sun-centred metaphors such as the coast rising to meet the sun.
proposes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: Triskele's death carries a coded message about the Claw

One of the oddest moments in New Sun comes when Severian reflects on how the language he uses is perspicuous, with one word having only one meaning, when the wording of every statement in new sun is ambiguous. "he was the smallest of those dead" - this is another example of the literal interpretation being the "right" one even though it doesn't fit the context. Grammatically it says Triskele (a three legged tripod used in worship of a sun god) is dead. Everything is heliocentric in the metaphors of new sun- the coast rises to meet the sun, etc. We know it can't be literally true because Triskele is alive, but the literal, coded message is that the Claw is not the actual source of healing. Does that make Severian and his dog ciphers?

2013-08-27 Marc Aramini replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that Wolfe deliberately implies multiple meanings of words and names in the New Sun, so that literal or etymological readings can matter even when they contradict narrative logic. Builds on the quoted Triskele example and cites Wolfe's name-meaning essays in Castle of Days, the meta-level introduction to Endangered Species, and his non-elitist speeches.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolfe implies all meanings of words despite narrative logic

"Things weaker than our words for them" - I have found this quote actually quite invaluable in approaching Wolfe. Words have meanings - Wolfe often implies all of those meanings despite pure narrative logic. He is obsessed with the meanings of names as a look at his essays in Castle of Days would prove where he tells you what the names of other prominent authors mean historically. This interest in a word and its essence and all its meanings no doubt preoccupies a self-conscious writer, but he tends to rarely descend to the completely meta level, though at times he flirts with it in his short work and stuff like the introduction to Endangered Species emphasizing the reader. I think this is slightly disingenuous, but his speeches and his intros are surprisingly completely non-elitist.

proposes · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe's essays target New Wave science fiction

Last post this morning - the intro to Enangered Species and several other essays indicate a vague hostility to an academic environment that promulgates the cult of dead authors. Yet ironically the current trend of colleges to emphasize modern stuff that students can relate to would be, possibly, equally inimical to the traditional texts that Wolfe reveres. I can't help thinking that it is in particular the sophisticated New Wave and the sf of the 60s and 70s that he is really writing about. All these trendy modern texts that are being assigned to students might match up with Wolfe's big point in his intro, and yet ... Current trends feel so intellectually impoverished, bargain basement, and devoid of a traditional awareness of continuity.

Disputes Marc Aramini's literal reading that Kypris, Hyacinth, Mamelta, Chenille and Mother are one person and that gods live in the tunnels, arguing instead that these figures embody Woman and that the tunnel gods are a blasphemous joke. Grounds this in Mamelta's ordinary Urth job as a computer technician and in the wordplay of dog and god, and says reading everything as the author's code leaves characters no life.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Kypris and the Long Sun women are one person; Mamelta is Kypris's physical embodiment; The tunnel gods are dogs, a blasphemous joke

From: Marc Aramini

If I recall correctly, Silk is falling asleep when that occurs to me. They are all embodiments of Woman - that is how I see it. I don't think it makes any sense literally to make them the same person. We know that on Urth Mamelta was a computer technician of no special importance, for a start, and while we could invent some relationship between her and the Urthly Kypris, it seems like it would be strained.

As for the gods of the tunnels, I see it as a blasphemous joke that would be plausible in real life. 'Dog' is 'god' written backward, after all. The dogs ARE the lords of the tunnels. And they eat the human 'sacrifices' that are placed there.

I think if you interpret everything as a coded message from the author to the readers, there is no room for the characters to have life. And Wolfe's characters are important to him. They aren't intended to be just ciphers.

- Gerry Quinn

Argues that Severian is a more ambiguous case than Silk on free will and determinism, pointing to his taming of eata and his cruelty as signs of a casuist who claims to be a puppet while ruling harshly. Responds to David Stockhoff's remark that Severian is clockwork and Silk may be freer in Mainframe, adding the dog-love passage as evidence of Severian's adult outlook.
disputes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Severian uses determinism as a casuist's excuse

Severian is a tricky case as far as free will and determinism goes - a WAY more ambiguous guy than Silk. The whole might makes right attitude he shows in taming eata and the external descriptions of Severian as a very cruel and hard man in The Map shows to some degree a casuist: none of the bad stuff is my fault, I was a puppet... But I am a good ruler, obey me as I obey those powers we cannot understand. The love of the dog for its master as the highest form of love, besides being touching, really reveals something about sev's adult perspective as well.
