Themes: Memory and forgetting, Unreliable narration

1998-06-17 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Proposes that Severian's habit of omitting key details until later stems from his claimed perfect memory, which he sometimes lives in so intensely that he forgets what he has not yet told. Grounds this in Severian's own description of his memory, and also questions whether Severian and Thecla were really lovers, suggesting the passages might be fantasy.
proposes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Severian forgets to mention details because his memory is fallible; Severian and Thecla's relationship may be fantasy

Adam (who seems to write at my level, as opposed to some of you

...specifically, his habit of leaving out some critical detail at the time of narration and mentioning it only later, if at all...

tell us about Severian? Other quirks

Actually, it's pretty easy to attribute it (within story context; outside story context, it's Wolfe's delight in torturing the reader -- "puzzling you is the nature of my game," as the Stones' Lucifer sang) to Severian's allegedly (and that is just the right word; I strongly suspect his memory is quite fallible) perfect memory by noting that Severian claims (no, I do not have a page ref handy -- not that it would do any good, I have the h/c) that his memory is so complete and intense that he sometimes gets lost in it, it becomes as present to him as the present; given this, it seems quite likely that he might simply forget(!) that he has not mentioned some detail, and _especially_ an important detail, which seems perfectly "obvious" to him.

Side note. Are we in fact certain that Sev and Thecla _were_ lovers? The first time I read those passages I was quite convinced that they were fantasies on Severian's part, or perhaps on the part of the Sev+Thecla combined personality. While I am no longer convinced that this is so, I am far from convinced that it is _not_ so.

But then, what _are_ we certain of in the Wolfean shadow(!)play...?

1998-06-18 William H. Ansley #

Disputes the idea that authors generally torment their readers as torturers do with clients, arguing that most writers want to please buyers, though he thinks it holds more strongly for Wolfe. Cites Severian's comparison of torturers to men and women and of authors to readers, along with Wolfe's remark that readers are masters, and the list's continued loyalty as evidence.
disputes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe tormenting readers like a torturer with clients

Somewhere in TBotNS there is a passage where Severian compares the relationship between torturers and their clients to that of men to women and authors to their readers. (Maybe I am combining two separate passages.)

I won't touch the men vs. women thing (though I don't buy it totally; just call me naive) but I don't think that, in general, the author vs. reader thing holds up. A good many authors, at least among those who write for a living, must be very anxious to please their readers, so they will keep on buying their books. Perhaps the ones with "artistic integrity" don't care if anyone reads them, but I don't think they are trying to drive people away, in most cases. (Of course I realize that torture may attract as well as repel, at least for the sake of argument.)

I also realize that just because Wolfe has Severian say something, it doesn't means Wolfe himself holds it as a truth. (He has elsewhere referred to readers as masters to the authors' slaves.) But in the case of author vs. reader I do think it is true (or much more true than usual) in the case of Wolfe vs. us. As evidence, just look at this list.

The fact the we keep coming back for more may say something about us, but it definitely attests to Wolfe's skill. He is definitely a grand master.

William Ansley

P.S. If anyone can think of a way I could have qualified my statements above even *more* that I actually did, please let me know.
