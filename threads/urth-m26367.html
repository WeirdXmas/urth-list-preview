The thread: The thread began as a Latro-linked aside but drifted into whether the 'eye of a needle' gate explanation for the camel parable is historically real. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued it is unsupported, citing Wikipedia, and Marc Aramini and Fernando Gouvea agreed it was a popular myth, with Aramini also noting the parable still implies a tight fit. Lee pushed back that the Law's exceptions for the poor still left tithing to priests as the rule, so Jesus' attacks on wealth and priestly power mattered, and suggested this only differed from Danehy-Oakes in wording. Frederick Norwood shared childhood sermon memories and never engaged Wolfe's books, so the thread ended with no resolution and little connection to Latro.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the eye-of-a-needle gate explanation is unsupported
  • Marc Aramini: the gate claim is wrong, the parable still implies a tight fit, and it is about possessions
  • Lee: Jesus' preaching against wealth and priestly power mattered in Second Temple Judaism
  • Fernando Q. Gouvea: plenty of evidence against a Jerusalem gate called the eye of a needle
  • Frederick Hudson Norwood: shared childhood sermon anecdotes without engaging the argument

2015-04-27 Lee Berman #

Argues that the Law's sacrifice exceptions for the poor still left giving a portion of wealth to the priesthood as the general rule, so Jesus' preaching against wealth and priestly power implies it mattered in Second Temple Judaism. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's point that the Law made exceptions for the poor and that Judaism was not centred on wealth, power and status, and suggests the two may only differ in wording.
disputes

Sure, of course. Makes sense.

Not to be argumentative, but as you note, these were "exceptions" for the poor. Meaning the general

rule was that sacrificing a portion of your wealth WAS an essential part of being a good jew, at the time.

And who got that portion of your wealth? The ruling class of the priesthood.

Concomitantly, if the admiration of personal wealth and power and status wasn't an important part of

Judaic religion at the time, why would Jesus spend so much time preaching against it, especially the role

of the priestly castes?

Aren't such things a part of human nature? Don't we still hear sermons against worshipping money and

power? I can't imagine that Judean Hebrews were so different about that than everyone else, including

we of modern society.

I suspect we are not really in disagreement on this issue. Perhaps it is only a matter of how things are

worded.

Rick
