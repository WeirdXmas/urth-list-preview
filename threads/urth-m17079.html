Inhumi in the Whorl

Urth list · 6 messages · 2011-02-09 to 2011-02-10

2011urthThe Book of the Long SunGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffJames WynnLee Berman
The thread: Participants debated whether the Neighbors' phrase "Neighbor Whorl" in Wolfe's Long Sun books is a literal clue to Faerie or a literary allusion. James Wynn argued it is a factual pointer to a real place, Faeri, grounded in the Neighbor's wording, and Gerry Quinn argued the Vanished People moved to another dimension, citing the absence of Faerie from the Solar Cycle and their lack of fairy traits. Lee Berman pushed back that literal readings are not literary enough, defending myth-based subjects, while David Stockhoff said some allusions are just allusions and later proposed the Neighbors moved "next door" to a hidden realm, a view he partly revised to say the Whorl only resembles Faerie. The thread ended without resolution, with a brief agreement from Stockhoff with Quinn's reading and no settled consensus.
  • Lee Berman: Mythic and literary readings of the Neighbors and Faerie are valid and central to the Long Sun series
  • David Stockhoff: Some allusions are just allusions; later argues the Neighbors moved next door to a hidden realm that resembles Faerie
  • James Wynn: "Neighbor Whorl" is a factual reference to a real place, Faeri, not an allusion
  • Gerry Quinn: The Vanished People moved to another dimension or whorl; Faerie is absent from the Solar Cycle and they lack fairy traits

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, The dying sun and the cosmos, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2011-02-09 Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe's references to the Neighbors and Faerie should be read as literary rather than merely literal, since the Long Sun series centers on the mythic mindset of people facing powers they cannot fully grasp. Responds to David Stockhoff's and James Wynn's comments, pushing back on the claim that over-literal readings are 'not literary enough' by pointing to the origins of Western literature in myth and religion.

Old Earth or Urth? Well, either way I think you and James have a good idea on the right way to go with all this.

Hm..."not literary enough"? You can't mean Wolfe displays a poor use of the English language I think....

Aside from that, isn't the thing that turns a story into literature the ability of the author to use his characters to address timeless themes of human existence?

Isn't the origin of western literature rooted in the mythology and religion of ancient people? I can't imagine a more literary subject than trying to illustrate the mindset of the people who actually created those mythologies and religions.

Isn't that essentially what happens throughout the whole SUn Series- human beings contending with powers a bit too great, complex and advanced to fully comprehend but trying to use the written word to describe the situation anyway.

2011-02-10 James Wynn replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that the Neighbors' statement about "Neighbor Whorl" is a factual claim pointing to a real place, Faeri, rather than an allusion, since it refers to humanity in Western France to Ireland centuries ago. Grounds this in the Neighbor's wording, contrasting it with an obvious allusion such as Quetzal as a demon-Dionysus on the Whorl ship, and replies to David Stockhoff's remark that allusions are just allusions.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Neighbor Whorl means Faeri

I get what you mean about allusions, heaven knows. But I think this is different. We know the Neighbors left for some actual place, and the Neighbor says the _some_ of humanity call it "Neighbor Whorl". But the humanity he is talking about is not the humanity on the Short Sun whorls. Assuming "Neighbor Whorl" means "Faeri", the Neighbor has to be talking about people of Western France to Ireland 1000-2000 years ago.

It doesn't feel like an allusion. An allusion in my mind is Quetzal as a demon-Dionysus on the Whorl ship. This statement by the Neighbor seems to be a statement of fact.

2011-02-10 Gerry Quinn replying to James Wynn #

Quotes James Wynn's argument that the Neighbours' "Neighbour Whorl" need not mean Faerie, suggesting the Vanished People moved to another dimension or a whorl near another short sun. Gives two reasons, that Faerie is absent from the Solar Cycle and that the Vanished People lack fairy traits, then notes that Wolfe would have said where they went.
disputes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Neighbour Whorl is not Faerie; The Neighbors moved to another dimension

From: "James Wynn" <[email]>

I don't believe "Neighbour Whorl" means Faerie. The words themselves don't really carry any strong weight. After all, one obvious interpretation is that it just means "the whorl where the neighbours live" just as the "Human Whorl" could mean Urth. And it's not at all obvious that he is referring to huimanity in general (on Urth and the many other colonised planets) rather than humanity on the Short Sun whorls.

I do get the impression that they have moved to another dimension rather than another star. Silkhorn gives us no indication, however, as he tosses out both possibilities at different times. At one time he suggests a whorl circling another short sun, at another he says "the place beyond this place".

My main reasons for thinking it is not Faerie:

1. Why should it be a particular mythological dimension on distant a unrelated planet, a mythological dimension that is not mentioned anywhere in the Solar Cycle?

2. The Vanished People don't have major characteristics in common with fairy folk. [Now somebody is going to come up with some obscure four-legged Croatian river demon and argue that this is what Wolfe was talking about...]

They went somewhere; we don't know where. If Wolfe wanted to tell us where, he would have.

- Gerry Quinn

2011-02-10 David Stockhoff replying to James Wynn #

Argues that the Neighbors of the Short Sun did not really leave Urth but moved "next door" to a hidden realm, since Faerie is a mythology readers share and its beings live beside us unseen. Responds to James Wynn's claim that the "Neighbor Whorl" reference is a real clue to Western European folklore, offering the Faerie logic of the Soldier of the Mist gods and the Grey Havens as support.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Neighbors went next door to Faerie; Neighbors did not physically leave the Whorl

It does seem more like a real clue than some of the correspondences discussed here. But allusions aren't just for mixing up and tying together all the world's mythologies.

Consider that Faerie might be a mythology you and I share, as opposed to that the Greeks or Norse shared and which we observe from a distance. If so, then instead of "Here's a minor character named X who is the lover of Y, and Y is killed by fire; X is a Greek name of a god who loved someone killed by fire so Y must be like that person, who is Z, etc." we might expect something simpler like "Look for me east of the sun and west of the moon," i.e., nowhere you will ever find me.

Fairies always live right next door, but where most people can't see them. They make themselves visible to those they want to have discourse with, especially near their haunts or "sacred" places. In this they are like the gods in Soldier of the Mist, but that doesn't mean they are directly related. They just work by the same logic.

My working hypothesis, therefore, has always been that the Neighbors weren't really leaving in the sense we would know. They're just going "next door." (Kind of like the Elves leaving from the Grey Havens, from another mythology you and I share.) Naturally, I don't have any further evidence for this.

Argues that the Neighbour Whorl is not really a dimension but resembles Faerie only in that its inhabitants are never fully gone until they finally are. Responds to James Wynn's quoted claim that "Neighbour Whorl" need not mean Faerie, agreeing with the reading in a brief, unelaborated way.
