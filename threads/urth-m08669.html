The thread: Participants debated whether split infinitives add rhetorical force or simply sound natural in English. Allan Anderson argued they are worth using only when their elegance outweighs the risk of distracting readers who object to the rule. Jerry Friedman countered that splits often sound natural without special effect and that tmesis is usually associated with swear words, then drifted into iambs and nursery-rhyme meter. Jeffrey McBeth held that splits arise from spoken English rhythm and its ability to insert words mid-phrase, noting Spanish and German speakers do not seem to do this. The thread never returned to Wolfe's books and ended without agreement.
  • Allan Anderson: split infinitives are justified only when they add rhetorical force
  • Jerry Friedman: splits often sound natural with no special effect; tmesis is linked to swearing
  • Jeffrey McBeth: splits come from English spoken rhythm and mid-phrase insertion

2009-07-31 Jeffrey Brent McBeth replying to Allan Anderson #

Argues that split infinitives are a natural product of English spoken rhythm and its ability to insert words mid-phrase, comparing it with infixed swear words and noting Spanish and German speakers do not seem to do this. Replies to Allan Anderson's question about whether split infinitives add rhetorical force, but the post does not address Wolfe's books at all.
proposes · theories: Split infinitives arise from English spoken iambic rhythm

One of the beautiful things about spoken English is the common practice to insert syllables, words, and phrases in the middle of other words and phrases where the iambic rhythm largely inherent in English allows. I would suggest that splitting infinitives is usually a manifestation of this larger ability (that I've not heard in Spanish speakers, and my German coworkers claimed doesn't exist there either). One more vulgar example is the insertion of swear words into the middle of other words (Ex. abso-.......-lutely)

(On a poetical bent, I personally find heptameter more natural than pentameter, though)

Jeff
