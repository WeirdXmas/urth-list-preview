2010-10-22 Dave Tallman #

Proposes that James Morier's 1824 picaresque novel The Adventures of Hajji Baba is a source for names and ideas in Wolfe's "Seven American Nights", especially the name Nadan. Grounds this in the Mollah Nadan character, who swaps clothes with the narrator and is punished in his place, and in Persian meanings of Nadan and Nadan-am.
proposes · source-and-allusion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Wolfe drew on Hajji Baba for Seven American Nights; Nadan name chosen deliberately by Wolfe; Mollah Nadan is a Christ figure

James Morier's Hajji Baba of Ispahan, The Adventures of Hajji Baba<http://hajji.netfirms.com/index.html>is a famous book that may be a source Wolfe drew on for ideas and character
names in "Seven American Nights". This book was written in the 1800's and details the picaresque adventures of Hajji Baba, a barber. It features characters named the Mollah Nadan, Mirza Amak, and Osman Aga. Credit for this find goes to JazzCat, a poster on http;//usefulphrases.yuku.com (It's interesting that in this work the name Nadan is given to a religious leader, a mullah).

According to Robert Borski's posting on urth.net:

While seeming a plausible Arab name, "Nadan," in fact, is something you would be ill-mannered in calling anyone. In Farsi it means "stupid, idiot;"
therefore, by extension, the same thing that 'cretin' does.

With the discovery of "The Adventures of Hajji Baba" that claim about Nadan's name is partially invalidated. In translation, "Hadjji Baba" is a well-known work of Persian literature, and any name drawn from it would be a reasonable name for a child. In the printed text of the English version of the book (available online through Google books), the name shows up as "Molah Nad&acirc;n". According to
http://www.khamush.com/persian/rubaie236.htm, "Nad&acirc;n-&aacute;m" means "I cannot."

I'm becoming convinced that Wolfe read this book as part of his background research for the story and picked that name deliberately. Molah Nadan changes clothing with the narrator and ends up being punished for a crime in his place. A Christ figure, perhaps?

2010-10-24 Stone Ox replying to Dave Tallman #

Reports that the name Nadan in Seven American Nights may come from Morier's Hajji Baba and from an older source, noting the Persian phrase Nadan-am meaning 'I cannot'. Responds to Dave Tallman's find by quoting the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia summary of the Ahikar story, where the unappreciative nephew Nadan falsely accuses his benefactor and is punished.
informs · source-and-allusion · theories: Wolfe took the name Nadan from Hajji Baba; Wolfe knew the Ahikar story of Nadan

http;//usefulphrases.yuku.com

http://www.khamush.com/persian/rubaie236.htm, >"Nad&acirc;n-&aacute;m" means "I cannot."

Googling a while ago, I came across another instance of the name Nadan. It comes from the story of Nadan and Ahikar (or Ahiqar), which is a story
from the Middle East from Biblical times, and which Wolfe also may have been familiar with.

The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia summarizes it as follows:

"Ahikar ... adopts as heir his nephew Nadan. The unappreciative youth falsely accuses his benefactor of treason, whereupon Ahikar escapes death only through the intervention of an executioner whom he himself had once spared,"

Eventually Ahikar is restored to his position, and Nadan is punished.

Peter S
