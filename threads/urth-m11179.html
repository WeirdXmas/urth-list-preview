The Lochage's Rant...

Urth list · 8 messages · 2010-08-30 to 2010-08-31

2010urthThe Book of the New SunRyan DunnJerry FriedmanCliff JudgeGwern BranwenDavid StockhoffSteven BowenJeff Wilson
The thread: Ryan Dunn asked what the lochage means in The Shadow of the Torturer when he says Nessus changes nightly like chalked writing and that people take up cobbles in the dark to build houses. Jeff Wilson and Gwern Branwen both offered literal readings, Wilson via paved-street reuse and a favela comparison, Gwern via houses built out over or into the street. David Stockhoff read the city as a metaphor for Urth and the Commonwealth, linking it to modern megacities. Steven Bowen explained the chalk as graffiti marking illegal land claims and argued authorities cannot enforce even street boundaries, and Dunn accepted this as an anarchist dystopia reading. The thread ended in rough agreement on the literal, illegal-building reading.
  • Ryan Dunn: asks what the cobble and chalk imagery means, then accepts the illegal land-claim reading
  • Jeff Wilson: literal reading, people clear paved areas for quick sheds and resell stones, favela comparison
  • Gwern Branwen: houses extend over cobblestones or rise in the street, city grows like chalked writing
  • David Stockhoff: Nessus mirrors megacities and stands for Urth as a living, dying world
  • Steven Bowen: cobbles are street stones, chalk is graffiti signaling illegal claims, authorities cannot enforce boundaries

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, The dying sun and the cosmos, Colonization and the native

2010-08-30 Ryan Dunn #

Asks what the lochage means in The Shadow of the Torturer when he says Nessus changes every night like chalked writing and that clever people take up cobbles in the dark to build houses. Explains confusion about the cobbles image and whether the metaphor means buildings constantly appearing and crumbling from population.
questions · close-textual-reading

Hello All,

I have had a hard time with a passage from Shadow... the rant the lochage gives to Severian in the bartizan. He is telling Severian how vast Nessus is, how all attempts to count them have failed, and so forth.

But he describes the city in this passage, as follows:

"The city grows and changes every night, like writing chalked on a wall. Houses are built in the streets by clever people who take up the cobbles in the dark and claim the ground - did you know that?"

I think I understand the second part. I'm not sure what taking up the cobbles in the dark means exactly, but this has something to do with clever people claiming land as their own by stealing it in the dark? Anyhow...

More importantly, what does he mean by the city changing every night? And how is writing chalked on a wall the right metaphor here. Is he saying there are so many people, that every day a line of buildings might appear, while another might crumble?

If anybody can help here, I would appreciate it.

Thank You,

...ryan

2010-08-30 Jeff Wilson replying to Ryan Dunn #

Explains how the lochage's remark that Nessus changes nightly, with houses built by people who lift cobbles, could work in practice: clearing paved areas for quick sheds and reselling stones. Supports the literal reading with general knowledge of paved streets and reuse of materials, and compares the result to Brazilian favelas via a Wikipedia link.
supports · close-textual-reading

If you google on "cobblestone street" you can see lots of streets, sidewalks, and plazas paved with a variety of cobblesstones from natural found rocks to shaped and polished stones to bricks, closely or loosely tesselated to form a durable, weather-proof surface for foot and vehicular traffic in these public areas.

"Clever people" can arrange for a party to clear an area of these stones and put up a quick-and-dirty shed in the place so that by the light of the next day, it looks like the place was never paved to start with and has been a private home all along. The cobbles are also worth money if they can be hauled to where new development is taking place.

With multiple hand-made buildings per person, there are always opportunities to tear down here and reassemble there. I imagine it yields something like the the Brazilian favelas:

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Favela

2010-08-31 Gwern Branwen replying to Ryan Dunn #

Explains that the lochage's remark about houses rising in Nessus's streets refers to people extending buildings out over cobblestones or erecting whole houses in the street, with the city growing like chalked writing. Offers this as a literal reading of Ryan Dunn's quoted passage from The Shadow of the Torturer, with no further evidence cited.
answers · close-textual-reading

He's referring to the practice of extending houses out into the street (just plop your timbers on top of the cobblestones, or remove the stones first, and build upwards), or just brazenly building an entire house in the street. Once the building is *there*, who's going to object? He's being pretty literal there.

2010-08-31 David Stockhoff replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the slums and wasteland around Nessus in The Book of the Long Sun mirror modern megacities, and that Nessus, standing in for the Commonwealth and Urth, is a circle-of-life image of a living, dying world. Responds to Ryan Dunn and Jeff Wilson's cobblestone-street discussion by suggesting the favela comparison and reading the city as a metaphor for Urth itself.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: Nessus stands in for Urth

The favelas were my first thought as well. Nessus is a bona fide megacity, with a core (or two) and vast slums.

These slums border on wasteland---the only difference between Nessus and our megacities is that ours are growing. Nessus dies every day as it moves north, although so slowly you can't see it. It's hidden.

I'm also a little mystified by the need for this description. But it seems like a kind of circle-of-life metaphor. Nessus is both alive and dying. And since to some degree it stands in for the Commonwealth and the Commonwealth stands in for Urth, Nessus _is_ Urth.

I'd look there.

2010-08-31 Steven Bowen replying to Ryan Dunn #

Explains that 'cobbles' are the stones used to build streets and that the chalk-on-the-wall image suggests graffiti, which the lochage's rant uses to signal the illegal nature of the changes. Responds to Ryan Dunn's questions about taking up cobbles in the dark and the city changing every night by arguing that the authorities cannot enforce even basic boundary lines like streets.
answers · close-textual-reading

Cobbles are stones used to build the streets. The 'chalk on the wall' imagery suggests grafitti to me. Pretty obvious. He uses the grafitti idea to speak to the illegal nature of the changes. The inability for the authorities to enforce even simple boundary lines like streets.

Steve
