Sev's family tree/gender bender

Urth list · 3 messages · 2011-01-31 to 2011-02-01

2011urthThe Book of the New SunGerry QuinnLee BermanSon of Witz
The thread: Lee Berman proposed that the old storyteller in the Casdoe section is a woman, reading the description of Fechin's face and the line about women giving him what he wants as evidence, and linking Casdoe/Dorcas to Severian/Severa as a family connection. Son of Witz disputed this, arguing the passage shows only admiration, with the sun's light possibly recalling the New Sun, and that nothing in the text changes the old man into a woman. Gerry Quinn also disputed Berman, quoting the same passage to argue the speaker is male, since 'How could he share what he'd had?' implies a girl would share easily. The thread stayed a two-sided disagreement over the storyteller's gender, with no resolution recorded.
  • Lee Berman: the old storyteller is a woman, and Agia is an assassin read through mistaken gender
  • Son of Witz: the passage shows admiration only; nothing shifts the old man to a woman
  • Gerry Quinn: the speaker is male, based on the 'share what he'd had' line

Themes: Unreliable narration, Women, sex and gender, Fathers, mothers and children

Disputes Lee Berman's reading that the old person in Casdoe's Fechin story is a woman disguised in a way that explains an attraction to Fechin's face. Argues the passage shows only admiration, since the sun's light on Fechin may recall the New Sun, and notes nothing in the text shifts the old man to a woman or shows Severian misreading gender.

LOL

It provides a possible link to your Agia= Assasin of Valeria theory, By linking her to the term assasin. For me, it doesn't overcome the idea that he would have to not recognize her when she is in drag, but it does strenghten your idea.

I don't see attraction. admiration, sure.
Any boy in a group ought to know who is the handsomest around without being attracted to him. He will be the one getting all the play. Maybe "the light of the sun" on his face, nearly making him cry is the crux, but isn't there an excised section that talks about how the light that day seemed like the New Sun's? That would be enough to sensitize him on it's own, I think. I'm not denying that it is possible he has some attraction, but it is not the only way to read this passage. There seems to be nothing here to shift the old man to an old woman.

Again, there is lots of androgeny in the book, but I see nothing to suggest Severian has discernment issues. He describes the Autarch as androgynous. I think it's only once he uses "Androgyn" and it is in the context of an admittedly wrong first impression.

2011-02-01 Gerry Quinn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the speaker of the Fechin passage is male rather than female, reading the line 'How could he share what he'd had?' as implying a girl would share easily. Responds to Lee Berman's claim that the old person is a woman by quoting the same passage and drawing the opposite inference about the speaker's gender.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Old person is male, not female

From: "Lee Berman" <[email]>

I can't remember what her name was, but she was truly beautiful, the way the quietest are sometimes. I never knew up till then that Fechin knew her, but he asked me to wait, and I sat down on the first step in front of the gate." [--]

"He wasn't inside long, but when he came out, with the girl looking out the window, I knew what they had done. I looked at him, and he spread those long, thin, monkey arms. How could he share what he'd had? In the end, he made the girl give me half a loaf of bread and some fruit. He drew my picture on one side of the paper and the girl's on the other, but he kept the pictures."

How could he share what he'd had? Very easily, surely, if the speaker was a girl!

That suggests that the speaker was male, and if he is indeed indicating an attraction to Fechin, it was a homosexual one. Evidently Fechin was not much into androgyny.

- Gerry Quinn
