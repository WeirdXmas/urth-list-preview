Miles & Jolenta

Urth list · 4 messages · 1998-10-29 to 1998-10-30

1998urthThe Book of the New SunTony EllisAlice TurnerWilliam H. AnsleyPeter Westlake
The thread: The thread asked whether Miles and Jolenta are siblings or lookalikes, and whether the mirror scene in The Citadel of the Autarch shows Miles's face or Jolenta's. Alice Turner argued the Jolenta who appears is a plain serving girl given artificial glamour by Dr. Talos, citing the text's description of the original and Jonas's attraction to the artifice. Peter Westlake countered that the mirror scene shows Miles's own face and that the soldier's red-gold-haired girl memory concerns Jonas, quoting Severian's 'Is this the face?' passage. William H. Ansley and Tony Ellis backed Westlake's reading as the simpler one, with Ellis suggesting readers fix on one reading of ambiguous passages on first reading. The thread ended with the mirror-scene reading largely settled in Westlake's favor, though Turner's artificial-glamour argument went unanswered in detail.
  • Alice Turner: Jolenta is a plain serving girl made glamorous artificially by Dr. Talos, not a lookalike of Miles
  • Peter Westlake: The mirror scene shows Miles's own face; the red-gold-haired girl memory concerns Jonas
  • William H. Ansley: 'Is this the face?' refers to Miles's reflection; reading Jolenta in complicates the passage
  • Tony Ellis: The simpler reading of the mirror passage is preferred; readers should settle on one reading of ambiguous passages

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Mirrors, pictures and art, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting

1998-10-29 Alice Turner #

Argues against the idea that Miles and Jolenta are siblings or lookalikes, claiming the Jolenta who appears is not the original plain serving girl but a figure given artificial glamour by Dr. Talos. Grounds the point in the text's description of the original as plain, and in Jonas finding the artificiality attractive.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Jolenta is not the original plain serving girl

One strong argument against Miles and Jolenta's being actual siblings (or lookalikes) is that *Jolenta* doesn't look like Jolenta. The original is a plain serving girl whom Dr. Talos has endowed with artificial glamour. The artificiality is what is attractive to Jonas.

-alga

1998-10-30 Peter Westlake #

Argues that the mirror scene in The Citadel of the Autarch shows Miles's own face, not Jolenta's, and that the soldier's memory of a red-gold-haired girl concerns Jonas rather than Jolenta. Answers the earlier poster's claim about Jolenta's looks by quoting the Citadel passage in which Severian holds up a mirror to the soldier and asks whether this is the face.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Miles looks like Severian's memory of Jonas, not Jolenta

That's right. Anyway, what is all this about Miles looking like Jolenta? Severian doesn't show him Jolenta's face in the mirror, he shows him his own! That's how mirrors work, even on Urth - this is only a shaving mirror, not (half of) one of Hethor's portable teleporters.

Citadel, ch VI:

"What do you remember? Tell me all of it, and I'll tell you what I know, and what I can guess."

"Walking with you. A lot of darkness.... I fell, or maybe flew through it. Seeing my own face, multiplied again and again. A girl with hair like red gold and enormous eyes."

"A beautiful woman?"

He nodded. "The most beautiful in the world."

Raising my voice, I asked if anyone had a mirror he would lend us for a moment. Foila produced one from the possessions beneath her cot, and I held it up for the soldier. Is this the face?"

He hesitated. "I think so."

"Blue eyes?"

"...I can't be sure."

Condensed, this goes:

"Seeing my own face, ..."

"Was this the face?" - holding a mirror in front of him.

The face that he saw, in other words. The blue eyes Severian queries are the soldier's "honest blue eyes" (next page). The soldier appears to remember being Jonas, and Severian is asking him to compare the face he remembers seeing in the mirrors with his present face. It isn't physically the same, of course, but it keeps reminding Severian of Jonas, and the soldier can be forgiven for having the same problem, having been reprogrammed with Severian's idea of Jonas's memories. Nothing to do with Jolenta at all.

Sceptical Bear.

1998-10-30 William H. Ansley #

Agrees with Peter Westlake that the mirror passage in The Book of the New Sun shows Miles seeing his own face rather than Jolenta's. Argues that Severian's line "Is this the face?" refers back to Miles's own reflected face, and that reading Jolenta into the scene complicates a passage Wolfe probably did not intend.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Miles sees his own face in the mirror, not Jolenta's

Thank goodness (and evilness, to preserve duality)!

I am glad that someone else interpreted this passage in the same way I did, which seems to me to be the only sensible way to interpret it. In fact, I couldn't figure out what mantis was talking about when he stated his interpretation, until I went back and read the passage again. I suppose it is possible to read the passage his way, but I think to do so is to needlessly complicate matters. When Severian says, "Is this the face?" he is referring back to Miles's statement, "Seeing my own face [that is Jonas's], multiplied again and again" not to his vision of (a woman who might be) Jolenta.

The fact that Miles seems to have memories that belong to Jonas is mystery enough without creating new ones that I think Wolfe never intended or even thought of.

William Ansley

1998-10-30 Tony Ellis #

Agrees that a passage about Miles seeing his own face in a mirror can be read two ways, with the simpler reading preferred, and notes that he and Mantis took the same misreading. Suggests readers fix on one reading of ambiguous Wolfe passages on first reading, citing the rats' writing in Ultan's library as an example.
supports · close-textual-reading

Yup, that works for me.

Strictly speaking, I think the passage can be read either way: when Miles says "Seeing my own face, multiplied again and again. A girl with hair like red gold and enormous eyes", it -can- be interpreted as "seeing my own face... [which was that of] a girl with hair like red gold..." But it's a complicated interpretation, and I'm only too happy to be wrong in favour of a simpler one.

It's interesting that Mantis and I made the same misreading, though (if he'll accept that it -is- a misreading). There seem to be certain ambiguous passages in Wolfe that you either interpret one way or another the first time you read them, and that's how it gets hard-wired in your brain: it never even occurs to you on further readings that it might be read a different way. I'm thinking of the rats' "writing" on the walls of Ultan's library, for example.
