How would you rate Wolfe as a short story writer?

Urth list · 5 messages · 2003-06-25 to 2003-06-27

2003urthSam KeyesJoe CilluffoTony EllisAnthony FerraraJames Jordan
The thread: Posters asked how Wolfe ranks as a short story writer. Anthony Ferrara and cilluff1 argued that his stories are uneven and fall short of his novels, with cilluff1 citing Ted Chiang as a benchmark. Sam Keyes proposed that the short form is structurally less suited to Wolfe's grand-scale games and admitted having read few stories. Tony Ellis disputed the premise, calling the average story as rewarding as the novels and naming favourites. James Jordan split the stories into light, funny pieces and works of full literary quality. The thread ended without consensus, with the disagreement resting mostly on general impressions rather than close reading.
  • Anthony Ferrara: greatest novelists rank, but short stories are uneven
  • Sam Keyes: short form is poorly suited to Wolfe's grand-scale games
  • cilluff1: superb novelist, short stories good but below novels and Chiang
  • Tony Ellis: average story as rewarding as novels, better than most SF short fiction
  • James Jordan: stories are either light and funny or fully equal to the novels

Themes: Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Books, libraries and writing

2003-06-25 Sam Keyes #

Argues that Wolfe's apparent weakness in short stories may be inevitable, since the short form is less suited to the grand-scale questions and games Wolfe favors than the multi-volume novel. Responds to Anthony's earlier view, quoted in the post, that Wolfe's stories fall short of his novels, and notes the poster has read few of the stories, citing Island of Doctor Death and Wolfe's own remarks on big projects.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Short story form suits Wolfe's grand-scale games less well than novels

I, too, am really still unfamiliar with most of Wolfe's short fiction (though I did just buy Island of Doctor Death). I wonder if this seeming discrepancy is inevitable (and given what Wolfe has said about doing "big" projects, he knows this too). I mean, in the world of fiction, quantity does, perhaps more often than we would like to admit, tend to accompany high quality. So maybe it's not that Wolfe is weaker in short stories, but rather that short stories, formally, are usually weaker--or, I ought to add, weaker at asking the questions and playing the games that Wolfe likes to do, which are usually on a grand scale.

2003-06-27 James Jordan #

Argues that Wolfe's short stories fall into two kinds: light, often funny pieces such as "The Headless Man" that are enjoyable but not great, and stories of full literary quality equal to his longer work. Grounds the judgment in having read all the stories, excluding recent ones in hard-to-find editions, and offers no close reading of specific texts beyond the one example.
proposes · genre-and-craft

Having read all of Wolfe's short stories, except for recent ones published in odd and hard to find (expensive) books, I would say this: There are two kinds. Wolfe has written some fluffy, often funny pieces, like "The Headless Man." These are fun to read, but hardly "great." On the other hand, there are some excellent stories of full literary quality, and I think they are fully on a part with his longer work.

Nutria
