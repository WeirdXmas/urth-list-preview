The thread: Starting from a quoted remark on Hiroshima Day and cultural views of the bombing, the thread (which is off-topic from Wolfe) became a long debate on whether the atomic bombings were a necessary evil, a mercy, or a crime. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Marc Aramini defended the bombings on just-war and decisive-victory grounds, while Jeff Wilson and Thomas Bitterman argued they were a crime or unnecessary to Japan's surrender. James Wynn argued for the bombings as humane, and Son of Witz and Stuart Hamm disputed sources, including a withdrawn book on Hiroshima. The thread drifted into WWII history, side debates on Huckleberry Finn and postwar leftists, and ended without agreement.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the bombings were a necessary evil under just war doctrine
  • Marc Aramini: the bombings were a decisive knockout blow that spared lives
  • James Wynn: the bombings were humane and less destructive than conventional campaigns
  • Jeff Wilson: the bombing was both a horrible crime and a justifiable necessity
  • Thomas Bitterman: the bombs were probably not the main cause of Japan's surrender
  • Stuart Hamm: an invasion would have cost more lives, but the bombing terror argues against nuclear weapons

Themes: War and soldiers, Power, rulers and politics, Justice, torture and mercy

2011-01-18 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki was a necessary evil, weighing it against the feared cost of an invasion of the Japanese Home Islands. Grounds the view in the just war doctrine's three conditions: preventing a palpable evil, avoiding greater evil in the conduct of the war, and having a reasonable chance of success. Responds to Lee Berman's quoted remarks on Hiroshima Day and whether war can be a necessary evil.
supports

You're right: this American (and not a conservative by any means) considers the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki a necessary evil. The alternative would have been a much larger cost in Japanese lives, plus an unguessable number of American lives, as we attempted an invasion of the Japanese Home Islands.

The "just war" doctrine suggests that self-defense is not (quite) sufficient reason. The Just War must (a) be fought to prevent a palpable evil, (b) be fought in such a way that greater evil is not wrought, and (c) have a reasonable chance of preventing that evil.

2011-01-18 Marc Aramini replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki were a decisive knockout blow that spared greater loss of life than prolonged war, and contrasts Judeo-Christian restraint with purging ideologies and colonial conquest. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's endorsement of the bombings as a necessary evil, using analogies to boxing and the Spanish and Puritan treatment of natives rather than any passage from Wolfe's books.
disputes

In anything, the biggest most devastating knock out blow has a better chance of ending the conflict decisively. Just as in Boxing, sometimes that large blow can be more merciful than beating someone up for twelve rounds. Attrition over years could have cost many more lives. The nice thing about the current trendy Judeo-Christian majority is that once war is disengaged there seems to be very little wholesale slaughter, something you can have no assurance of with a more non-traditional take or a "purging" tradition (Hitler's, for example, he seems to be the most obvious monster in this particular room, or the older zeitgeist that there is only one truth, one word, inquisition style, and thankfully this has fled with the onset of modernity, at least in America).

If I were a minority in danger of being purged and I had to chose an ethos to prevail in the world, I would certainly want either a humanist one that is absolutely premissive and too forgiving as ours seems to be, or one that starts with "love thy neighbor as thyself" with a very very broad definition of neighbor. The easier to exploit the suckers in charge and avoid getting eliminated, where intimations of heinousness are pretty much ignored until after the fact.

The Spanish conqeurors did seem to assimilate a bit more in their hostile takeovers with the natives than, say, the Puritan tradition, where they just kind of got them out of the picture one way or another definitively.
