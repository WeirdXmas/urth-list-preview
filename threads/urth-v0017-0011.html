Digest Urth, or brains thereof

Urth list · 3 messages · 1998-07-14 to 1998-07-15

1998urthThe Book of the New SunMark MillmanCharles DyeMichael Straight
The thread: Readers debated how the Autarch's legitimacy and succession work in The Book of the New Sun, starting from Charles Dye's question of whether brain-eating or designation confers the title. Dye argued a captured Appian's corpse, knife and drug could let Inire install a bystander as Autarch, without a designated heir. Michael Straight questioned how a claimant could be recognized through the words of command, since anyone judging would need to know them and could then impersonate the Autarch, and floated hypnotic triggers. Mark Millman countered that the words are verified through mechanical devices, officers' inherited passwords, and Inire as verifier of successors. The thread stayed at the level of textual argument and ended without resolution.
  • Charles Dye: succession can run through a bystander installed by Inire, not only a designated heir
  • Michael Straight: recognition by the words is circular and open to impersonation, perhaps via hypnotic triggers
  • Mark Millman: the words are verifiable through mechanical devices, inherited passwords, and Inire

Themes: Hidden identities and parentage, Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Power, rulers and politics

Argues that the Autarch's succession need not depend on a designated heir, since a captured Appian's corpse, knife and drug could let Inire install a bystander and continue the dynasty. Builds on Charles Dye's question about whether brain-eating or designation makes an Autarch legitimate, citing Appian's capture and Inire's skulking in the Citadel scene.

Such an ugly phrase, that. Can't we come up with some polite euphemism? "Breakfast for your head" ... no, wait, I like "eating brains" better. Never mind. Yes, that was my point: you need a body for succession to proceed.

But note that ol' Appian has been captured by the enemy! It's made pretty clear that Severian is the "designated successor," but I'm not certain that there always is one. Alls ya needs is a corpse, a knife, and the drug -- all provided by Appian -- and instructions, which presumably Inire would have provided had A been unable to. The next- in-line may be a randomly chosen bystander, if you don't have a designated successor handy.

(You'll notice that after Appian's injury and capture, Inire can be seen skulking about. Severian thinks he's trying to carry out a rescue. Fat chance -- Appian's as good as dead. I believe Inire is there to ensure the continued dynasty.)

That said, yes, the regime does have some problems. It also has a few advantages. Fr'instance, it's easy to decide whether or not an individual claiming the Autarchy is the real McCoy. He knows the words!

And also provides a slick justification for the royal plural.

1998-07-15 Michael Straight #

Questions how the Autarch's claimant can be recognized by knowing the words, since anyone judging a claimant would also need to know them and could then pose as the Autarch. Responds to Charles Dye's remark that the regime's test is easy because the real Autarch knows the words, and floats the idea that the words might be hypnotic triggers people recognize without remembering.
questions · close-textual-reading · theories: the Autarch's words are hypnotic triggers

I have to admit some confusion on that point. Wouldn't anyone judging the Autarch also have to know the words in order to recognize them? Shouldn't any such person be able to speak "the words" and pose as the Autarch?

Are they perhaps some sort of hypnotic triggers that people recognize but are unable to remember?

-Rostrum

1998-07-15 Mark Millman #

Argues that the Autarch's legitimacy can be verified through the words of command, which open mechanical devices, pass to subordinates, and are checked by Inire. Disputes Rostrum's objection that the castellan's reaction to a password is unknown, citing the mechanical devices, the officers' inherited passwords, and Inire as verifier of successors.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Words of command verify the Autarch's legitimacy; Inire can verify each successor

This seems entirely possible, though if I recall (and you've all just had a taste of how bad my memory can be) we're
really given no evidence of how the castellan of the Citadel reacts to Severian's word of command--whether, that is, he jumps to an implanted compulsion or simply recognizes
the password, as it were (ignoring the possibility you raise, Rostrum, that he recognizes a word that deliberately has been made unavailable to his conscious mind, which I'd
call a special case of the latter situation).

However, I don't think this is an insuperable obstacle. For one thing, many of the words command mechanical contri-
vances, so all a putative Autarch would have to do, for ex- ample, would be to open the door behind the throne and
maybe operate a couple of other devices to prove his or
her legitimacy. Or the words of command for three or four subordinates could be employed; even if the officers are consciously aware of their passwords, they may only be
inherited, so to speak, in the line of succession for each office. The person who knows several must therefore be
the Autarch.

Or (and to me this seems most likely) Inire, who seems to be as ubiquitous as flan in Spanish restaurants, could
assert the legitimacy of each successor based either on
his having witnessed the essential act of succession or on a test of the individual in question. It seems as though he must know a great many, if not all, of the words of power; and one would think him, in this context at least, to be for all practical purposes incorruptible.

Mark Millman
