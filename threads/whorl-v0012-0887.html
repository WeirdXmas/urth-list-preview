The Narrator's mistakes

The thread: Adam Stephanides argued that Narr's admitted mistakes in Return to the Whorl show his manuscript is less reliable than it seems, possibly hinting he is secretly Horn, citing RttW 184 and the editors' corrections. Michael Straight countered that Silk's admission in Nightside the Long Sun concerns how the account is written, what it focuses on and omits, not false facts, offering a broader sense of 'mistake'. 2001-06-21 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that Narr's admitted mistakes in Return to the Whorl show his manuscript is less reliable than it seems, and that his claim of honest mistakes may hint he is secretly Horn. Grounds this in the passage at RttW 184, noting the editors' corrections go against Narr's policy and that his original account was written after a delay like Horn's own sections.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Narr is secretly Horn; Narr's manuscript is unreliable due to delayed writing

While thumbing through RttW, I came across a passage in which Narr notes he has made a mistake, but says he won't correct it, for various reasons, concluding, "No, there really are such things as honest mistakes; this account is full of them, and I intend to leave it that way." (184) This passage raises several interesting points.

1) In correcting Narr's "obvious errors," the editors are going against Narr's own policy.

2) Narr says that "I cannot correct all or even most of them [his mistakes] without tearing the whole account to shreds and starting again." If Narr is not just being humble, his original manuscript had a lot of mistakes. We may wonder how many of them the editors caught (there's no reason to think they were in a position to recognize all the mistakes) and how many of them the editors missed and we are also unable to catch.

3) Narr goes on to say "My new account, moreover, would be bound to be worse than this, since I could not prevent myself from attributing to myself knowledge and opinions I did not have at the time the events I recorded occurred." But of course, everything in his original account dealing with Horn's experiences in his own body was written down after a similar or greater delay.

We've already lost the third-person sections of RttW as a reliable source; it appears that Narr's manuscript is not as solid as it seems.

One other point about this passage: Narr's statement that "there really are such things as honest mistakes." It's odd that Narr should throw this in, because nowhere in the preceding paragraph (or anywhere else that I can recall) is it suggested that there aren't. Does this remark reflect Narr's subconscious awareness that his entire account is based upon a "dishonest mistake," that he is fully Horn?

--Adam

2001-06-22 Michael Straight #

Argues that Patera Silk's admission in Nightside the Long Sun that he cannot correct his mistakes refers to how the account is written, what it focuses on and omits, rather than to false factual statements. Responds to Adam Stephanides's reading, which assumes the manuscript contains many errors the editors missed, by offering a broader sense of the word 'mistake'.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk's mistakes concern emphasis and omission, not factual falsehood

The fact that he felt like he would need to tear up the whole account made me think he was referring more to the way things were written, the things he focused on, the things he left out, than specific "factual" errors.

"Mistake" is a broader word, encompassing, "I should have started telling about this, skipped that, gone into detail about this other, and done a better job of explaining these things." He's not necessarily saying that there are lots of false statements.

-Rostrum

2001-06-22 Adam Stephanides #

Argues that Narr's admission in the Long Sun books of confusing Seawrack's ring with Oreb's refers to specific factual errors rather than broader lapses in organization or emphasis. Supports this by quoting Narr's passage about refusing to line out his mistake and the sentence that follows it, and responds to Michael Straight's reading that 'mistake' means the way things were written.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Narr's mistake refers to specific factual errors

Narr begins the paragraph by saying that he has confused Seawrack's ring with Oreb's. He goes on to say "I should go back and line out my mistake, I suppose, but I hate lining things out--it gives the page such an ugly appearance. Besides, to line out is to accept responsibility for the correctness of all that is let stand. To correct that or any other error would be to invite you to ask me (when you read this, as I hope you soon will) why I failed to correct some other." (184) He goes on with the sentence I quoted above. It seems to me that he is indeed talking about specific factual errors.

--Adam
