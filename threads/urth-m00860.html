The thread: Posters debated how The Wizard Knight ends and how its cosmic hierarchy of gods is arranged. Aramini1 proposed that Able loops back to his lost childhood, while StoneOx17 and James Wynn read him as serving the Most High God after finishing his mission, and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes insisted Kleos must be completed first. Stilskin floated a return to our world, and Andrew Bollen objected that a homecoming would make Able's long letter to Ben pointless. Lebling and Wynn tied worship to cosmic level and to the Most Low God's self-worship, while Rolsky and Robertson argued over Arnthor, Crowley's Little, Big, and the mythic sources. The thread ended in scattered disagreement, with the late dream-swap reading left unresolved.
  • aramini1: Able returns to his childhood missing years with Desiri, closing the book as a loop
  • StoneOx17: Able is a coming-of-age story that does not loop; he regains his lost years and serves the Most High God
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the Most High God sits above Kleos, so Able must finish serving Kleos first
  • David Lebling: worship depends on cosmic level, and the Most Low God worships only himself
  • James Wynn: Able's later service to the Most High God comes after his Mythgarthr mission; the levels may hold other worlds
  • Andy Robertson: the cosmology draws on older mythic streams and the places are modes of consciousness, not planets

Themes: Gods, angels and the Increate, The dying sun and the cosmos, Time loops and causality

2005-01-06 Marc Aramini #

Proposes that Able in The Wizard Knight is returning to the lost years of his childhood with Desiri, a loop that closes the book as a circle and will lead him to forget what happened. Grounds this in the passages where Able watches himself plant the spiny orange in Skai and where Michael says Able was alone on the island when the knight appears.
proposes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Able returns to his childhood at the end; Able is freed from the passage of time

I only have about 36 more messages to read before I'm caught up, so forgive me if someone has said this.

It seemed pretty clear to me at the end of the Wizard Knight that Able is going back to the missing years of his childhood with Desiri, the part between leaving the log cabin and showing up on the island where he planted the spiny orange. (The years when he would have grown up to be the man he was acelerated into)

The Wizard EXPLICITLY states that Able watches himself plant the spiny orange during his time in Skai, and Michael also explicitly states Able was the only person on the island when the knight appears - this means that Able has, in some fashion, been freed from the passage of time.

Able is going back to his childhood at the end, the lost time that was taken from him. The great lord whose service will be required is Arnthnor, and Able will go on to forget everything that happened. The book is a big circle. But it isn't the first Wolfe book to work like that.

Maybe someone said that - I'll see either tonight or tomorrow when I catch up.

Marc

2005-01-06 Stone Ox replying to aramini1 #

Disputes aramini1's idea that Able returns to his childhood missing years with Desiri, arguing the Wizard Knight is a coming-of-age book that does not loop back. Cites Able's recovered memories on pages 56 and 404 as showing he regains the lost years after delivering his message, and prefers a reading where Able serves the Most High God.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Able returns to his childhood missing years; Able serves the Most High God in America

Watching himself plant the spiny orange was one of those things he did during his missing 20 years in Skai. Here we are (it's really difficult to find things in
this book, although not as difficult as in Peace). Page 56.

"We went to the spring Mimir," I said at last. "I drank its water and remembered
you and Gylf, and a lot of other things. I visited myself, watching myself drink
water in the ruins of Bluestone Castle. Afterward the Valfather laid his condition
on me. ..."

Presumably, respecting time is part of the condition the Valfather has laid on him.
Although, even when he broke the condition, he still wasn't able to go back in time
and save those people who had died, so even time travel has its limitations in
Mythgarthr.

But he remembers his missing years after delivering his message to Arnthor, so
they're not lost to him in the end. Page 404.

I shook my head. "We played together as children, Uri. Disiri and me. I remember now."
Her voice was tender. "Do you, Lord?"
"I do." Until that moment I had not known I remembered. "I thought they wiped those memories away, but they only hid them under the message. ..."

I don't see any evidence for this, and I don't like the idea at all. WK seems to me
to be a coming-of-age book, where Able learns stuff and grows up. You don't make
big circles out of coming-of-age books -- it defeats their whole purpose. (OK, in
some sense, Fifth Head of Cerberus is an exception to this. But my point still
stands.)

The idea (that somebody proposed here) that Able's going back to America to serve the Most High God -- and I sure hope the great lord in need of a knight is
the Most High God and not the American President -- I find much more convincing.

-- Stone Ox (Peter)

2005-01-06 stilskin replying to James Wynn #

Proposes that the ending of The Wizard Knight has Able taken not to his America but to our world, suggesting Wolfe's characters gain reality as fictional beings in the mind of God, as souls rise in Catholic belief. Responds to James Wynn's idea that Able serves the Most High God, noting Able's own musing that America lies sideways to Mythgarthr.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Able is taken to our world at the end; Wolfe's characters gain reality like souls in Heaven

I'm not sure, in the context of the WK universe, that
America is above Kleos. I seem to recall a place
where Able muses that it is located sort of sideways
to Mythgarthr. Of course, he might very well be wrong
about that!

One thing I kept thinking throughout was that Gene
Wolfe was the Most High God, as is every author in
terms of his/her fictional creations. But I got the
feeling that Wolfe was setting up an analogy between
his creations and God's -- that we are fictional
characters in the mind of God who may, through our
actions, take on a reality we might not otherwise
possess. I wonder if Able, at the end of the novel,
is not being taken by Michael to Able's America but,
rather, to ours . . . That just as, in the Catholic
faith, virtuous souls receive the reward of rising to
a realer existence, in Heaven, so, too, does Able rise
(with Disiri)to an incarnation outside the pages of
the novel.

Paul

Argues that Wolfe's divine hierarchy in The Wizard Knight is confusing, since several gods sit on each level except the highest and lowest. Asks why Kulilli, the original inhabitant of Aelfrice, is not worshipped by the dragons of Muspel, and whether descent from Angr determines who may be worshipped; this responds to David Lebling's remarks on Arnthor as god of Mythgarthr.
questions · close-textual-reading · theories: Descent from Angr determines who is worshipped

It seems that there can be multiple gods on every
level except the highest and the lowest. I find
Wolfe's scheme confusing, I must admit. Kulilli is
the original inhabitant of Aelfrice. Why isn't she
worshipped by the dragons of Muspel? Nor does it seem
that the Most Low God worships the dragons. And
though the Angrborne live on Mythgarthr, they are not
worshipped by the Aelf. I wonder if descent from Angr
has anything to do with who is and isn't a proper
object of worship.

2005-01-06 Dave Rolsky replying to David Lebling #

Argues that Arnthor is a god of Aelfrice living within Mythgarthr rather than the god of Mythgarthr, which would be the Valfather, and that Kulilli may be an Aelfrice god depending on whether she belongs to Aelfrice or Mythgarthr. Responds to David Lebling's paraphrase of Crush's question about the message Able carries, objecting that its phrasing 'the god of Mythgarthr' is confusing and refining the distinction between 'of' and 'in'.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Arnthor is an Aelfrice god living in Mythgarthr; The Valfather is the god of Mythgarthr; Kulilli may be a god of Aelfrice

That's a terribly confusing phrasing. Arnthor is not the god _of_ (over) Mythgarthr, he's a god of Aelfrice who lives _in_ Mythgarthr. I think the god of Mythgarthr is the Valfather, clearly, though he's just one of many.

Kulilli may be another god of Aelfrice, as James Wynn suggested, although it's not clear to me if she is _of_ Aelfrice or Mythgarthr. I think it'd have to be the latter for her to be a god of Aelfrice.

-dave

2005-01-06 Dave Lebling replying to stilskin #

Argues that worship in The Wizard Knight depends mainly on each being's cosmic level, with lower levels less moral and less likely to worship the proper god. Replies to stilskin's confusion about the gods' scheme by offering reasons: the Aelf's shame at being made from leftovers, Muspel dragons' rebellion, and the Most Low God's self-worship.
proposes · theology-and-religion · myth-and-structure · theories: Worship follows the cosmic level a being lives on; Aelf disdain worship because they were made from leftovers; Muspel dragons attempt to displace higher rulers

I think a more important component of who is worshipped and by whom is the level on which they live. As you go lower in level, the inhabitants are less likely to be moral beings, though Able admits there is a range on each level (from the Valfather to Lothur in Skai, for example, not to mention the Giants of Skai). The less moral, the less likely to worship the proper god for your level.

Given that Kulili created the Aelf, their position is somewhat unusual. I can imagine several reasons why they wouldn't worship the Angrborn, including the Aelf's apparent desire to conceal their true natures by mimicking humans, which indicates they are ashamed of being created from leftovers and thus would disdain worship of other beings so created.

The dragons of Muspel would be extremely unlikely to worship the god appropriate for them, and in fact attempt to displace the gods/rulers of the next two higher levels. The Most Low God worships himself and states that he is also the Most High God.

We know little of Kleos, but obviously they are patterned on angels and all faithfully worship the Most High God. Those that did not were expelled, if the mythology parallels Christianity, as it does in most instances.

-- Dave, aka Vizcacha

2005-01-08 Andrew Bollen replying to stilskin #

Proposes that Dream is a shared realm linking Mythgathr, Earth and Aelfrice, and argues that the Aelf swapped Art and Able through dreams, with Mag's American-born dream-self splitting into a Myth copy. Grounds the case in Art's dreams of the ambulance and Able, Escan's left-behind copy, and Art's remark about his mother's dream of Aelfrice.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Art and Able swapped by the Aelf through dreams; Myth-Mag is a copy left by American-Mag; Mag is the mother of Berthold, Able, Ben and Art

I think Dream is a shared realm, as Crush says. I think it is the key to Mag and Able/Art.

First of all my understanding of Able/Art: they have been swapped, in some way, by the Aelf. The original, "real" Able goes to America; Art takes Able's place in Mythgathr. I think this is very clear: when Art dreams of the ambulance, he knows in his dream that it carries the "real Able".

Mythgathr and Earth are connected though Dream. Art dreams of Able in America on several occasions. Dream exists on the same plane as Mythgathr, and must be connected to Aelfrice as well.

The Aelf have power over dreams. Baki (can't remember) bring dreams to both Idnn and Art. From this we can guess how the Art/Able swap happened. Art dreamed in the Downs and the Aelf enticed his dream-self down to Aelfrice. Able dreamt and had his dream-self sent back to Art's body on Earth via Dream. Art's dream-self replaced Able's in Able's body, which was later sent back to Myth via Parka's cave.

As to why the Aelf did this: I speculated earlier that the Aelf wanted an American, rather than a local, as their messenger.

As to why Able/Art: because they have the same mother, presumably facilitating the swap. We have to assume that Mag and Art's mother are in some sense the same person; not least because Art says he thinks Mag was both the mother of Berthold and Able, and of himself and Ben (p236).

But the situation is not straightforward. Mag recognizes that Art is not actually her son Able. Able seems to recognize that Mag is not actually his mother. She seems to have no memory of Art as such, but she does appear to remember her "girlhood in America" (p335). Although her message in the green glass tube opens thus: "Mag is my name *here*" (p236), implying that she has another name elsewhere (ie America), we cannot assume that she has simply travelled from America to Mythgarthr.

I think the solution to this conundrum might be suggested by what happens to Escan: he leaves behind in Aelfrice a copy of himself (p424). I think the American-Mag dreamed of Aelfrice as a child and left a copy of herself there, which continued on as Myth-Mag, the eventual mother of Berthold and Able, while her original dream-self returned to America, to marry and bear Ben and Art.

I think we know that Art's mother did dream of Aelfrice. Writing of his descent to Aelfrice in search of Toug he says, "How far to the dream my mother had?" (p314)
