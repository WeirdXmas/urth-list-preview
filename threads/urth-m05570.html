Fuligin invented

Urth list · 16 messages · 2008-01-16 to 2008-01-17

2008urthThe Book of the New SunLane HaygoodMatthew KeeleyMatthew MalthouseJosh GellerDaniel D JonesGwern BranwenSteven BowenDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: Posters on the Urth list asked whether Terminus Est is based on a real executioner's sword and whether a mercury channel filled with fuligin could be built into a blade. Gwern0 and Matthew Keeley tied the sword to historical 'swords of justice' such as the Higgins Armory example, while Matthew noted that no reference to a blade of Terminus Est's length had turned up. Lane Haygood argued from metallurgy that a hollowed blade would lose its shock-absorbing core and that no known metal fits, while gwern0 and brunians countered that fullers can be pressed in and that a brass tube with a screw cap could work. The thread ended in friendly drift, with Matthew offering photos of his Viking sword and a museum visit, and no real agreement on the metallurgy.
  • Lane Haygood: a mercury channel would structurally weaken the blade, and no known metal fits Terminus Est
  • gwern0: fullers can be pressed or bent in, so a sealed fluid channel is not obviously impossible
  • brunians: a thick blade with a bored brass tube and screw cap could hold mercury
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the sword may be an unknown or advanced metal, with 'steel' a translation factor
  • Matthew Keeley: Terminus Est is probably based on a real European hand-and-a-half sword, and he plans to examine a museum sword

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Books, libraries and writing, Myth and folklore sources

2008-01-17 Lane Haygood replying to Matthew Keeley #

Argues that a mercury channel in a sword's blade, as the Fuligin-related discussion imagines, would be structurally unworkable, since removing the softer central steel would weaken the blade. Grounds this in metallurgy: hard edges are brittle, softer core absorbs shock, and a channel would strip out that core; offers to ask smiths on a swordsmanship forum.
disputes · genre-and-craft

I've never heard of an actual mercury channel in a sword. I can ask on a swordsmanship forum I frequent (where several smiths post) to see if it's feasible. From what I understand of sword structure, it's not. You need the center part of the sword to be the strongest part, and that usually means it needs to be softer and more ductile than the surrounding steel. Cutting a big channel out of the middle of it might weaken its structural integrity to the point where it would be useless for cutting.

The idea is that the edges, the parts that need to be sharp, should be harder. When steel hardens, it becomes more brittle. Brittle steel will sharpen and hold an edge. But it's also weak. If you made the whole sword out of really hard steel it would chip and shatter whenever it made contact with something. So you make the non-edge part of the sword out of softer, more flexible metal so that it will actually absorb shock. But cutting a big channel out of the middle would remove a lot of the shock-absorbing material.

Lane

When it was described, the way I thought it was done was like this: swords sometimes have 'blood grooves' (fullers), right? This produces a slight volume down the middle which you could fill up - maybe you could weld a flat strip down the length of the blade, or fold over a section of the blade and create a sealed space that way. 2008-01-17 Lane Haygood replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Terminus Est cannot plausibly be a super-metal, since no known metal combines the hardness, weight and flexibility a cutting sword needs, though magic or advanced technology might explain it. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's suggestion that the sword's metal is a translation factor, and adds a sword-maker's account of fullers, the hollow grooves in blades, to show why hollowing weakens shock absorption.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Terminus Est is an advanced super-metal

That's one of the things commonly debated in sword-aficionado circles (metallurgy, not Gene Wolfe). The problem is something on a more basic level -- there is a constant, almost-perpetual trade-off between the properties that make a sword useful. The harder something is (e.g., diamond) the more brittle it is in larger blades. But the softer something is, the more of a chance it will set at an unnatural angle or be impossible to sharpen.

Even with metals other than steel that display a greater strength (e.g., titanium) there are other, associated defects. Titanium is lighter than steel, which means that although you can create a sharper, stronger sword out of it, it won't do you much of anything insofar as cutting is concerned because there's insufficient weight behind the blade to cut. In order for TE to be Hiero-something technology, it would have to be made of a metal that surpasses the current limitations of our understanding, not just our ability.

Which is not to say that in the context of a world where time travel and the supernatural is possible that it would be unfeasible for TE to be made according to magical/advanced technological rules (I'm reminded of Heinlein's quote that one man's sorcery is another's engineering), but rather that there is no good reason based on our current metallurgical science to believe that such a material is feasible.

In re: fullers

A fuller (erroneously called a blood groove since it does nothing with blood) is usually just hammered in to the blade. It's done to either reduce weight or to change the cross-section shape of the sword (which is useful for various purposes). The reason why I'm skeptical (and I am by no means the expert; I've asked the experts and am waiting on a response) is that the hollow space in the middle of the blade would necessarily take away from the shock-absorbing value of having a mass of softer metal in the middle of the blade. If you get a chance, go handle a longsword or two-hander from a collector, or see if you have a friend that has a functional replica made by a reputable company (e.g., Angus Trim, Arms and Armor, Del Tin, etc.). Watch the sword flex and look closely at the way it's constructed in the middle of the blade. I can take pictures of my Viking sword at home (which has a very wide fuller) and show you. It's a pressed/hammered fuller.

Lane

> I've never heard of an actual mercury channel in a sword. I can ask on
> a swordsmanship forum I frequent (where several smiths post) to see if
> it's feasible. From what I understand of sword structure, it's not.
> You need the center part of the sword to be the strongest part, and
> that usually means it needs to be softer and more ductile than the
> surrounding steel. Cutting a big channel out of the middle of it might
> weaken its structural integrity to the point where it would be useless
> for cutting.
>
> The idea is that the edges, the parts that need to be sharp, should be
> harder. When steel hardens, it becomes more brittle. Brittle steel
> will sharpen and hold an edge. But it's also weak. If you made the
> whole sword out of really hard steel it would chip and shatter
> whenever it made contact with something. So you make the non-edge part
> of the sword out of softer, more flexible metal so that it will
> actually absorb shock. But cutting a big channel out of the middle
> would remove a lot of the shock-absorbing material.
