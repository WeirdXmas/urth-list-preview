Silkhorn's mission after BotSS

Urth list · 5 messages · 2004-10-05 to 2004-10-05

2004urthThe Book of the Long SunThe Book of the Short SunDan'l Danehy-OakesJames WynnChris PChristopher Culver
The thread: Christopher Culver proposed that Silk's departure at the end of The Book of the Short Sun is an optimistic finale, with Silk as a galactic bringer of the Outsider's good news, grounded in a Cambridge Companion essay calling him a fisher of men. James Wynn argued that Silk's return heals the damaged pantheon and that the ship will travel on to other stars, but then disputed any clear religious target, noting the Neighbors and Inhumi are poor missionary fields and the text says nothing about conversion. Chris countered that Silk left so the messenger would not overshadow the message, planting a seed rather than solving Blue's problems, and said he never saw the ending as negative. The thread ended without a clear resolution, with the disagreement centering on whether the ending reads as hopeful or cruel.
  • Christopher Culver: Silk's departure is an optimistic finale, a galactic fisher of men spreading the Outsider's good news
  • James Wynn: Silk's return heals the pantheon and aims to bring the gods to recognize the Outsider, but the text gives no sign of a religious mission
  • Chris: Silk left so the messenger would not overshadow the message; he planted a seed and the ending is not negative

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Gods, angels and the Increate

2004-10-05 Christopher Culver #

Proposes that Silk's departure at the end of The Book of the Short Sun is an optimistic finale, with Silk as a galactic bringer of the Outsider's good news rather than a cruel abandonment of Blue. Grounds this in a Cambridge Companion to Science Fiction essay calling Silk a fisher of men, and in the idea that the Holy Spirit's work continues.
proposes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Silk is a galactic bringer of good news; Silk's departure is an optimistic finale

The relatively-new CAMBRIDGE COMPANION TO SCIENCE FICTION includes an essay which dedicates about a page and a half to Wolfe's solar works. The overview isn't terribly in-depth, but I found it most interesting that the writer sees Silk as going on to become "a fisher of men" among the stars at the end of the Book of the Short Sun.

This changed my view of the ending of the BotSS entirely. I originally thought it was an inexplicable and cruel abandoning of Blue by Silkhorn after saving some of his buddies from it (as in the traditional belief of the Harrowing of Hell). Now, however, I can see that perhaps Wolfe wanted it to be an optimistic finale in which worship of the Outsider will spread all over the universe.

Sure, Blue is abandoned by Silkhorn himself, but there are still those who knew Silk, or read Horn's book, so they know of the Outsider, and monotheism might continue on, especially if (as Wolfe's Catholic faith would have it) the Holy Spirit continues its work eternally.

Does the idea of Silk as galactic bringer of good news make anyone else appreciate the ending of BotSS more?

Christopher Culver

2004-10-05 James Wynn replying to Christopher Culver #

Argues that Silk's return to the Whorl heals the damaged pantheon, with Pas, Echidna, Scylla, Molpe and Hierax standing for the gods and Silk aiming to make them recognize the Outsider. Grounds this in the returning characters Silk, Nettle, Seawrack, Marble and Oreb as an analogy for the gods, and suggests the ship will travel on to other stars.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Returning characters heal the damaged Whorl pantheon; Silk aims to establish the Outsider's pre-eminence

Hmmm.. Who will he take the "good news" too? The Neighbors and Inhumi hardly needed whatever theological messages the Short Sun Narrator had to offer.

Rather becoming a mere foil for the Whorl pantheon (which in fact, the Whorl cannot live without - they're are modules of the Mainframe) it seemed to me that the characters who return are supposed to "heal" the pantheon in some way (I don't now exactly how). After all, Pas was almost rubbed by his wife and at least three children. He returns in pieces really -- and is it likely he's yet entirely whole? Then he proceeds to erase Echidna, Scylla, Molpe, and Hierax.
Who returns to the Whorl? Silk, Nettle, Seawrack, Marble, and Oreb. A nearly perfect analogy of the damaged gods (I grant that Nettle is a bit of a wedge-job).

Naturally, Silk's goal would be have the pantheon and their worshipers recognize the pre-eminence of the Outsider.

You are probably right about where the Whorl is going, however. It seems obvious to me after a year of reflection that the ship was always expected to continue on from star to star. Otherwise, what would happen to all the "non-Cargo" on the ship? Wolfe has said there will be no more sequels to the Silk segment, but I think it would be very interesting to see a couple stories on the crew of the Whorl.

~ Crush

2004-10-05 James Wynn replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Disputes the idea that Silkhorn's mission in The Book of the Short Sun has a clear religious target, arguing the planets Neighbors and Inhumi are poor examples of a missionary field. Notes the text says nothing about the Neighbors' religion or about any conversion to the Outsider, and responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's literal reading of the 'many mansions' verse.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Silkhorn's mission is a religious mission to the Neighbors

Why not? But if so, the planets Neighbors and Inhumi do not strike me as obvious exemplars of the missionary field that the Short Sun Narrator is supposed to evangelize. We know nothing of the religion of the Neighbors (it does not seem likely to me that Mother was an object of worship) and if they were converted to the worship of the Outsider (the Narrator excluded) it is not so stated in the text AFAIK.

~ Crush

2004-10-05 Chris P replying to Christopher Culver #

Argues that Silk's departure at the end of The Book of the Short Sun was meant to keep the messenger from overshadowing the message, so he returned to plant a seed rather than solve Blue's problems himself. Responds to Christopher Culver's quoted claim, from a critical essay, that Silk becomes a fisher of men among the stars, which Culver found cruel; this poster never saw the ending as negative.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk leaves so the messenger does not eclipse the message

I always thought of his leaving as a way to ensure that the man/messenger didn't eclipse the message. He was back, really, to plant a seed more than to fix all of Blue's problems himself. So I didn't really get that negative connotation in the first place.
