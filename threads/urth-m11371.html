The thread: Ryan Dunn asked why Severian, as Autarch, uses the collective "we" in speech while his narrator self never does. Son of Witz read the royal "we" as a performed role and the narrator's downplaying of power as a character trait. Jeff Wilson linked the "we" to the many minds hosted within one man, suggesting Thecla might narrate, and later argued the narration integrates Thecla's memories, citing the ten-year gap and shifts into her viewpoint. Dunn disputed that the plural is tied only to the Autarch role, arguing it stems from Severian's state after consuming Thecla. The thread ended without resolution, with the disagreement between role and absorbed identity left open.

Proposes that Severian as Autarch uses the royal "we" as a performed role, while as narrator he downplays his time in power, which reads as a character trait. Responds to Ryan Dunn's observation about the shift in pronouns and notes Wolfe chose not to write an Autarch-era book.

Interesting.
I hadn't thought of that.
I guess that Severian plays the Autarch as he should, using the Royal "We" befitting the position (with delicious irony). But when he is writing his Memoir, it is Severian's story, and he minimizes any discussion of his time as Autarch. Come to think of it, this is a compelling character trait. Of all the things in his life, he doesn't relish in having obtained the highest position a person could hope to attain. It's interesting that Wolfe doesn't write those stories, when an Autarch Severian book would sell really well.

2010-12-03 Jeff Wilson replying to Ryan Dunn #

Argues that Severian's Autarch "we" is a royal convention that, in the Autarchy's case, reflects the many minds hosted within one man, and that the shift from "I" can hide Thecla as narrator. Responds to Ryan Dunn's question about why the Autarch speaks as "we" while the narrator never does, citing the Autarch's hosted identities as the reason.
answers · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Autarch's we reflects the hosted minds; Thecla occasionally narrates as I

As you know, Bob, it was somewhat customary for western sovereigns to do this, "we" including the notional persons of the state, the crown, the royal consort in unity, etc. and "I" the natural person who happens to hold the above positions, speaking in some lesser capacity. The Autarchy takes this about as far as literally possible, the Autarch being the minds of hundreds of actual people hosted by the humble Commonwealther who was called to that office.

It is also a clever bit of misdirection, since "I" occasionally becomes Thecla the Narrator without either of them noticing.

2010-12-04 Ryan Dunn replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that Severian's use of the collective first-person plural in The Book of the New Sun is tied to his state after consuming Thecla, not only to his later role as Autarch. Responds to Ryan Dunn's point by noting that Severian as narrator remains objective about his condition while writing with all the memories in his mind.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian speaks in collective voice before becoming Autarch

No, he speaks with the collective voice before he is Autarch, after consuming Thecla, to my knowledge. No?

I think the peculiarity is how objective Severian the Narrator is about his own condition. He is writing all of this as Autarch, with all the memories in his brain. He is able to sort them and explain their differences within his mind this way, yet in conversation he almost explicitly speaks in what I guess you would call first-person plural.

...ryan

2010-12-04 Jeff Wilson replying to Ryan Dunn #

Argues that Severian's narration reflects an integration of Thecla's memories and mind after he consumes her, with the analeptic making multiplicity concrete. Supports this by pointing to the ten years between events and writing, and to passages where the manuscript shifts into Thecla's point of view without transition, such as a childhood memory of her father's cavalry.
supports · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Thecla's mind is integrated into Severian after consuming her

The analeptic makes it more actual rather than notional to be multiple people, though there is an identifiable point where she begins to be less of a separate person and more integrated.

The books are written after he's had ten years to fully integrate the minds...but there are still a couple of places in the ms where he begins writing things from Thecla's POV without a formal transition, like when they recall childhood and mentions her father's cavalry.
