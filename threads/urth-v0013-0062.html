Three Fingers

Urth list · 4 messages · 1998-06-15 to 1998-06-16

1998urthadam louis stephanidesDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: The thread asks whether the suited figures at the end of Wolfe's 'Three Fingers' are Disney characters in masks, with Michael Mauss as a metaphorical Mickey Mouse. Adam Louis Stephanides argues the masked figures are real people revealed as paunchy businessmen and businesswomen, and that Michael stands closer to the mythic sources than the corporate suits. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes concedes a possible error but holds the suited figures are the Queen, Captain and Wolf in this world, so the failed poisoned bonbon fits the villains' repeated failures. Stephanides responds by questioning whether that reading fits Danehy-Oakes's own remark that a pipe is just a pipe. The exchange ends without agreement, with each side restating its reading.
  • adam louis stephanides: Michael Mauss is a metaphorical Mickey Mouse, real people hide behind the Disney masks, and the suits are corporate businesspeople
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the suited figures are the Queen, Captain and Wolf in this world, and the failed bonbon fits the villains' failures

Themes: Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Myth and folklore sources, Power, rulers and politics

1998-06-15 adam louis stephanides #

Argues that Michael Mauss in Three Fingers is metaphorically Mickey Mouse, because he rather than the corporate suits in the final scene is in touch with the mythical sources Mickey derives from. Grounds this in the unmasking scene, extending an earlier afterthought that answered a poster's remark about the Mickey Mouse theory and reserpine.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theories: Michael Mauss is metaphorically Mickey Mouse

My remark about Michael Mauss being Mickey Mouse in a metaphorical sense was just an afterthought, but thinking it over I realize that it is true and important. Michael Mauss, not the "suits" we see in the final scene (whether real or not) is the true "owner" of Mickey Mouse, since he, not they, is in touch with the mythical springs from which he derives, as the unmasking scene indicates.

Of course, you may all think I've already spent too much time on another of Wolfe's "oneiric jeux d'esprit," to use the phrase alga quoted from Clute.

--Adam

1998-06-15 adam louis stephanides #

Argues that the Disney figures in 'Three Fingers' are not pretending but are real people in masks, revealed at the ending as paunchy businessmen and businesswomen in suits. Answers Dan'l Danehy-Oakes by citing Hook's bonbon threat, Michael's swallowing, and the Wicked Queen's final remark, granting that Disney's trademark zeal adds resonance.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Disney figures are businesspeople in masks

"Captain Hook" tells Michael he will be suffocated with the bonbons, but Michael has no trouble swallowing the bonbon, and the "Wicked Queen's" final remark shows that that's what's supposed to happen.

Because they remove their masks, and are revealed to be "paunchy [men/women] in a business suit." This is all plainly depicted in the story; unless you've misremembered the ending, I'm missing your point.

To be sure, knowing the determination with which Disney defends its copyrights/trademarks adds resonance to the story. (I'm tempted to make a joke about how I would rather have Captain Hook et al. after me than Disney's lawyers, but I'll refrain.)

--Adam

1998-06-16 adam louis stephanides #

Questions whether Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's claim that the suited figures are the Queen, Hook and Wolf in disguise, or their manifestations, fits his earlier remark that a pipe is just a pipe. Restates his position back to him as a paradox, quoting his words that the suits are the Queen, Captain and Wolf in this-world.
questions · theories: The suited figures are the Queen, Hook and Wolf manifested

So your argument is that the characters who are portrayed on the surface as paunchy men/women in business suits disguised as the Queen, Hook, and Wolf are really the Queen, Hook and Wolf disguised, or manifesting themselves as, paunchy men/women in business suits disguised as the Queen, Hook, and Wolf?

So where does this leave your earlier contention that "sometimes a pipe is just a pipe"?

--Adam
