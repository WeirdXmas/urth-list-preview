The thread: Stuart Hamm asked for a picture of Terminus Est, and the thread turned into a long physical debate over whether the sword's mercury-filled channel could work without weakening the blade. John Smith, Gwern Branwen, brunians, Tim Walters, Kerry Benton and Gary traded proposals such as I-beam sections, twin tubes, one-way valves and vacuum chambers, while Adam Thornton and Dave Lebling argued that the setting's advanced technology should license a stronger sword. Later posts shifted to whether the man-edge and woman-edge distinction makes the blade asymmetrical and whether the quillions are carved heads. The thread ended without agreement, drifting from engineering into textual readings.
  • John Smith: mercury channel would weaken the blade and any vacuum or air-filled design would fail
  • Gwern Branwen: an I-beam or double-channel design with ordinary air would hold up
  • brunians: a brass tube with a pumped vacuum and a pump nipple could work
  • Adam Thornton: the setting's advanced tech suggests the sword is more than skilled smithing, and the quillions are worked heads
  • Dave Lebling: the sword is probably not really strong, since BotNS treats medieval-seeming things as advanced tech
  • Gary: a mercury channel could be engineered strong enough, and a single vacuum channel along the centre of balance would work best

Themes: Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Names and their meanings, Myth and folklore sources

Argues that the mercury channel in Terminus Est would weaken the blade regardless of construction, and that the air in an empty channel would resist the mercury's free flow, so the channel would need a vacuum that is hard to craft and weakens the sword further. the empty part of the channel is filled with air, the
pressure of the air would impede the mercury from
flowing freely when the sword descends. The empty
area needs to be a vacuum. This would be hard to
craft and would further weaken the sword.

2008-07-02 Gary replying to Lane Haygood #

Argues that a mercury-channel sword like Terminus Est could be engineered strong enough despite swordsmiths' objections, comparing it to bridge girders and invoking Clarke's law of prediction. Responds to Lane Haygood's quotation of a swordsmith's doubts about the mercury channel in the Terminus Est story.
disputes · genre-and-craft

One of Clarke's laws of prediction should apply to swordsmiths as well as scientists, eh?
"When a distinguished but elderly scientist states that something is possible, he is almost certainly right. When he states that something is impossible, he is very probably wrong."

Unquestionably it may be weaker than a solid-core sword, but this doesn't mean it can't be engineered to be sufficiently strong - as others have pointed out we do for bridge girders. This is especially true if it's built around a mercury-and-vaccuum-filled tube of unobtanium - I'm sure it's true that it can't be done to give the wonderful combination of properties seen in Terminus Est using current techniques & materials. And I'm very willing to assume they can make a "perfect" seal around a sufficient vaccuum using some arcane machine.

Gary.

2008-07-02 John Smith replying to Stuart Hamm #

Argues that Gwern's proposed air-filled circular tube for the mercury channel in Terminus Est would fail, because mercury would pool and flow down both channels on each stroke and compress the air. Grounds the objection in physics and in the text's description of a single channel with a river of hydragyrum in The Shadow of the Torturer.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving

Gwern has a clever suggestion about the mercury
channel in Terminus Est:

"Well, why do we need a vacuum at all,
when we could just have air in it like normal and
avoid displacement
problems by making our channel a circular tube."

This is a good idea, but I don't think it would work.
When you raise the sword, the mercury would pool at
the bottom in both channels. Then on the downstroke,
the mercury would flow down both channels, resulting
in the same air compression problem. We might want
the mercury to flow down one channel and the air to
flow up the other, but mercury will simply follow the
laws of physics.

The text also indicates a single channel:
"There is a channel in the spine of her blade, and in
it runs a river of hydragyrum" (Shadow, XIV).

Best wishes,

Jack

2008-07-02 Gwern Branwen replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that Terminus Est's mercury-filled blade needs no exotic forging, proposing instead an I-beam with a covered channel or a double-channel design with ordinary air. Responds to Jeff Wilson's earlier mechanical explanation, building on it and refining it with a simpler alternative to the vacuum seal Gary doubted.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Terminus Est is an I-beam with a covered channel; Terminus Est uses a double-channel design with air

On 2008.07.02 17:00:41 -0500, Jeff Wilson <[email]> scribbled 0.2K characters:

Honestly, I think everyone is overcomplicating Terminus Est.

Everything about its construction is explicable through simple mechanisms.

It has a channel running down the center but is still strong? Simple, it has an I-beam shape and one void is covered up to make a channel.

It requires an exotic method of forging which creates a vaccuum in it to let the mercury slosh around? Well, why do we need a vacuum at all, when we could just have air in it like normal and avoid displacement problems by making our channel a circular tube.

(This suggestion leads to a nice design where we have two channels, one on either side of the I-beam, and they are linked by a hole at the top and bottom; even better, a double-channel design would hide the I-beam shape and make it basically the flat rectangular prism shape we expect of a sword of justice.)

2008-07-02 Gary replying to Gwern Branwen #

Argues that the mercury channel in Severian's sword would work best along the centre of balance as a single channel with a vacuum, and that the blade may do more than training alone explains. Responds to Gwern Branwen's joking remark about the I-beam shape by reasoning from physics and from the sword's handling, citing Severian's description of the blade's action.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Severian's sword has a single central mercury channel with vacuum; The sword's blade is more than Severian's training

I like the Area 51 thermometer on my deck... I assume the supposed "bluebird" decoration is actually the depiction of an alien overlord.

I really think the channel needs to be along the centre-of-balance - the centre, assuming a symmetrical sword. Otherwise the channel would make it much harder to wield, not easier. So instead of an I-beam, use a tube... or a square rod if you prefer, with the sides being your "I-beam" equivalent, and the channel within.

As others have said, although the circular channel (to skip the vaccuum) seems appealing, it can't work as well as a vaccuum. Unless the mercury is all on one side (one channel if you will), some of the mercury will be working against the rest... it'll never be perfectly balanced, so the mercury will certainly be displaced, but not nearly as efficiently as with a single channel and a vaccuum. But it may work well enough to do the job.

As Transentient says, Severian may be deceiving us, but I think the way he describe's the sword action requires there to be more to the blade than just his training. He clearly feels this blade is unusual, and though the mechanism may not really be a "hydragyrum" channel, but something is giving an effect akin to this.

Clearly it's not _needed_ in a head-lopper, but it's a cool little feature, serving no other purpose than to make the sword more interesting to the reader I think. As one who's never lifted a heavy sword, but lifted many other long, heavy things, I can see how it's easier to raise without wobbling when the weight is near one's body, and how it has greater angular momentum on the downstroke with weight redistributed to the tip. It would take practice to get used to, and gain advantage from, the weight shift.
