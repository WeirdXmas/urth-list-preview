The thread: Readers discussed historical grounding in Wolfe's Latro books, starting from a post on Latro's name and sword. Alice Turner argued that the politicians and royalty are real historical figures while his companions are invented, and that the Greek gods he meets fit a time before Rome adopted the Greek pantheon. Michael Straight disputed the date, citing a Classics professor who said Latin then was only a farmers' dialect, and suggested Latro might be an impressed farmer. Derek Bell, unsure of his memory, recalled Latro fighting Ulysses, and the thread ended with the historical questions unresolved.
  • Alice Turner: the real and invented figures are mixed, and the Greek gods show the setting predates Roman adoption of the Greek pantheon
  • Michael Straight: Latin is anachronistic for the early date and Latro could be an impressed farmer

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Myth and folklore sources, Unreliable narration

1997-09-16 Alice Turner #

Argues that Latro's surrounding characters, politicians and royalty, are real historical figures while his close companions are invented, and that the gods he meets are Greek rather than Roman because the Romans had not yet adopted the Greek pantheon. Responds to a prior post on Latro's name and the magic sword by offering historical grounding for the Latro books' setting.
informs · source-and-allusion

Plus, there are a lot of real people and real events in the books. Latro's nearest and dearest are fictional, of course, but the "important" periphal characters, politicians, royalty and such (as opposed to farmers and the like), are not. Latro gets caught up with these people, never quite understanding his role. The gods are the Greek versions; though Latro is Roman, this is early enough so that the Romans had not yet adopted (and adapted) the Greek pantheon---and of course the gods would necessarily be local, anyway.

Eheu fugaces!
-alga-

1997-09-16 Michael Straight #

Argues that the Latin Latro writes in does not fit the novel's early date, since a Classics professor says Latin then was only a dialect spoken by a few farmers near Rome. Responds to Alice Turner's quoted claim that Latro is a Latin named Lucius by suggesting he could be a farmer impressed into service, and asks whether the Phoenician-speaking people he rescues at the end undercut this.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · source-and-allusion · theories: Latro is a farmer impressed into military service

Spoilers for Soldier Series...

I have a friend who is a Classics professor who specializes in this time period. I thought he might enjoy these books, but when I described it to him, he said that Latro's chronology doesn't work. At the time of the events of the novel, the closest thing to Latin in the world was a dialect spoken only by a few farmers in a ten-mile radius somewhere near what is now Rome, and even then, who knows how close it was to what we know as Latin.

The best I could think of to make it work is that Latro is somehow one of those farmers impressed into military service or gone off to seek his fortune as a mercenary, except isn't it implied that the language of the people Latro rescues at the end (Phonecians?) is the same Latin he's been writing?

-Rostrum
