The thread: The thread asked whether Wolfe's Long Sun contains a Jesus-like figure who brings salvation, and whether Briah is a different universe that could hold a Christ. Son of Witz and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued for a salvation reading, with Witz seeing a gnostic story in which the Outsider saves humanity from the false Whorl, while Lee Berman held there is a Jesus-like figure but no salvation. The discussion drifted into Christian doctrine: grace versus salvation, whether salvation is possible only through Christ, anonymous Christianity, and Judaism's relation to the Torah. Stockhoff and Gerry Quinn repeatedly disputed whether absence of salvation in the text means it is absent from the universe, and no side conceded the core question. The thread ended in impasse, with Stockhoff's late speculation about pruned universes admitted to be unsupported by the text.
  • Son of Witz: Long Sun has an unambiguous gnostic salvation metaphor with the Outsider as Christ-physician
  • Lee Berman: Long Sun has Jesus-like references but no salvation, and Briah is a different universe
  • Gerry Quinn: Wolfe's cyclic cosmogony can accommodate a Christ-like figure, and absence of salvation in the text is not absence of salvation
  • David Stockhoff: Salvation in Wolfe's cosmogony must run through the Incarnation, leaving only grace for Severian, and unmentioned salvation is an unjustified l
  • Matthew Weber: Grace as Christian gift through Christ's sacrifice, with the Torah as a debit-ledger morality
  • Jeff Wilson: Hebrew scriptures contain forgiveness, and Christ's sacrifice saves those who never hear the gospel

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Catholic faith and sacraments

disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Whorl is a gnostic false creation; Outsider is Christ the physician saving humanity; Silk was enlightened, not possessed by the Outsider

Wait. There is a very unambiguous salvation metaphor here. The Whorl is a sort of gnotic false creation with a false pantheon. God sends his healing energy, Logos- Christ the physician - the Outsider to usher them out of the false creation into a new world free of the Demiurgos of the Whorl, Pas. How much clearer could the salvation metaphor be? The humanity of the whorl has no future if they don't leave it. The outsider saved them, acting through silk, who was enlightened, not posessed by the Outsider.

Man Lee, you should read Phillip K. Dick's VALIS and Divine Invasion for some great sci-fi permutations of this idea if you haven't already. ~witz

2010-12-16 Matthew Weber replying to David Stockhoff #

Explains the Christian sense of grace as a gift that frees believers from the consequences of sin through Christ's sacrifice, contrasting it with the Torah's ledger-like morality. Responds to David Stockhoff's use of the term grace by clarifying the concept of salvation and noting that it did not exist before Christ's passion.
informs · theology-and-religion

If I may (as one who does theology from time to time)...

I'm trying to understand the sense in which David is using the term "grace". I find it difficult to separate from the concept of salvation, because salvation is only possible through grace. In other words, it is by the grace of God that we are saved, not through any striving of our own (although obviously we need to respond to that grace in some way).

Christians believe that salvation comes only through Jesus Christ. This is not to say that Jews, Muslims, virtuous pagans, etc cannot be saved; it is, however, to say that whether they are aware of it or not, it is Jesus who saves them. Salvation consists in being freed from the just consequences of our sins, due to the sacrifice of Christ on the cross. We are no longer subject to punishment for our misdeeds, because Another has taken that punishment on our behalf. The word "grace" denotes that this is a gift, not something we are expected to earn; our good works are not a means of winning this gift, but a natural response of gratitude--you could say "thank-you notes to God" if it doesn't make you want to throw up. (NB : I am aware that ultramontane Catholicism and five-point Calvinism alike will find points of disagreement in this paragraph)

Grace in the Christian sense doesn't seem to have existed before Christ's passion and resurrection. The Torah seems to embody an accountant's approach to morality, with each transgression appearing in the debit column and requiring the sacrifice of a corresponding asset. In comparison, the sacrifice of Christ is a general debt amnesty, a super-Jubilee if you will.

I hope this clarifies rather than muddies. If the latter case obtains, feel free to ignore!

2010-12-16 Jeff Wilson replying to David Stockhoff #

Explains the theological concept of anonymous Christianity, arguing that Christ's sacrifice saves people who never hear the gospel, including innocent pagans and unbaptized infants. Responds to David Stockhoff's remark that readers should consult theologians before judging Wolfe's probable beliefs, offering the doctrine as background for that discussion.
informs · theology-and-religion

The concept of "anonymous Christianity" is built on the precept that Christ's sacrifice is sufficient to save everyone for all times, and that His transcendent grace is not restricted by human ignorance of the gospel. This is held to allow innocent pagans, unbaptized infants, etc. to constructively embrace salvation without exposure to the gospel or the capacity to intellectually comprehend it. There's a sort of darker side to it, in that missionaries who bring the good news give people who otherwise wouldn't the chance to affirmatively embrace it but also to overtly reject it.

2010-12-16 David Stockhoff replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that the question of whether entire universes were left in the dark, which Jeff Wilson's anonymous-Christianity quote raises, should be discussed, and proposes that infinite universes are ruthlessly pruned like natural selection, as Severian drowned Urth. proposes · theology-and-religion · theories: Infinite universes are ruthlessly pruned like natural selection

I think this issue is a critical point. Certainly something to be addressed in discussing whether entire universes may have been left in the dark by chance or intent.

Personally, I envision infinite universes which are ruthlessly pruned as needed, as Severian drowned Urth, in a process akin to natural selection. I understand if this idea is distasteful to some, and I can't support it from the text even though we know this principle applies to life on Earth.

But why else would entire races of beings work to uplift one (even the one that received the Logos), if there were not some background process that was deemed insufficient?
