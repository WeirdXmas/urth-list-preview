The thread: Larry Miller asked whether Nightside the Long Sun hides puzzles, and James Wynn listed open questions: the dead woman in Silk's vision, the skin on Blood's roof, and Orpine's killer. Wynn identified the skin as a rabbit taken by a gyrevulture, and Antonin Scriabin argued the bird's attack gives Crane a reason to tend Silk's injured ankle. Wynn then pinned the leg injury on a fall from Hyacinth's taunting and drew mythic parallels, while Lee Berman proposed an Egyptian shift in Wolfe's mythology. David Stockhoff and Jane Delawney widened the wounded-hero pattern to Jesus, Jacob and the Fisher King, and the thread drifted into comparative myth. Gerry Quinn's answer that Chenille stabbed Orpine was the only attempt to settle a mystery.
  • Larry Miller: asks whether Nightside has hidden puzzles and reads the skin and dead woman as mirrored events
  • James Wynn: lists unanswered mysteries, reads the rabbit and gyrevulture as thematic, and argues Silk's injury comes from a fall
  • Antonin Scriabin: the gyrevulture attack matters because it draws Crane to Silk, and Wolfe often makes protagonists lame
  • Lee Berman: Wolfe moves his mythic references from Greco-Roman toward Egyptian, with birds contrasted to New Sun fish
  • David Stockhoff: the leg wound follows the wounded-hero and sacred-king pattern, and heroes need a weakening blow
  • Gerry Quinn: Chenille stabbed Orpine, who was possessed by Mucor

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Myth and folklore sources, Suffering and sacrifice

2011-10-28 Antonin Scriabin replying to James Wynn #

Argues that the gyrevulture attack on Silk's ankle matters to the plot, because the injury gives Crane a reason to tend him and so brings the two closer. Responds to Larry Miller's point about the vulture's narrative function, and notes that Wolfe repeatedly makes his protagonists lame, naming Weer, Severian and Silk.
supports · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft

His injury by the vulture is also one of the reasons he is able to become close with Crane; his hurt ankle serves as a pretext for Crane to give him the wrapping, etc. So the vulture serves an importance narrative purpose as well. Also, Wolfe seems to have a thing with lame protagonists. Weer, Severian, Silk ... not sure why that is.

2011-10-28 James Wynn replying to Antonin Scriabin #

Argues that Silk's leg injury in The Book of the Long Sun comes from a fall caused by Hyacinth's taunting rather than the gyrevulture, and links it to myths of Hephaestus, Genesis, Jason, Cinderella and Oedipus. Responds to Antonin Scriabin's remark on the vulture and on Wolfe's lame protagonists, offering mythic parallels as support.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Silk and Auk enact the life of Hephaestus

His leg injury occurs when he falls out of the window because Hyacinth is taunting him with her azoth. He only gets a cut from the gyrevulture.

The Silk leg injury another example of Silk and Auk acting out the life of Hephaestus. Hephaestus was alternately tossed off Mt Olympus by Zeus (lighting bolt) and Hera (make of it what you will), and injured his leg. There's the prophesy in Genesis in which the serpent bites the heel of the "enmity" which in turn crushes his head. Also, Jason and Cinderella had only one shoe and Oedipus had a club foot.

J.

2011-10-28 Lee Berman replying to larry miller #

Proposes that Wolfe shifts his mythological references in Long Sun from Greco-Roman toward Egyptian, citing the bird imagery and the gods, especially Typhon's family and the Sphigx. Responds to James Wynn's question about the gyrevulture on Blood's roof by offering a thematic reading that ties birds to Egyptian myth and contrasts them with the fish imagery of the New Sun.
proposes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Wolfe shifts mythic sources from Greco-Roman to Egyptian

I agree. I remember being struck by the pervasive bird theme from the first reading of Long Sun. Character names. Musk's hobby. Flyers. Almost as pervasive as the fishy theme which inhabits so much of BotNS.

I get the sense it is part of a mythological reference shift for Wolfe from Greco-Roman to Egyptian. Birds are more important to the Egyptian myths than the Greek. Of course there are obvious reasons Egyptian mythology is less sea-oriented than for the Greeks. (same contrast for mostly dry Whorl and mostly wet Urth)

I see it in the gods; on the Whorl, Typhon has a family of which there was no hint of in BotNS (well, aside from veiled references to Scylla and Echidna). In reading Egyptian mythology I get more of a sense of family ties (dysfunctional but tied) among the gods than I do reading Greek or Roman.

Typhon does make a nice bridge between Greek and Egyptian mythology. In the former he is just a terrible monster but for the Egyptians (as Typhon-Set) he was more of a dark, rather unpleasant god. The inclusion of another crossover god, Sphigx (Sphinx) as one of Typhon's kids could be a slight hint of the shift from Greek toward Egyptian. (though the Egyptian sphinx is male while the Greek and Whorl Sphinxes are female).

2011-10-29 David Stockhoff replying to James Wynn #

Argues that Silk's leg injury belongs to a widespread pattern of wounded heroes and sacred kings, citing Jesus being stabbed in the thigh and the old link between a king's virility and the land's fertility. Replies to Antonin Scriabin's claim that Silk's injury echoes Hephaestus, and adds that heroes need a weakening blow or their stories would be very short.
disputes · myth-and-structure · source-and-allusion · theories: Silk's leg injury echoes Hephaestus

And Jesus was stabbed in the thigh. Don't forget that one!

The provenance of this trope is ancient. As I recall, it has to do with the sacred status of a king and his ability to procreate; symbolically, if the king is virile the land will be fertile. If he is wounded, this is put in doubt. Groin = thigh = leg = foot ::: foot = leg = thigh = groin.

All good heroes have been dealt blows that weaken them; they can't all be King of the Prom and captain of a winning football team. In fact none can be unless they then suffer a blow. If they were, they would always win and their stories would be very short.

2011-10-29 Jane Delawney replying to David Stockhoff #

Proposes that the thigh wound in the Long Sun books may echo Jacob's wrestling with the angel, and suggests Robert Graves's King Jesus as a source with a hip wound and a royal marriage ritual. Responds to David Stockhoff's thread on being stabbed in the thigh, comparing Silk and Severian's injuries and mentioning the Fisher King.
proposes · source-and-allusion · myth-and-structure · theories: Silk's thigh wound echoes Jacob wrestling the angel

Possibly Jacob, who was wounded in the 'thigh' (read: groin, I strongly suspect) when he wrestled with the angel?

However ... David, is it possible you were thinking of Robert Graves's novel 'King Jesus' when you wrote your OP? In this, the 'wrestling with an angel' motif is transformed into a rite of passage for the rightful king of Israel; Jesus undertakes this rite and is wounded in the thigh/hip area (his hip is dislocated) giving him a permanent limp.

Not exactly being stabbed in the thigh/groin but close.

In King Jesus, this rite is undertaken as a preliminary to the marriage ceremony for the rightful King. Jesus is married to Mary Magdalene who is also of the royal line - but he derails the ritual by refusing to consummate this marriage (he tells Mary on their wedding night, 'You are my sister! - I can't remember whether or not Graves intends this to be literally true, ie. that here MM was actually Jesus's sister and that this was supposed to be an Egyptian-royalty style sibling marriage). It's all very 'White Goddess' as one might expect from Graves.

This is the only occasion, literary, mythological or otherwise, when I recall Jesus being wounded (not stabbed though) in the 'thigh'.

This is a long way from what happened to Silk, though - though rather nearer to what happened to Severian, whose injury is undoubtedly in the thigh.

And let's not forget the Fisher King either.
JD
