Themes: Fathers, mothers and children, Beasts and the animal in us

2013-04-11 Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe's parasitic figures, the inhumi in The Book of the Short Sun and the Aelfr, dramatize ambivalence about parasitism when it appears within one's own family, and that Horn's children are conflated with the inhumi. Responds to Jerry Friedman's question about political and economic meaning in these parasites, agreeing with it and suggesting a veiled autobiographical element in the work.
supports · author-and-biography · theories: Horn's children are conflated with the inhumi; Wolfe's parasites express veiled autobiography

I truly think so.

Furthermore, given the conflation of Horn's children with the inhumi in Short Sun, I think Wolfe takes the human principle of anti-parisitism and demonstrates the ambiguity of feelings which occur when such parasitism finds a place in one's own family.

And I do think there is some veiled autobiography going on there.
