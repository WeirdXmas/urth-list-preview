Short Story 117: Read Me

Themes: Books, libraries and writing

2015-01-26 Marc Aramini #

Argues that Wolfe's hundred-word story "Read Me" uses tarot card meanings and wordplay on "troubled" and "tell the future" to read the Fool, Tower, Devil and Magician as a reading. Quotes tarot descriptions of the Tower and Devil and cites Adam Stephanides's earlier suggestion that the tarot pack stands for science fiction writers.
informs · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: the tarot pack represents science fiction writers

*READ ME*

?Read Me? first appeared in *The Drabble Project* in 1988 and has not been reprinted.

The story is only one hundred words long, as seen below:

Alice looked about her. "You're the most curious cards I've seen."

"That's because we're a tarot pack. If we weren't curious, we wouldn't have troubled the future, and so couldn't tell it."

"You mean troubled *to learn of* the future," Alice corrected him.

The Fool appeared offended. "I mean what I said. If we hadn't troubled the Future, we couldn't tell it how sorry we are."

Alice was puzzled by that, though she proceeded bravely. "But you foretell the future."

"No," said the Fool, "We four tell it." And he indicated the Falling Tower, the Devil, the Magician and himself. (Wolfe)

DISCUSSION:

Included in *The Drabble Project*, consisting entirely of exactly 100 word short stories by SF writers, the brief ?Read Me? employs Wolfe?s cryptic and ambiguous wordplay to full effect. The verb and object combination the Fool uses to ?tell the future? flirts with slipping between an indirect object and a direct object depending on perspective (The Fool wants to tell the future he is sorry, but Alice wants him to tell the future to her). The punning on troubling and foretelling is obvious.

Of course, given the idea that Alice is talking to a Tarot Deck with four cards in the future position, we should do a reading. Since The Fool is talking, and it represents 0 in the tarot, we should also explore its relationship to the person seeking the reading. Generally, the Fool stands in for that person (as the title also reinforces). It represents a zero or fresh starting point, almost childlike in nature. It can also represent foolishness or an immature understanding of events.

The Tower in the future position usually indicates an immediate future, days or hours away. It represents the decimation of some structure in life completely and totally. Because of its pertinent description of the spiritual significance of The Tower and those falling from its battlements, a further description is enlightening:

The Tower shows a tall tower pitched atop a craggy mountain. Lightning strikes and flames burst from the building?s windows. People are seen to be leaping from the tower in desperation, wanting to flee such destruction and turmoil. The Tower signifies darkness and destruction on a physical scale, as opposed to a spiritual scale. The Tower itself represents ambitions built on false premises. The lightning bolt breaks down existing forms in order to make room for new ones. It represents a sudden, momentary glimpse of truth, a flash of inspiration that breaks down structures of ignorance and false reasoning ? the lightning bolt is oriented left to right (from the perspective of the card), from heaven to earth, and from Spirit to material. The falling figures correspond to the chained prisoners in the Devil card. They fall headfirst, because the sudden influx of spiritual consciousness represented by the lightning flash completely upsets all our old notions about the relations between subconsciousness and self-consciousness. The flames are actually the Hebrew yods. There are 22 flames, representing the 22 Major Arcana. The gray clouds are the clouds of misfortune that rain on everyone indiscriminately. The ruling planet of this card is Mars. (?Tower Tarot Card Meanings?)

Since the people falling from The Tower correspond with the chained figures depicted on The Devil?s, these cards are thematically linked. The Devil forebodes ill fortune and defeat in the future, but if it is paired with The Fool (which it is) then its meaning is augmented and might even indicate that the person being read is safe and separate from the disaster about to unfold, though the world and the external surroundings are facing a situation with a dark agenda. The Magician in the future position represents achieving the power and influence one craves. In conjunction with the Fool, it represents success when one passively avoids it as long as the Magician is identified with the person seeking the reading instead of someone else. As previously hinted, when the Fool shows up, it represents free will, creation and, especially in the future position, a new life. Since the Fool appears in conjunction with both the Magician and the Devil in the future, there are some contradictory signs from a standard reading:

When The Devil is near The Fool in a reading, your pleasure-seeking ways are about to create a disaster. ? The Fool is a great card when The Magician is paired with it as long as a sensitive Tarot reader can assure you that both cards apply to you. If this is the case, the chance to break free and create a new reality will be a success. If it is determined that The Magician represents someone else, beware. (?The Fool Tarot Card?).

Adam Stephanides on the Urth Mailing List once posited that this short work is a metaphor for science fiction and speculative future writers: ?I take the Tarot pack to represent science fiction writers, who once troubled? the future and now tell it they're sorry. I'm tempted to go further and say that the writers who "troubled" the future are the Campbell-era writers with their technological optimism, and the ones who apologize to it are present-day, less optimistic writers.?

The goal of the tarot deck, using mystical forces to divine one?s current position and plot out or seek a warning for the future, actually does echo some of the speculative trends of science fiction in general: either seeking to extrapolate a future or apply a warning to the modern era by showing how certain current trends might result in disaster or success (on the one hand, the goal of didactic utopian or dystopian fiction, on the other the spirit of speculative adventure that asks, ?What if ???)

While at first the parallel between SF and tarot doesn?t seem that strong, there is a synchronicity of process: harnessing some energy, creative or mystical, to foretell or estimate the future. Here, we do have the specific tarot symbolism to guide us. Overall it seems that the future is grim and disastrous, and innocent girls like Alice, here equated with The Fool, will face a difficult choice. Even in the face of physical, material disaster, there is still the possibility of spiritual salvation. The future may be damned, but if the person seeking the future can create a spiritual reality and become their own ?Magician?, then they can escape. Once again, innocence and free will are implied no matter the surroundings. All of these cards are related in tarot readings: the prisoners in The Devil?s card are seen being cast down from The Tower into the material world; the Fool modifies both The Devil and The Magician. Certainly Wolfe selected these cards for their related meanings: physical and material disaster with the possibility of a dualistic escape through free will and the creation of a higher or separate reality beyond that terrible, troubled future ? but only if the person being read and The Magician are one ? if Alice becomes trapped in the ?magic? or falls under the influence of a more base, material force (which might perhaps represent the anti-spiritual ?modernity? of our times), then the reading is grim indeed even for her.

LITERARY ALLUSIONS:

Of course we are aware of Lewis Carroll?s Alice stories. In Alice?s *Adventures in Wonderland*, Alice encounters the Queen of Hearts and the cards under her, and the thematic continuation of sentient cards into the decks of the tarot. In Carroll?s versions, Alice comes upon three cards trying to paint white roses red (for their queen detests white roses), but they are sentenced to death anyway.

CONNECTION WITH OTHER WORKS:

This cryptic strain runs throughout Wolfe. Carroll has clearly influenced him, and references in *The Book of the New Sun* and ?In Looking Glass Castle? drive that point home. The concept of the innocent child exploring a strange unknown world is a motif we can see developed in Wolfe?s fiction, evoking both pathos (such as in ?The Eyeflash Miracles?) and horror (as in ?And When They Appear?, some of the Wolfe archipelago stories). However, nowhere else is it expressed quite so succinctly. The reliance of meaning on external or intertextual sources is also typical of Wolfe.
