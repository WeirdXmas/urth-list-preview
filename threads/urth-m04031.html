Tracking Song and vizcacha, and misc.

Urth list · 5 messages · 2007-06-17 to 2007-06-17

2007urthDaniel D JonesDave LeblingJosh GellerMatthew Groves
The thread: Matthew Groves asked what a vizcacha is, whether the Tracking Song setting is an asteroid with moons, whether Mantru is a minotaur, and what several symbols and the winged man signify. Daniel D Jones proposed that the setting is an asteroid and that Mantru's staff was a robot-makers' artifact. Dave Lebling answered that a vizcacha is a South American rodent related to the chinchilla, chosen for the Long Sun books' South American flavour, and that the setting could be an asteroid, pointing to the Great Sleigh's two-week circuit. Jones then questioned how large an asteroid would need to be to hold an atmosphere and whether that circuit is plausible, conceding Wolfe may simply have taken poetic license. The thread ends without a settled answer on the setting's size.
  • Matthew Groves: asks a string of questions about the vizcacha, the asteroid setting, Mantru as minotaur, and the winged man's identity
  • Daniel D Jones: proposes the setting is an asteroid and the staff is a robot-makers' artifact, then doubts the two-week circuit and allows poetic license
  • Dave Lebling: explains the vizcacha as a South American rodent and supports the asteroid reading with the Great Sleigh's circuit

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, The dying sun and the cosmos, Myth and folklore sources

2007-06-17 Matthew Groves #

Asks several questions about Wolfe's Urth of the New Sun-era fiction: what a vizcacha is, whether the setting is an asteroid with moons, and whether Mantru with his horned staff is a minotaur. Points to the staff's insect-like antennae, the labyrinth, the Aeneid epigraph, and the winged man catching the sun as puzzles, then lists possible identities for the winged man.
questions · puzzle-solving · source-and-allusion · theories: Mantru is a minotaur; the winged man is Icarus

Woops, sorry about that. Take "Liebling" as a sign of my affection. What sort of thing is a vizcacha, by the way?

Well, that wouldn't be a first. So there is another layer there, or several layers in one, as you observe. And you thing this all takes place on an asteroid? That would not be a first either. Can you expand on that at all? It is obviously extremely small, but do asteroids have moons?

On another matter: If all the tribes carry weapons keyed to their "totemic" animals, then what are we to make of Mantru's horned staff? I though perhaps he might be a minotaur, particularly because he lives in a "labyrinth." But in that case it seems like Mantru should be a lot bigger. And the description of the staff gives it insect qualities: it has "a pair of slender and nearly parallel horns," it's metallic, and the horns are "gracefully curved antennae." But what about the "geometric designs interspersed with faces" on the shaft and the fine script on the antennae that is "of a form I felt certain I had never seen before."

Still the correspondence among the words "Min," "Mantru," "Minotaur," and "King Minos" is suggestive, and the epigraph from the Aeneid points in that general direction also. And what would this say about the *winged man* at the end!!! The winged man who is catching the *sun*!!! Any thoughts?

2007-06-17 Daniel D Jones replying to Matthew Groves #

Proposes that the setting of the story might be an asteroid orbiting Earth, so that the word "moon" for Earth and Luna should be read loosely by a stone-age inhabitant. Adds that Mantru's staff was probably an artifact of the robot makers rather than something his people built, based on memory of the story.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: The setting is an asteroid orbiting Earth; Mantru's staff is a robot-makers artifact

Suppose an asteroid were in orbit around the earth? Would a stone-age level inhabitant consider the Earth and Luna as two moons? I'm not certain that it makes sense to say that they're actually on an asteroid orbiting our Earth, just pointing out that the word "moon" might need to be taken rather loosely.

It's been awhile since I read this story, but I was under the impression when I read it that Mantru's staff was an artifact (probably of the robot makers) and not something that he or his kind had constructed.

Grass is green. The sky is blue. :-)

2007-06-17 Dave Lebling replying to Matthew Groves #

Explains that the name vizcacha refers to a small South American rodent related to the chinchilla, chosen to fit the South American flavour of the Long Sun books, and says the Tracking Song setting could be an asteroid with moons. Answers Matthew Groves's questions about the name and the setting, citing the Great Sleigh's two-week circuit of the body as evidence of its size.
answers · source-and-allusion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Tracking Song is set on an asteroid

Vizcacha is usually (but not always) spelled "viscacha," and it's a small rabbit-like rodent native to South America; it's closely related to the chinchilla. Given the far future South American setting of New Sun and the South American culture of Long Sun (which prompted all the animal and plant nicknames), I thought it fit.

Here's the Wikipedia article, with a decent photo:
http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vizcacha
Some asteroids do indeed have moons. Check the archives for discussion of the size of the setting for "Tracking Song." The Great Sleigh circumnavigates it in two weeks (by my memory here), going at a rather sedate speed. I suppose it could be a moon, but I think asteroids with moons are more common than moons with moons.

Dave Lebling, aka vizcacha
