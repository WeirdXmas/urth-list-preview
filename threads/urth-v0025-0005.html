Sev as anti-hero

Urth list · 4 messages · 1999-04-06 to 1999-04-12

1999urthThe Book of the New SunKevin J. MaroneyRoy C. LackeyAlex David Groce
The thread: Roy C. Lackey argued that Severian, though he makes real moral progress, is an anti-hero rather than a sympathetic Everyman, unlike Silk, Weer or Green, because of his power to harm and his lack of relatability. Alex David Groce disputed the label, holding that Severian stumbles to glory and is chosen by the Hierodules because he finishes the task, and that the reading depends on the last pages of Urth. Kevin J. Maroney countered that Weer also leaves many dead and that Wolfe's protagonists are generally neither admirable nor likable, so identification is not the test. Groce then rejected the claim that Weer is a mass murderer, citing only the boy on the stairs and possibly the frozen man. The thread ended in disagreement over how to compare Wolfe's protagonists and what counts as killing, without resolution.
  • Roy C. Lackey: Severian is an anti-hero, not a sympathetic Everyman, because of his power to harm and lack of relatability
  • Alex David Groce: Severian is a stumbling hero chosen for completing the task; the anti-hero label depends on the last pages of Urth
  • Kevin J. Maroney: Weer also leaves many dead, and Wolfe's protagonists are generally unlikable, so identification is not the test
  • Alex David Groce (later): Weer's only likely killings are the boy on the stairs and possibly the frozen man; the mass-murderer idea is nonsense

Themes: Justice, torture and mercy, Free will and choice, Power, rulers and politics

1999-04-06 Roy C. Lackey #

Argues that Severian, though he makes real moral progress in The Book of the New Sun, is not a sympathetic Everyman but an anti-hero, unlike Silk, Weer or Green. Responds to Alex David Groce's view of Severian as a sinful but redeemable everyman, grounding the claim in Severian's power to do harm and his lack of relatability.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian is an anti-hero rather than Everyman

<snip>
see ourselves in them, with veins of dark and light. A picture of a (to sue the religious terms that are relevant) sinful but redeemable man. And I think
it's clear that there IS moral growth in Severian throughout the BOTNS. The final talk with Palaemon, in my opinion, establishes that for the pre-Urth Severian, at least.<<

Oh, I agree he makes moral progress. I said much the same thing in an off-list e-mail to someone here. But I still do not like Sev and have trouble seeing him as Everyman--even a dark Everyman. Silk, Weer, and the other figures you mention are much more sympathetic figures, whatever their flaws, because they come closer to Everyman than Sev. They do not wield the powers, temporal or supernatural, that Sev does. They do not have his capacity for doing harm. mantis used the term "anti-hero" and I have to agree with it. I could never identify with Sev, as I could with Weer or Green or Silk or Free's boarders.

Roy

1999-04-06 Alex David Groce #

Disputes the label anti-hero for Severian, arguing he stumbles to glory and the Hierodules choose him because he finally accomplishes the task. Contrasts Severian with Silk and Green as protagonists of differing darkness, and notes the reading depends on how one interprets the last pages of Urth.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian stumbles to glory rather than anti-hero

I won't argue that Severian is easy to identify with; as to Silk, I would place him in another category of Wolfe's protagonists. Green is somewhere in the middle--not as dark as Severian (or even Weer), but not an almost saintly (and likable--which is hard to portray) fellow like Silk. I'm just not sure anti-hero is the right term for Severian. It seems to me that in the end he stumbles to glory, at any rate, and that's not just an accident--the Hierodules choose him because he does, in the end, get the job done. Of course, this depends on your interpretation of the last pages of Urth. --

1999-04-12 Kevin J. Maroney #

Disputes Roy's claim that Severian is less sympathetic than Silk or Weer because he wields great power and capacity for harm, arguing that Weer also leaves a trail of dead bodies. Argues that Wolfe's protagonists are generally neither admirable nor likable, with the Free quartet as an exception, and so identification is not the test.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Wolfe's protagonists are generally not admirable or likable

Considering the trail of dead bodies Weer leaves behind him, I'm not sure that I *want* to identify with Weer. Wolfe's protagonists are not admirable or likable people, for the most part (the "Free" quartet being a very pleasant exception).

Wombat, a.k.a. Kevin Maroney [email]

1999-04-12 Alex David Groce #

Disputes the claim that Weer is a mass murderer, arguing his only likely killings are the boy on the stairs, which is not really murder, and possibly the frozen man in the freezer. Responds to Roy's comparison of Severian with more sympathetic figures, which Kevin J. Maroney quoted, by calling the Weer-as-mass-murderer hypothesis nonsense.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Weer is not a mass murderer

Well, some of us aren't convinced that Weer is reponsible for anyone's death other than (A) the boy on the stairs, and that's not exactly murder and (B) maybe (there is someting sinister about it at any rate) the frozen-freezer guy. I think the "Weer as mass murder" hypothesis is most likely nonsense. --
