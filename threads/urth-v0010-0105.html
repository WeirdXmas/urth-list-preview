Suzanne

Urth list · 5 messages · 1998-05-07 to 1998-05-11

1998urthadam louis stephanidesPeter WestlakeAlice Turner
The thread: The thread asked whether Wolfe's story Suzanne hides a supernatural or secret-group puzzle. Peter Westlake argued that Suzanne belongs to the Pie Club, is marked 'Unable to be photographed' and may be a ghost, vampire, or rejuvenated mother, while adam louis stephanides disputed the photograph reading and said the daughter's beauty is overstated by a lonely narrator. Alice Turner argued the story is a realist, Proustian meditation on a life that might have gone differently, and Westlake later agreed with that reading, so the thread ended in rough agreement on the realist interpretation.
  • Peter Westlake: Suzanne is a Pie Club member and possibly a ghost, vampire, or rejuvenated mother; the narrator's past is a puzzle
  • adam louis stephanides: the narrator would have known of any supernatural absence; the photograph caption shows no extra name; the daughter's beauty is overstat
  • Alice Turner: the story is a realist meditation on regret, Proustian rather than Kafkaesque or Borgesian
  • Peter Westlake (later): agrees with the Proustian, non-fantasy reading and the narrator's failure to recall Suzanne

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Ghosts, dreams and visions

1998-05-07 Peter Westlake #

Argues that Suzanne is a member of the Pie Club and is among the figures in its photograph, and is listed as "Unable to be photographed," implying she is a ghost or otherwise absent from the image. Offers hints about the daughter's description and the narrator's question about cliques before giving the spoiler. Responds to Alice Turner's request for the spoiler, after she mentioned Mantis's Proust discovery.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Suzanne is among the Pie Club girls in the photograph; Suzanne is unable to be photographed because she is a ghost

Well, I'll start with a couple of hints, and then put the spoiler right at the end.

First, why do we see Suzanne's daughter? I think she's there to tell us what Suzanne looks like. I would have been surprised if she had been a tanned redhead, for instance. We don't meet Suzanne herself because that would be too much like the Stoppard parody of Waiting For Godot - "there he is now". It would undo the plot.

Second, the narrator wonders if Suzanne belongs to any cliques. I think she belongs to at least two: the Pie Club, and one other group of people, mentioned in the story.

Final hint: what's the problem with looking for Suzanne in the picture of the Pie Club?

Okay, last chance. ** SPOILER follows **

Here's the sentence, and in particular the phrase, that still gives me the shivers:

Suzanne is listed among those "Unable to be photographed".

And the girls in the Pie Club photo are too loosely grouped to be identified easily. But I bet they wouldn't have looked that way at the time .... when you could see Suzanne.

Peter.

1998-05-08 adam louis stephanides #

Disputes Peter Westlake's claim that Suzanne belongs to a secret group because she is "Unable to be photographed" in the Pie Club picture, arguing the narrator would have known of any supernatural absence. Points out the caption would have shown an extra name and that "loosely grouped" only describes the posing, then offers that the daughter's beauty is overstated by a lonely narrator.
disputes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Suzanne is a supernatural being absent from photographs; Daughter's beauty reflects the narrator's sentimentality

Which? The "dazzlingly beautiful ones"? That's certainly what one interpretation of Suzanne's daughter's appearance, but see below.

I hate to pour cold water on your theories, especially since this story has always mystified me. But if, as you seem to be implying, Suzanne is some sort of supernatural being who doesn't show up in photographs, I think the narrator would have heard about it and remembered it, along with the entire town. And if Suzanne had been absent from the Pie Club photo, instead of just unidentifiable, wouldn't the narrator have noticed that the caption contained one too many names? I think "loosely grouped" means only that the girls are posed as if in the middle of cooking, rather than lined up in rows as is usual.

I really would like an explanation for this story. The best I've been able to do is: if Suzanne's daughter had really been as strikingly beautiful as the story's next-to-last paragraph describes, then the narrator would certainly have noticed Suzanne. Ergo, the daughter is really not so remarkable, and the flowery prose of the description reflects the sentimentality of a lonely middle-aged man. But if that's all there is to it, it's not much of a story. And the way Wolfe writes it, it feels to me that there should be some greater payoff.

--Adam

1998-05-09 Peter Westlake #

Argues through several details of Wolfe's story Suzanne that the narrator's past is a puzzle, floating theories that Suzanne is a supernatural being such as a vampire and that her mother rejuvenated herself. Grounds the case in the missing photographs, the Pie Club or Pudding Club name, the daughter's age, the narrator's early retirement, and the mother's exhausted but eager trips.
proposes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Suzanne is a supernatural being who does not appear in photographs; Suzanne's mother is a vampire; Suzanne's mother rejuvenated herself and invented a high school career for Suzanne

No, the ones who don't show up in photographs.

I did wonder about that myself. But as Craig has just reminded us, the story starts with the narrator reading about the idea that everyone has had some truly extraordinary experience and forgotten all about it. Mirrors might be more of a problem.

And if Suzanne had been absent from the Pie Club photo,

It would depend on what and who was in the background, I think.

However ... looking again, I see that the narrator isn't quite certain of the name of the Pie Club. So maybe Craig is right and it is the "Pudding Club" instead! Suzanne's daughter is only about fifteen, so that would mean the story takes place only about sixteen years after high school, and we are told the narrator has retired; but "much sooner than most men", so it might still work.

One more small bit of evidence for the supernatural theory, and the vampire theory in particular, is the way the narrator's mother is so exhausted by her trips with Suzanne's mother, but is always so keen to go again. Not much to go on, I admit.

Another theory is that Suzanne never was at the school, and her mother didn't die while the narrator was at college, but rejuvenated herself and then invented a high school career to account for her appearing on the scene in her teens. Then we really *do* meet her at the end. I'm not entirely convinced by this, though.

Peter.

1998-05-09 Alice Turner #

Argues that the story Suzanne is best read as a realist meditation on regret about a life that might have gone differently, not as a work with a fantasy element, and sees it as Proustian rather than Kafkaesque or Borgesian. Responds to Adam's explanation and to Alice Turner's puzzlement over the story's loosely grouped girls and the beautiful daughter described in the next-to-last paragraph.
supports · myth-and-structure · theories: Suzanne is a realist story of regret, not fantasy

I tend to go along with Adam's explanation here, but I don't agree that it diminishes the story. I think there is something powerful and poignant about a middle-aged man's shock of realization that his entire life could have been entirely different, had it not been for the coincidences of fate. I think you're all looking too hard for a fantasy element. The godfather of this story is Proust (and for once that is proven, not conjectural), not Kafka or Borges. The flavor of regret is bittersweet.

-alga-

1998-05-11 Peter Westlake #

Agrees with alga's reading of the story Suzanne as a Proustian meditation on a middle-aged man's realization that his life could have differed by chance, not a fantasy. Responds to alga's quoted argument, saying the reading makes sense and that the narrator's failure to recall never seeing Suzanne fits the idea raised at the story's start.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Suzanne is a Proustian meditation on chance and fate

That *does* make a lot of sense. In fact, I shall enjoy reading that version of the story very much even if another explanation proves to be the true one, just as I enjoyed reading "my" version. So do you think that never seeing Suzanne is sufficiently odd to fit the notion at the start of the story? Actually, I suppose it is, and the narrator hadn't called it to mind before he read the idea.

It certainly is a powerful and poignant story, and at the moment I think you're right.

Peter.
