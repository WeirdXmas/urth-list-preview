Themes: Catholic faith and sacraments

2010-03-08 James Wynn #

Argues that the complaint about Wolfe's recent books growing too Christian is unfounded, since Wolfe treats religious characters without cynicism and only occasionally echoes Judeo-Christian doctrine. Names The Wizard Knight and Pirate Freedom as the likely targets, noting the Christian content there is minor, with Gnosticism and Norse material more prominent, and that Pirate Freedom's priest protagonist is expected to be handled sympathetically.
disputes · theology-and-religion

I think the blogger should read MORE of Wolfe's more overtly Christian stories, starting with the old "The Detective of Dreams", in order to develop a thicker skin. In my literary and cinematic SF, I tolerate --mostly with aplomb-- a constant barrage of (what I consider) nonsense regarding anthropologic climate change and (IMO) derangement regarding the last Presidential administration. So I'm not sympathic of someone who gripes about a teensy bit of religious friendliness in order to enjoy the top 5% of SF fiction that Wolfe consistently delivers.

I reiterate: We're not talking about proselyzing. We're merely talking about a writer who does not deal with religious characters with cynicism or condesension. AND occassionally you'll see something that *could* be analogous of a Judeo-Christian doctrine.

You didn't link to the post so I'm not sure what works this blogger was thinking of, but I would suppose he was referring to Wizard Knight and Pirate Freedom. Those works seem to have affected some readers the way Neil Gaiman claimed to have been affected when he realized the Christian elements in The Chronicles of Narnia.

Frankly, I found the Christianity in WK to have been not that significant. It was there in the conclusion, but (once again) there's a lot more Gnosticism in there, as well as a genuine joy of Norse mythology and errant knight trophes. And the conclusion was certainly rationalized. Pirate Freedom has a priest as a protagonist so what do you want? Naturally, Roman Catholic Wolfe handled this character differently than a non-believer would. Big surprise.

J.

2010-03-08 Dann Burdette replying to James Wynn #

Argues that Wolfe handles religious elements and ideas in his fiction better than his peers, without them feeling out of place or obvious. Responds to James Wynn's thread on whether Wolfe is becoming more Christian, offering a personal reaction as a non-religious reader and contrasting Wolfe with the Chronicles of Narnia.
supports · theology-and-religion · genre-and-craft

While I haven't read the newer books you speak of, I've often felt like Wolfe's religious elements or ideas that appear in his work are head and shoulders above those of his peers.

As an entirely non-religious person, I've very rarely found an author that can tackle these things without it feeling totally out of place or obvious (like the aforementioned Chronicles of Narnia), but Wolfe does this very well.

-Dann
