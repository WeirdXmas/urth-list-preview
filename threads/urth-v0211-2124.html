The leatherskin

Urth list · 5 messages · 2002-11-16 to 2002-11-18

2002urthThe Book of the Long SunCharles ReedWilliam H. AnsleyDon DoggettDan'l Danehy-Oakes
The thread: The thread asked what the leatherskin in Wolfe's New Sun books really is and whether it is alien. Don Doggett proposed an evolved shark whose three jaws are rows of teeth that Horn misreads, calling it the only truly alien animal on Blue. Charles Reed replied that nearly all Blue animals are alien, with settlers borrowing Whorl names for eight-legged creatures. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes suggested a lobster-like crustacean, possibly with a soft molted shell, citing Huxley. William Ansley disputed the teeth reading using Horn's double-door description and argued that any shark-like creature with limbs would be alien anyway. The posts end in disagreement with no resolution.
  • Don Doggett: the leatherskin is an evolved shark, and its three jaws are teeth Horn misread
  • Charles Reed: nearly all animals on Blue are alien, so the leatherskin is not special
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the leatherskin is a lobster-like crustacean, perhaps with a soft recently molted shell
  • William Ansley: the jaws are real hinged jaws, and the creature is alien anyway, like a shark with at least six limbs

Themes: Evolution and the future of humanity, Beasts and the animal in us, Names and their meanings

2002-11-16 Don Doggett #

Proposes that the leatherskin in The Book of the Short Sun is not an alien creature but an evolved shark, with its three jaws actually three rows of teeth that a character unfamiliar with sea life misreads. Grounds this in the shark's rough skin and the creature's being the only truly alien animal in Blue, as an alternative to the alien-world reading.
proposes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: The leatherskin is an evolved shark

Howdy all,

One of (imho) Wolfe's first tricks in SS to give us the impression of Blue as an alien world is his introduction of the leatherskin, a terrible creature with three (gasp!) jaws. It is the only really alien creature in the book (even the neighbors are four limbed humanoids). Or is it? Here's a more mundane (sort of) explanation: The leatherskin is a sort of evolved shark. Yes, a shark. Its three jaws are really three rows of teeth. To someone like Horn, or Silk, who has probably never had any previous experience with salt water creatures, these rows could very well appear to be three jaws. Sort of the way people with no experience with horseback riding might have seen the first horsemen as centaurs. Sharks also have extremely rough skin. Just a thought (a scary thought - a shark that can climb into your boat. Ugh.)

Don

2002-11-18 Charles Reed #

Argues that nearly all animals on Blue in Wolfe's Long Sun and Short Sun books are alien, not just the leatherskin, with settlers borrowing familiar Whorl names like elephant and crocodile for creatures that have eight legs. Responds to Don Doggett's claim that the leatherskin is the only truly alien creature, citing the octopedal body plan of the native animals and the departed Neighbors as evidence.
disputes · close-textual-reading

Hello Don.

I'd like to respond to your idea about the "alien-ness" the animals of the Blue/Green system. You say that the leatherskin is "the only really alien creature in the book," but I think you're mistaken. ALL the animals we encounter on Blue are very and extremely alien to anything that existed on the Whorl. It's just that settlers, where possible, have given the alien animals names that reflect the only animal names they know.

For example, there's a beast on blue that is rather large, lives in the forest, and has a very long snout. Instead of making up something new, they start calling it an elephant, despite the fact that it has eight legs. It's much easier to do that than try to create a new taxonomy. Every settler knows what a real elephant is, and this new creature of Blue is similar enough in appearance that when somebody says "elephant" everybody immediately knows what is being talked about. Similarly, there's a creature that lives in the river that is at least vaguely reptilian, probably looks like a log floating in the water when still, and might eat you if you fall in, so instead of making up something new, they call it a crocodile, even though these new crocodiles are obviously not the same animal as the "real" crocodile whence their name derives ("real" crocodiles don't have eight legs).

That's not to say that some Blue animals don't get new names. Some animals are so different that they're not reminiscent enough of a Whorl animal to inherit a Whorl name. In those cases, the names given are very utilitarian and descriptive in nature -- such as "leatherskin."

You're also forgetting the inhumi, and the great variety of alien beings on Green, which were pretty much mentioned only in passing. Still, some of their descriptions bear little resemblance to anything we ran into on the Whorl.

Blue is an alien world. The evolution of land-based life on Blue for some reason favored octopedal locomotion rather than quadrupedal locomotion, as was the case on Urth and subsequently in the Whorl. In fact, I can't think of a single animal we meet that is native to Blue that does not have eight limbs, and that includes the departed Neighbors.

Wolfe is an extremely surprising writer, and is often subtle -- sometimes subtle to point of being frustrating -- but I really don't regard him as being "tricky." I tend to take his (and his narrator's) explanations at face value until there is a reason to do otherwise. Sometimes there is such a reason, but I don't think this is one of those times.

Charles

2002-11-18 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Proposes that the leatherskin in the Book of the New Sun is a lobster-like crustacean, possibly with a soft or recently molted shell, and that its three jaws could be fewer than a real crustacean's. Supports this by quoting Thomas Huxley's description of a crayfish-type carapace and its six pairs of jaws, suggesting Horn may not see them all.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: The leatherskin is a lobster-like crustacean

Actually, since first reading that scene, I've always envisioned the leatherskin as some kind of lobster ... possibly one with a slightly softer shell, possibly one that has molted recently; as for the three jaws, consider this from Thomas Huxley:

"... when I turn to the forepart of the body I see, at first,=20 nothing but a great shield-like shell, called technically the=20 'carapace,' ending in front in a sharp spine, on either side=20 of which are the curious compound eyes, set upon the ends of=20 stout movable stalks. Behind these, on the under side of the=20 body, are two pairs of long feelers, or antennae, followed by=20 six pairs of jaws folded against one another over the mouth..."

_Six_ pairs of jaws!?! Okay, that's a bit of overkill... but perhaps Horn doesn't see them all...

--Blattid

2002-11-18 William H. Ansley #

Disputes Don Doggett's claim that Horn mistook three rows of teeth for three jaws in the leatherskin passage, citing Horn's 'slamming of double doors' description of two hinged jaws closing on a fixed one. Argues the creature is alien anyway, since a shark with at least six limbs would be as unearthly as three jaws, and points back to his 1999 argument for twelve legs.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Leatherskin is an evolved shark with three jaws; Leatherskin has twelve legs

Don,

I don't agree that Horn mistook three rows of teeth for three jaws. Horn says that the jaws clashed shut like "the slamming of double doors." [p. 61 of the hardcover edition of OBW]. I don't see how this can be interpreted to mean anything other than two separate, hinged jaws closing against a third fixed one.

In any case, a shark with at least *six* limbs [1] is as alien to Earthly evolution as one with three jaws, in my somewhat informed opinion (I was a genetics major in a previous life).

--
William Ansley
