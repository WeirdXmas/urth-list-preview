Wolfe and Calvino

Urth list · 7 messages · 2000-02-22 to 2000-02-23

2000urthThe Book of the Short SunNicholas GeversJesper SvedbergAlice TurnerAlex David GroceThomas JeenickeUrash, Tom
The thread: Participants asked whether Italo Calvino influenced Gene Wolfe, with Alex David Groce, Nicholas Gevers and Jesper Svedberg suggesting possible parallels and Thomas Jeenicke disputing any direct influence. Jeenicke argued that memory is not a main Calvino theme and that If on a Winter's Night a Traveler is the nearest parallel, asking whether Wolfe is postmodern. Svedberg conceded that the two share more in style than content, citing Invisible Cities' sense of wonder. Alice Turner agreed influence was not evident and both she and Groce called Wolfe a modernist rather than a postmodernist. The thread ended without resolution, drifting into side remarks about Cosmicomics and the Calvino comparison.
  • Alex David Groce: Wolfe is a modernist rather than postmodernist; Calvino's If on a Winter's Night suits Wolfe's tastes despite lacking SF
  • Nicholas Gevers: In Green's Jungles shows an Italianate bias possibly from Calvino, to be checked while reading
  • Thomas Jeenicke: Little evidence Wolfe drew on Calvino; If on a Winter's Night is the nearest parallel
  • Jesper Svedberg: Wolfe and Calvino share style more than content, based on Invisible Cities and The Baron in the Trees
  • Alice Turner: Calvino's influence on Wolfe is not evident; Wolfe is a modernist

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both, Memory and forgetting

2000-02-22 Alex David Groce #

Recalls that Italo Calvino's If on a Winter's Night a Traveler, translated by William Weaver, is a favorite because it celebrates reading, and notes it lacks a science fiction pastiche. Argues Wolfe is better called a modernist than a postmodernist, pointing to the list archives' earlier debate on the question.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe is a modernist rather than postmodernist

"If On a Winter's Night a Traveler" was translated into English by William Weaver, who also does Eco's translations. It's my favorite Calvino book-- probably because it's a tribute to the act of reading. If I recall, a genre science fiction pastiche is, unfortunately, not among the half-begun books that it contains.

The list archives contain considerable discussion of whether Wolfe is a post-modernist, etc. I would say he's more accurately described as a modernist.

2000-02-23 Jesper Svedberg #

Argues that Wolfe and Calvino share more in style than in content, citing Invisible Cities as producing a sense of wonder like that of The Book of the New Sun. Responds to Thomas Jeenicke's doubt about Wolfe's influence from Calvino, based on the poster's reading of The Baron in the Trees and the opening pages of Invisible Cities.
supports

The only thing I've read by Calvino is _The Tree Baron_ (title? the original was
_Il Barone Rapante_ anyway) and the first few pages of _Invisible Cities_. I'd say
that Wolfe has more in common with Calvino stylewise than contentwise, the other
similarity would be that they are both intelligent authors who write intelectual
novels. Although, after taking a quick look at _Invisible Cities_ I can't help
wondering if Wolfe has read Calvino, _IC_ had the same sense of wonder that I got
from reading TBotNS, albeit it seemed even more obscure.

// Jesper Svedberg

2000-02-23 Alice Turner #

Agrees with a Danish newcomer that Calvino's influence on Wolfe is not evident, while joking that the city Pajarocu is an Invisible City. Notes that Italo Calvino's If on a winter's night a traveler lacks science fiction references, yet suits Wolfe's tastes, and calls Wolfe a modernist rather than a postmodernist.
supports · theories: Pajarocu is an Invisible City

I agree with our new Danish member (welcome, Thomas, and please turn off that Rich Text feature for this list) that the Calvino influence, if any, is not so evident. Except for my half-joke that Pajarocu is an Invisible City, which it really is. Alex is (I think) right; IOAWNAT doesn't have any out-and-out sf refs or jokes in it (I've never thought of Italians, especially of his generation, as being into it)--still, it's the sort of book that I think might attract Wolfe. It was only a half-joke and those of us who are really conversant with Calvino (I have read some, even mini-reviewed one book, but I'm not) might think about this.

I also would call Wolfe a modernist rather than a post-modernist, except for some experimentation in a few of the stories (and no, I can't cite them off the top of my head). This could lead us into a labyrinth (hey, cool!).

-alga
