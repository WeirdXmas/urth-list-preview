2013urthThe Book of the New SunLee BermanCraig BrewerGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffAntonin ScriabinAndrew MasonDaniel Petersen
The thread: The thread opened with Andrew Mason and Lee debating whether Silkhorn's contact with Seawrack is Silk or Horn, and David Stockhoff argued that sex in Wolfe drives change and mortality while gods and Ascians stay static. Lee tied the choice to a mythic pattern involving Echidna and the Gaon, but admitted the relationships are not fully resolved. The thread then drifted when Craig Brewer asked when the moratorium on The Land Across would lift, and Brewer, Daniel Otto Jack Petersen and Stockhoff compared the novel with Miéville's The City and the City and Kafka. Antonin Scriabin said he enjoyed the novel, and Gerry Quinn offered Last Letters from Hav and Ruritania as precursors. The Silkhorn questions were left without resolution.
  • Andrew Mason: the Rajan contacts Seawrack as Silk, since he no longer considers himself married to Nettle
  • David Stockhoff: sex is central in Wolfe because reproduction brings change, mortality and transcendence, unlike static gods and Ascians
  • Lee: Wolfe deliberately links Silkhorn's choice to a mythic picture with Echidna, Abaia and Scylla, while admitting loose ends
  • Craig Brewer: The Land Across and The City and the City are worth comparing as similar yet opposed
  • Daniel Otto Jack Petersen: The Land Across shadows The City & the City structurally, with Kafka-esque qualities also in There Are Doors
  • Gerry Quinn: Last Letters from Hav is an early reference point, and the novel belongs to a line of fictional Balkan countries from Ruritania

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Myth and folklore sources, Women, sex and gender

2013-12-01 Andrew Mason #

Argues the Rajan acts as Silk, not Horn, when he contacts Seawrack, since he no longer considers himself married to Nettle. Responds to Lee's view that Silkhorn is fully Silk, citing the Rajan's remark about never using the contact while Nettle lived.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: The Rajan contacts Seawrack as Silk, not Horn; The Mother is Greater Scylla's sister; Undines descend from women of the drowned cities

Great question! One I think Marc would have no trouble answering as he That's certainly possible, though it would be tricksy - the 'return to your sons' native place as a stranger' bit would seem to fit only Horn, while 'find new love' can apply only to Silk. Though one might argue that Hoof and Hide are Silk's sons in a way, by adoption, since they call the Rajan 'Father' although, as becomes clear, they don't really think of him as Horn.

The Rajan says towards the end that he asked Greater Scylla how to contact the Mother, so he could find Seawrack again, but 'would never have used it while Nettle was alive'. Yet at the end, though Nettle is alive, he does use it - I would take it because he no longer sees himself as Horn and therefore no longer considers himself married to Nettle. That would support the idea that it is as Silk that he finally makes contact with Seawrack.

Regarding the Mother: at one point Horn speculates that she is in some way the same person as Scylla, but decides that she is not - they fulfil the same function, that of a water-goddess, but it makes sense to have different people doing this on different worlds. However, later events illuminate this a bit more; the Mother turns out to be in some sense the sister of Greater Scylla on Urth, and the Whorl goddess took her name from Greater Scylla, to whom she had dedicated herself. So, even without a genetic connection between the two, the link between Scylla and the Mother is not a pure coincidence. As for how the Mother and Greater Scylla can be sisters, there are some hints in _New Sun_ that the monsters came from space - so they may either have come from Blue, or come to both Blue and Urth from their original home.

I agree that Seawrack is in some way like Juturna, but Juturna's own status is a bit unclear. At one point she insists that she is human. Greater Scylla produces images of women to speak to the Rajan and Whorl Scylla, but later he seems to say that the undines are something else: 'There are women in that river, women who swim up from the sea. I do not speak of the feignings of the sea goddess, but of real women.' I would take it that the undines are descended form women of the drowned cities, taken prisoner by the sea-monsters - so this might actually support Seawrack's story of shipwreck.

2013-12-02 David Stockhoff replying to Lee #

Argues that sex in Wolfe's work is central because reproduction brings change, mortality and transcendence, while asexual beings like the gods and the Ascians stay static. Grounds this in Silk and Horn's sexual abstinence with Nettle, Baldanders' growth, the Urthiverse gods, Seawrack, Hy and Abaia's brides, citing the Fifth Head of Cerberus.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theology-and-religion · theories: Sex in Wolfe represents change and transcendence; Asexual immortals in Wolfe cannot evolve

Also, toward the end, Remora seeks a confession from SilkHorn, in regard to whether he has had sex with Nettle since his return. He has not, though some excuse is used rather than the truth obvious to everyone else which is "I am no longer Horn (or fully Horn)".

-y.

heh

But seriously. Clearly sex means something big to Wolfe. It is both a blessing and a curse, is it not? It enables Love, but also stands in its way. It allows increased mutation and faster evolution through death. It is no surprise that a converted Catholic would want to consciously and logically work it all out. A-sex is so much easier, but it is a brute-force approach to life. With no need to stop and put energy into a new generation, one could live forever---but without change. It is non-iterative, noncyclical, non-individual.

Recall the old cliches of angels wanting to be mortal so they could feel love, as well as experience the fear of death. Reproduction is creation and destruction; it is the basic way we interact with the material universe. Even more basic than eating/shitting, since without birth we as individuals would do neither.

Asexual monsters do not experience the universe in this way. Whereas the gods of the Urthiverse grow forever and become huge (like Baldanders tried to do), humans experience the potential for transcendence as well as evolution. The old Earth gods did have lives and families (all made up but based on human reality, just as were the gods of the Whorl), but as immortals they don't change, don't advance, don't evolve. Whatever powers one might gain from living forever they are nothing compared with ours. (Cue music.)

Is it any wonder sexual aliens do not often appear in Wolfe? And when they do, as in 5HC, where it is humans who are static, their "model" is hopelessly tangled and difficult to interpret. Larvae and sons are mentioned, but these cases need to be taken with a grain of salt. Seawrack is quite capable of sexuality, although almost surely without the ability to actually give birth. (Like Hy, if she is indeed male.) The Ascians reproduce sexually but have lost almost everything else through their imitation of Abaia, as far as humans can lose their humanity. And so on.

As you note, Abaia's sexuality is unclear, but all we have is a pronoun and the overtly feminine gender of his "brides," who themselves do not appear to reproduce (with whom would they?) except perhaps parthenogenetically, but they do keep growing. Unlike Seawrack.

Grounds this in the Gaon Echidna worship, the undines called Abaia's brides, the Baldanders path to godhood, and cacogens as mutated human stock, while admitting the relationships are not fully resolved.
