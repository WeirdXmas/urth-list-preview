Essential Background Reading for Soldier Series

Urth list · 13 messages · 2006-11-30 to 2007-01-15

20062007urthAlan LewisthalassocratCliff JudgeBob MillerDan RabinDon DoggettNils GöranssonMichael Straight
The thread: The thread began as a request for background reading before Soldier of Sidon. Don doggett recommended Homer, Herodotus, Roman history and Graves, while Alan Lewis and Bob Miller argued that little or no prior reading is needed, with Lewis citing Egyptian mythology and Bob Miller naming the first two Latro books. Michael Straight kept adding classical items, and Transentient challenged the claim about Spartan helots and asked for a source. Later posts shifted to a Soldier of Sidon discussion of the Hellenes' last page, the bulls-head amulet, and Sehuset's motives, and the thread drifted from reading lists into plot questions.
  • don doggett: classical reading (Homer, Herodotus, Roman history, Graves) recommended, later says no prior reading is required
  • Alan Lewis: no prior reading required; Egyptian background helps
  • Michael Straight: lists classical background and says historical details clarified the plot
  • Transentient: doubts the helot-slaughter story and wants a source
  • Bob Miller: lists prerequisites as time, the first two Latro books and some Egyptian mythology
  • Michael Straight: the bulls-head amulet is the one Sehuset described as ensuring loyalty, and Sabra's claim was a lie

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Memory and forgetting, Power, rulers and politics

2006-12-01 thalassocrat replying to Michael Straight #

Argues that historical events after Pausanias's death, including his conspiracy with Persia and the helot plot, Themistocles's exile, and Cleomenes's oracle, illuminate Latro's Greek episodes and the figure of Gaea. Grounds this in the historical record of the Spartan and Persian intrigues and in reinterpreting the oracle's lines as warnings about Latro's fate, then questions why Gaea's helot uprising plan fails.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Cleomenes's oracle warns Latro about his fate; Gaea's helot plan fails because she is old

On Fri, 01 Dec 2006 09:17:31 +1100 Michael Straight

I think knowing something about what happened afterwards is useful.

Pausanias was supposed to have conspired with the Persians with the intent of taking over one of the Spartan king positions. He was also supposed to have plotted to arm & free the helots in supports of this coup (or something like that; can't remember exact details). He was betrayed, and the ephors/"judges" locked him up in a temple & starved him to death.

Apart from the color this sheds on the Gaea/Artemis stuff, I think it also helps to make sense of the oracle spoken by Cleomenes at Acheron. "Seek peace" is obvious. "Don't drink from Lethe's cup" = don't rely on Latro the forgetful as a talisman of vistory. "Ask who will make the defenders of Sestos yield" = "Hunger", a warning about his eventual fate.

Themistocles was dogged by accusations of arrogance and corruption. Interestingly, one example of the former was supposed to have been his building a temple to Artemis next to his house (or something like that). He was exiled & then hounded out of Greece, also accused of Persian plots by the later Spartan leadership. He did in fact "defect", and ended up as an aristocratic subject of the Great King in charge of <somewhere>.

One thing I don't quite understand is Gaea's alternative plan for the helots: if she doesn't succeed with her Pausanias as helot- liberating king play, she will nevertheless use them to destroy Sparta via an uprising. There was some kind of uprising in the next decade, after an earthquake, but the Spartans suppressed it fairly quickly. After that I don't know of any real revolt until the helots were "freed" a few centuries later by some of the later kings. Is it just that Gaea in this part of the world is too old & feeble to make her plans work?

2007-01-14 Dan Rabin replying to Transentient #

Answers Transentient's question about the Hellenes' name for the last page by proposing 'colophon', and reads the book's ending as Wolfe's playful fictional providence for Lucius's writing. Predicts a Babylonia sequel and recalls that Soldier of Arete skipped Phoenicia, citing Lucius's vague sense of what he should record as evidence.
answers · source-and-allusion · theories: The last page is called a colophon; Lucius's book is a fictional colophon; Soldier of Sidon sets up a Babylonian sequel

Transentient <[email]> asks:

The best candidate I could find in an hour of online research was "colophon". This is the term used today for an account of the making of the book, such as the designer, the type selected, the paper and printing process chosen, the binder, and so forth. As in "written by Lucius on papyrus of Riverland with reed pen".

I thought this was just Wolfe being playful with the way he provides a fictional providence for the work. Lucius has a vague notion that he should be writing something like what I just suggested, only he can't remember any of the facts he should set down. Anyhow, the story isn't over just because the book is done. I think we're being set up for a sequel in Babylonia.

Of course, at the end of _Soldier of Arete_ I thought we were going to have a sequel in Phoenicia, but Wolfe skipped over that and merely gave us Phoenicians.

-- Dan Rabin

2007-01-14 Cliff Judge replying to Michael Straight #

Asks a series of questions about Soldier of Sidon, including what the Hellenes call the last page, which amulet Sehuset gave Myt-ser'eu, what Latro saw in Thotmaktef, what the scroll from the temple contains, whether Mehit wounded Latro's memory, and whether Falcata is a kopis. Opens by praising the book's structure and warning spoilers, noting the council scene as a shift in narrative expectations.
questions · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Mehit is the goddess who wounded Latro's memory

In case you didn't read Soldier of Sidon and are reading this, well, hey, what's taking you so long? It was good. Superbly done, in fact. But please delete this mail or put it away and come back to it because I don't want to spoil it for you.

One of the things that really stood out about the book was the overall narative structure. The part of the book dealing with the voyage down the river was smooth and sequential. Then there is the scene with the council discussing whether they should look for Kames and the mines, and it is really a discussion of whether or not the narrative structure should be changed. I've always loved how Wolfe can play games with your head by changing your expectations of the story. Sure enough, chaos ensues after Latro leaves the boat. The end of the book basically "perks up" as he starts returning north.

I've got a couple of questions that I'm wondering if anybody caught answers to in their readings so far.

1) Does anybody know what the Hellenes actually call the last page? (page 302)

2) Was the bulls-head amulet of Myt-ser'eu's the one that Sehuset made for her to seal her from Sabra? Or was it the one that he told Latro would ensure her loyalty to him?

3) Anybody know what it was about Thotmaktef that Latro, upon first meeting him, thought "Perhaps I should not write of it at all/" Was it just that he first noticed Thoth, the baboon at this point? (page 48)

4) What the heck is in that scroll that he recovered from the temple beyond the last temple? I'm wondering if that is actually mentioned and I just missed it.

5) Is it too obvious, given the lion symbolism, that Mehit is the very goddess who wounded his memory in the beginning? One thing that works against this is the passage where he helps Arensnuphis hunt her, and Latro thinks he sees astonishment in her eyes in being seen by him.

6) The glossary entry on Falcata indicates that she is, in fact, a Falcata. I seem to remember a passage in the first book where it is said that Falcata was actually a kopis. I wonder if there is anything to this.

Thanks for your thoughts.

2007-01-15 Michael Straight replying to Transentient #

Argues that the bulls-head amulet Sehuset gave Myt-ser'eu is the same item Sehuset described to Latro as ensuring her loyalty, and that Sabra's claim it was a tool for stealing her was a lie. Reasons that Sehuset's loyalty story was also a fiction meant to keep Latro wearing it around her neck, since Latro would have forgotten the danger of Sabra that day.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Bulls-head amulet is the same item in both accounts; Sabra's amulet claim was a lie; Sehuset's loyalty amulet was a fiction

I think they are the same.

I remember Sabra claiming the amulet was Sehuset's tool for stealing Myt-ser'eu from Latro. But that was a lie designed to manipulate Latro into delivering his wife into Sabra's hands.

I don't quite remember Sehuset telling Latro about an amulet to make his wife loyal, but I would guess that was also a fiction designed to do the opposite--get Latro to keep it safely around her neck--without having to explain everything about the danger of Sabra that Latro would have forgotten that day.

I think the item in your question #3 was what you said. Latro didn't want to mention the baboon.

-- Rostrum
