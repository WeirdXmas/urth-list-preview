1998-09-23 Michael Straight #

Agrees that the ending of Free Live Free is too abrupt and hints at a happy ending rather than showing one, while saying they enjoyed the book overall and found the four characters engaging. Compares its tone to There Are Doors, responds to David Wells's complaint about the slow opening, and asks what else David means by his joke.
supports · genre-and-craft

While I agree that that the payoff at the end was not as satisfying as I'd hoped (too abrupt: maybe I didn't understand everything, but I think Wolfe has a tendency to wimp out by hinting at a happy ending instead of trying to show us one--which is a lot harder to pull off), I enjoyed the book overall.

I'm not usually a fan of humdrum, "realistic" characters, but somehow Wolfe got me interested in these four. There are some good hooks in the begining (What kind of story IS this? Is she really a witch?) and I thought the hijinks (there's no other word for it) in the middle of the book were fun.

Somehow, the tone of the book reminds me a lot of _There Are Doors_. Do you it's just that they both have a contemporary setting?

1998-09-23 David Wells #

Argues that Free Live Free is a one-idea novel that drags for its first few hundred pages, and that it is essentially an allegory about the properties of composite materials. Responds to David Wells's request for a review by giving a negative verdict, citing Wolfe's Book of Days remark that novels should contain many ideas.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · genre-and-craft · theories: Free Live Free is an allegory about composite materials

I too found it very hard going indeed. It seemed to me like a writing exercise which had gotten out of hand to the tune of 500 pages or so (it seemed that long, anyway). Somewhere in Book of Days, Wolfe suggests that even a short story should have more than one idea, and that novels should have many. Free Live Free seemed to me to be too much of a one-trick-pony.

In terms of style/plot (and trying not to spoil too much) - there is a Good Reason why Wolfe spends so much time in the first few hundred pages describing the rather sad and humdrum lives of some not-terribly appealing characters. The big question is whether having struggled through this, you think the payoff is worth it. As is probably apparent, I didn't.
- The whole book (and I don't think this is really a spoiler unless you've read it!) is essentially an allegory about the properties of composite materials. Yes, I do think this a weird thing to write a novel about ;-).

newt

1998-09-23 Tony Ellis #

Questions David Wells's claim that Free Live Free is an allegory about the properties of composite materials, asking him to explain how he reaches that reading. Notes John Clute's view that the novel retells The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, and praises its refusal to fit one genre, comparing it to Titus Groan.
questions · source-and-allusion · genre-and-craft · theories: Free Live Free is an allegory of composite materials; Free Live Free retells The Wonderful Wizard of Oz

No offence intended, but having read Free Live Free several times, I can't for the life of me see how you arrive at this conclusion! I'm intrigued - could you explain, please?

John Clute says that it's a re-telling of The Wonderful Wizard of Oz, and I can certainly see that.

To throw in my two pence, I have always found Free Live Free fascinating, funny, and - far from being a one-trick-pony - packed with ideas and unusual insights. Christopher R. Culver asked in his original post "what style is it", and I would respond that one of this novel's great strengths is that it doesn't fit into any one pigeonhole. Like "Titus Groan" it's one of those books you can give to someone and say with total confidence "this isn't like anything else you've read before".
