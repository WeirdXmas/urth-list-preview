The thread: Readers debated whether Silk's personality survives in Horn in the Long Sun books. Rostrum argued little of Silk remains in the later books, and akt held that Horn and Silk share an eating problem, which Michael Straight recast as a physical limitation of Silk's body and a possible Imitatio Silki. 2001-01-30 Alice Turner #

Disputes Ratty's suggestion that inhumi have fairy-style weaknesses like cold iron, arguing they are animals with no supernatural resistances or weaknesses. Agrees with Rostrum that Horn and Silk have an eating problem evident in In Green's Jungles, and welcomes newcomer James to the list.
disputes · theories: Inhumi have no supernatural weaknesses

Ratty, Mark Millman is surely right--you are thinking of fairies and cold iron, and we ain't got none of those here. Inhumi have no supernatural resistances or weaknesses; they are animals like us. (And unlike a couple of other types of critters.)

Rostrum, IMO, is also right about Horn/Silk's eating problem, so evident in IGJ; I posited the same observation (I point out delicately) several times quite a while ago.

2001-01-31 Michael Straight #

Argues that Horn's eating problem in On Blue's Waters and In Green's Jungles could be a physical limitation of Silk's body rather than evidence that Silk's personality persists in Horn. Offers the alternative that Horn fasts as part of an Imitatio Silki, and notes that the only sign of Silk's personality is Horn attributing Seawrack's singing to Hyacinth.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn's eating problem is a physical limitation of Silk's body; Horn fasts as an Imitatio Silki

I may have missed your post in the archives, but most of the discussion I saw assumed it had something to do with Silk's personality influencing Horn, whereas I was pointing out that it could just be a physical limitation of Silk's body and prove nothing about how much of Silk's personality still remains.

For that matter, it could also be explained by Horn having decided to take up fasting as part of his Imitatio Silki.

In fact, the only indication I can see that any of Silk's personality is still in there is that bit where he refers to Seawrack's singing as being Hyacinth's.

-Rostrum

2001-01-31 Michael Straight #

Argues that the closing ball-catching lines of On Blue's Waters are Horn's metaphor, not Silk's memory, summing up the philosophy Horn voiced to Silk about using one's best gifts for others. Responds to Alex David Groce's claim that Silk's personality survives in those lines, citing Horn's role in Gaon's and Mora's lives and Silk's lame reply to the boys as evidence.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Horn narrates the ball-catching ending, not Silk

As I posted before, there's no reason to think Silk would even remember anything about catching the ball and winning the game - at the time he was preoccupied with his enlightenment (note his lame response when the boys ask him what was the lesson in it). Horn, on the other hand, obviously remembers it well, since it's the first scene in his book about Silk.

The line makes sense as a metaphor Horn uses to sum up the philosophy he articulated at the time to Silk: "You were their best player. It wouldn't have been fair to them if you hadn't played your best." Which he has lived out by helping Gaon. Which he continues to preach to Mora. It's a statement that Horn has, like Silk, used his gifts to help the weak.

The line makes no sense read as "Silk" literally remembering winning that game a long time ago. Why would he mention it? Does Silk habitually reminisce about his atheletic triumphs over children?

-Rostrum
