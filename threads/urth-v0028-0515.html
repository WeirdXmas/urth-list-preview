Religion of the New Sun

Urth list · 3 messages · 1999-11-23 to 1999-11-24

1999urthThe Book of the New SunOuroboros The WormWilliam H. AnsleyMatthew Malthouse
The thread: Posters debated whether Wolfe's Book of the New Sun is a fiction that invites Christian readings or a world whose religion stands apart from Christianity. Ouroboros argued that Severian is only a fictional, non-identical Christ-figure, citing Tolkien's letters on allegory, Wolfe's interview, and the Typhon temptation scene, and that Christian readings are misplaced. Matthew Malthouse countered that Wolfe's divine beings are reachable, which makes faith moot, while the promised New Sun and Second Coming parallel gives popular religion a root, and that allegory remains possible in Wolfe's world. William H. Ansley briefly agreed that religion can exist without ritual when gods are directly known, and suggested the reasoning might extend to Wolfe, without developing a specific argument. The thread ended without resolution across three posts.
  • Ouroboros: Severian is a fictional, non-identical Christ-figure; Christian readings of the Increate and saints are misplaced
  • Matthew Malthouse: Urth's reachable divine beings make faith moot, yet the New Sun and Second Coming parallel gives popular religion a root; allegory remains po
  • William H. Ansley: Religion without ritual works in Tolkien because gods are directly known, and the same may hold for Wolfe

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Gods, angels and the Increate

1999-11-23 Ouroboros The Worm #

Argues that Wolfe's Book of the New Sun is fiction, not speculative SF or theology, so Severian is only a fictional, non-identical Christ-figure and Christian readings of the Increate and saints are misplaced. Grounds this in Tolkien's letters on allegory, the St. Catherine of Alexandria motif, Wolfe's interview refusing to equate Severian with Jesus, and the Temptation in the Wilderness scene with Typhon.
disputes · theology-and-religion · author-and-biography · theories: Severian is a fictional messiah, not Christ; Wolfe's fiction is not Christian allegory

I seem to recall from the published edition of his
letters (publisher Houton-Mifflen sp?) that when
people asked J.R.R. Tolkien (also Catholic) if "Lord
of the Rings" were a Christian allegory, he said "No,
I just can't write that way".

I also, recall him conceding that despite his devout,
and practicing Faith, there was no real Religion in
Middle Earth (unless you count the Swarthy Men's
worship of Sauron), let alone a Judeo-Christian one.
Although the Middle Earth sub-creation was supposedly
placed in the nether-pre-historic past, it *was* still
sub-creation. To insert the super-reality of his Faith
into a fragile sub-creation would rend it to shreds
and blur the line between Fantasy and Reality.

While TBOTNS is set in the distant future, it is not
speculative SF. Wolfe is NOT trying to argue that this
is a possible future. I know there's an Increate in
TBOTNS, there's one at the beginning of the
Silmarilion. That's not religion. Wolfe is just trying
to tell an interesting, plausible story. So despite
the reference to a Christian saint, Wolfe is NOT
speculating about the possible future of the Body of
Christ.

St. Catherine of Alexandria is a useful motif for
TBOTNS because roses and torture are an important
element of her story. I'll also point out that of all
the canonized saints Wolfe could have selected, he
chose one whose very existence is all but certainly
fictional, whose feast day the Catholic Church has
removed from the calendar.

Finally, in an interview Wolfe balked at the
opportunity to say that "Severian is Jesus". He said
instead...I know it's not in the story's text but he
said it none the less...that Severian is a
"Christ-figure" not Jesus. While he's suggested that
he believes Jesus made crosses (ala "The Last
Temptation of Christ"), Wolfe clearly does not intend
to map "one to one" Severian and Wolfe's Lord and
Savior Jesus Christ. Roy's right. It would be
blasphemous to equate Jesus and Severian.

Severian is a kind of messiah ("promised one") and
Wolfe points to that fact by creating a Temptation in
the Wilderness scene between Sev and Typhon. He's the
"head" and "savior of his race". But he's only "a"
messiah not "the" Messiah. And a fictional one to
boot.

So speculation about how the biblical promises of God
and the Hope of the faithful apply in TBOTNS is
senseless because they don't. One is a something Wolfe
has committed his life to and the other is a story he
made up.

-Ouroboros

1999-11-23 Matthew Malthouse #

Argues that Wolfe's Urth differs from Tolkien's Middle-earth because its divine beings are reachable, making faith moot, while the promised New Sun and Second Coming parallel gives popular religion a root. Responds to Ouroboros's quoted claim that Tolkien kept Middle-earth free of his Faith by suggesting that an allegorical statement remains possible in Wolfe's world.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: The New Sun parallels the Second Coming; Urth's Heirogrammates know the Increate directly

although the world of Middle Earth has no overt worship or ritual that's not quite the same thing as saying that there is no religion.

One profound difference between Middle earth and our world is that Manwe and the Valar knew Illuvatar and the Eldar knew the Valar - even men have known some of the Maia, even if only the wise recognise this. There is a certainty possible to them, even at several removes, that has no analogue in this worlds faiths. Indeed 'faith' is not required when any passing elf could supply you with the chief angel's address.

The situation on Urth is not dissimilar. Although we have ritual with forms that we recognise as religious and orders such as the Pelegrines we also have the Heirogramates who appear to have a closer connection to the Increate of that universe than we do. Again faith becomes moot if one can board ship and visit the Increate's servants.

Faith however does rear its head (ugly or otherwise) in the matter of the New Sun. In its promised coming we seem to have some root for popular religion. We also have a parallel for the Second Coming.

Indeed that is not to indicate that here we have a predictive statement directly about Christianity. What there is still room for however is that which Tolkien denied writing: an alegorical statement. Today God or the Increate is not susceptible to proofs of reality. What might change if at some future date it were otherwise?

Matthew

1999-11-24 William H. Ansley #

Agrees with a quoted point that Middle-earth lacks overt ritual yet has religion, because its peoples directly know their divine beings, and says the same reasoning might apply to The Book of the New Sun. Responds to an earlier post comparing Tolkien's world with Wolfe's, without developing a specific argument about Severian's world.
supports

Exactly so! I have never been able to understand how anyone could complain that there is no religion in _The Lord of the Rings_ when it is obvious that there is no need for religion as we know it in that world.

I had not though of applying the same argument to TBotNS, however. I'll have to give this more thought.

William Ansley
