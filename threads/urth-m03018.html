Jolenta the beautiful via Talos

Urth list · 8 messages · 2006-10-30 to 2006-11-04

2006urthThe Book of the New SunScott KBob MillerAdam ThorntonRex Lycanthrosaurusb sharpDavid DiGiacomoJack Redelfs
The thread: Scott K opened by arguing that Talos's glamour, not surgery, made Jolenta beautiful, a costly illusion that wore her body out. b sharp countered that the specific medical and magical procedures described, including the Cumaean's treatments, point to real physical transformation. Bob Miller and Scott K then leaned toward confidence and glamour, while David DiGiacomo questioned why Talos would bother with invasive implants when glamour would do. The thread then drifted into an unrelated dispute about Phaedrus's name and copyediting errors in Wolfe's books, ending without resolution on Jolenta.
  • Scott K: beauty came from glamour or fast biochemical enhancement, not surgery
  • b sharp: beauty comes from the specific medical and magical procedures Talos performed
  • Bob Miller: attractiveness came from confidence Talos gave her, like Marilyn Monroe's persona
  • David DiGiacomo: surgery with implants is implausible when glamour or confidence would suffice
  • Adam Thornton: Phaedrus means Wolf, and he leans toward Borski's werewolf reading
  • Rex Lycanthrosaurus: Phaedrus means light, and Wolfe's books have poor copyediting

Themes: Masks, disguise and shapeshifting, Robots, androids and artificial life, Names and their meanings

2006-10-30 Scott K #

Argues that Talos's glamour, not surgery, made Jolenta beautiful, and that the glamour was a costly illusion that faded and wore her body out. Grounds this in Talos's own words about casting the glamour and in the faerie and Zeus senses of the term, and suggests Baldanders' laboratory experiments as the source.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Talos's glamour, not surgery, made Jolenta beautiful; Baldanders' experiments produced Talos's glamour potion

David Di Giacomo notes: Talos's surgical skill made Jolenta irresistably attractive to everyone, even Severian's dead grandmother.

I do like this idea, whatever the true source of Sev's notion of a tryst between Dorcas and Jolenta, or whether it happened or not. Jolenta was a walking aphrodesiac to many, including Jonas and the cash-holding members of any potential audience. Instant Marilyn Monroe treatment, anyone? Sure to increase your box office. However, I think it is wrong to attribute Jolenta's beauty to any surgical skills of Dr. Talos. That would be a case of explaining his abilities in terms of our own age, i.e., he is a doctor so it may have been a kind of plastic surgery as we know today, or something faster and more advanced. But the foxy doctor does say after meeting the thin waitress with straggling hair at breakfast, "I will make you beautiful because we require you as an actress. It is one of my powers," and more importantly, "...I must cast the glamour and teach you your lines, all in a day."
Though not in common usage today (raise the Wolfean caution flag), the term glamour didn't always mean mere beauty or celebrity as it does to us, but it is a kind of illusory spell that the faeries of old might use to make a handful of flower petals seem like gold coins to a human, or as Zeus might use to look like a mortal woman's husband so he could gain her bed for a night. As we see later, the glamour rapidly fades from poor Jolenta, at a terrible cost to her mortal span. The hidden cost in the deal, she was made to burn more brightly, though more briefly. She did not have the basic muscular strength to carry her enhanced frame's fuller thighs and breasts etc., and tired much more easily than someone who'd developed them naturally. I believe the Doctor may have cast this glamour through some potion or advanced bio-mutagen related to Baldanders' weird experiments in his castle laboratory (the captive boy).
-Scott Kimball

2006-10-31 b sharp replying to Scott K #

Argues that Jolenta's beauty comes from the specific medical and magical procedures Dr. Talos performed, not from ordinary surgery. Cites the Cumaean's scene at the end of The Claw of the Conciliator, listing the silk threads in her hair, ground and capped teeth, skin broths, skeletal wires, and a redistributing potion, and compares it to Thecla's belladonna eyes.
supports · close-textual-reading

Scott K writes: (snips)

Some good points are raised. I'll mention that the scene with The Cumaean, at the end of Claw, spells out in rather specific detail what was done to Jolenta. As I remember, silk threads were sewn into her hair, her teeth ground down and capped, healthening broths cleared up her skin and metal wires and bands were attached to her skeleton. The potion (the effect of which is lost as she loses blood) redistributes her flesh. Perhaps her large eyes were achieved through a belladonna type poison, as it was for Thecla as a girl. Definitely a faster process than whatever we might have today- Jolenta was ready for the stage a few days after her treatment.

-bsharp

2006-11-03 Bob Miller replying to b sharp #

Argues that Jolenta's attractiveness came from the confidence Talos gave her rather than from surgery, likening her to Norma Jean's Marilyn Monroe persona. Builds on b sharp's point that glamour is an illusory spell, citing the story of a young woman who offered street passersby a meeting with Monroe before she turned on her persona.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Jolenta's beauty came from confidence gained under Talos; Jolenta's attractiveness is glamour not surgery

Scott K writes: (snips)

Very interesting, thank you. I had remembered the "adornments" Talos had made to Jolenta. It had always seemed to me that a good deal of her attractiveness came from the confidence that she gained under Talos. Why? I well remember stories of a young and slightly mousy looking lady bundled up in loose clothes going up to people on the streets of New York City and asking, "Would you like to meet Marilyn Monroe?" And if the answer was yes, throwing back her hood and turning on her persona with a big smile. This was while Norma Jean was studying with Lee Strasberg.

With her Sun(Talos) leaving her, there was no energy left for her to "shower?" on the rest of the world.

2006-11-03 David DiGiacomo replying to Scott K #

Questions why Talos would perform invasive surgery on Jolenta, with wires and metal placed under her flesh, when a glamor or confidence could have made her beautiful. Asks where the surgery took place and how she recovered so quickly, raising the possibility of work inside the flying saucer while noting it would have been conspicuous. Responds to Scott K.'s remark that Jolenta's attractiveness came from confidence gained under Talos.
questions · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic

Severian sees "wires and bands of metal" that were placed under Jolenta's flesh. Why would Talos bother with that if he could make her beautiful with a "glamor" or by raising her self esteem?

What I have always wondered is where Talos took her to perform this invasive surgery, and how she recovered so quickly. I suppose it's possible that the flying saucer descended and Talos did the work in there, but it would have been conspicuous, to say the least.

2006-11-04 Scott K replying to Scott K #

Proposes that Jolenta's beauty owes more to a glamour or fast biochemical enhancement than to surgery, and wonders whether Talos could use the lighter machine technology that made Jonas's mech body. Raises doubts about the metal wires Severian sees under her flesh, citing Severian's remark that she was heavy and asking why Talos would bother with implants at all.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Jolenta's beauty is a glamour or biochemical enhancement; Talos's enhancements use cruder iron parts

I have to admit I forgot about the wires, metal and such- I'm just re-reading Conciliator now, first time in about 6 years. Recently finished Return To Whorl and Solar Labyrinth (and joined this group), so I'm plunging in from the beginning again, like that swim in fetid Gyoll, so long ago. I stand corrected by both of you who posted about the metal and hair implants.
But surely it is more than just surgery at work (whatever passes for surgery in that far-flung time to those of a class to have access to Baldanders' or Hierodule level of technology), so that the overall effect is as a glamour in the old sense, an illusory or fast biochemical enhancement as if by magic.
I wonder if Talos could use the machine technology that made Jonas' original mech body, which was so much lighter than flesh. Or do I remember somewhere that Sev says Jolenta was rather heavy? Perhaps Talos' enhancement are not as refined as Jonas-tech, or uses more common iron parts or something. Not that there's as much metal used on Jolenta, much of it is clearly flesh.
-Scott Kimball

2006-11-04 Adam Thornton replying to Jack Redelfs #

Reports that the poster has received a new Wolfe book and complains that Borski's essay collection repeatedly misspells a name as a plural. Points out that Borski misses the onomastic hint in The Fifth Head of Cerberus that Phaedrus means Wolf, making Phaedria an obvious echo, and says he is inclined to agree with Borski's werewolf reading.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Phaedrus means Wolf in Fifth Head

First: _Sidon_ arrived today. Haven't opened it yet.

Second: Borski, _Long and Short_. One of the essays is atrociously edited: he keeps saying "Pendelton's" for the plural. His editor should have caught this. Also, he doesn't mention, that I noticed, one of the big hints in _5HC_ onomastics: "Phaedrus" means "Wolf"; "Phaedria" is therefore pretty obvious. I'm not all that far into it; I think he might be right about the werewolf thing.

Adam

2006-11-04 Rex Lycanthrosaurus replying to Adam Thornton #

Disputes Adam Thornton's claim that Phaedrus means 'Wolf', arguing it means 'light' and linking the mistake to Pirsig's Zen. Also complains about copyediting errors in Wolfe's books and jokes about Warren Zevon's werewolves. Cites the Garvaon/Garsecg confusion in The Wizard Knight and a 'one hand is dead' error in The Book of the New Sun as evidence of poor copyediting.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Phaedrus means light, not wolf

Self-published books do not have editors--not that that makes much difference given the overall atrocious copyediting of Mr. Wolfe's works by alleged professionals. Remember the Garvaon/Garsecg confusion in TWK or the amphisbaena whose "one *hand* is dead" in _tBotNS_? If memory serves, _Soldier of Sidon_ was originally scheduled to be published on 10-17, but then had its release date pushed back by two weeks; was it, I wondered, because newly discovered typos needed to be corrected? (Kudos to the TOR design team at any rate -- this is easily Wolfe's best-looking book.)

Also, he doesn't mention, that I noticed,

*Groan.* Absolutely not true, and yet it's accepted by millions of readers because Robert Pirsig makes the claim in _Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance_. (An interesting parallel is Mark Twain's assertion that mummies were used for fuel in Egyptian trains -- a canard that is still accepted today as fact.) Pirsig corrects the mistake in his most recent introduction to _Zen_; rather, if memory serves (since I can't find the book), Phaedrus means "light."

And while the subject of werewolves has been broached, I wonder if Gene Wolfe is fond of Warren Zevon's music, and especially his "Werewolves of London." The name 'Zevon' apparently derives from the Russian Zevotosky, "Son of the Wolf.' Ahooooo!
