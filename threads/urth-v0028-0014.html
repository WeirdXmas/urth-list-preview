de Sade, Plato, and Jack the Ripper.....

Urth list · 4 messages · 1999-07-22 to 1999-07-22

1999urthThe Book of the New SunJonathan LaidlowMichael Andre-DriussiPeter StephensonAlex David Groce
The thread: Jonathan Laidlow asked where the de Sade, Plato and Jack the Ripper references cited by Budrys actually appear in The Shadow of the Torturer, and proposed that the list collectively trace them. Alex Groce and Michael Andre-Driussi offered concrete hooks: Groce pointed to Platonic Ideals behind Severian's symbol-making remark and argued de Sade and the Ripper suit The Fifth Head of Cerberus better, while Andre-Driussi cited torturers' algophilia, beatings of prostitutes and the evil twins' killings. Peter Stephenson argued the references are generic, suggesting a decadent aristocratic world, citing the autarch as a Platonic philosopher-ruler and the film Murder by Decree as a likely source of Budrys's association. The thread ended without consensus, with the allusions largely left as suggestive rather than confirmed.
  • Jonathan Laidlow: asks for textual locations of the Budrys allusions and wants a collective hunt
  • Alex Groce: Plato is the easiest source; de Sade and Ripper fit Fifth Head of Cerberus better
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: faint de Sade and Ripper traces in Torturer via torture, beatings and twins
  • Peter Stephenson: references are generic, evoking decadent aristocracy; Murder by Decree may have inspired Budrys

Themes: Justice, torture and mercy, Power, rulers and politics, Books, libraries and writing

1999-07-22 Jonathan Laidlow #

Asks where the references to de Sade, Plato and Jack the Ripper appear in The Shadow of the Torturer, after quoting a 1980 Budrys review that praises the book's references. Proposes that list members jointly tease out these allusions from the text.
questions · source-and-allusion

'With its references to DeSade, Plato and Jack the Ripper, this is a fully realized culture, utterly strange and utterly believeable, as might be expected from the author of 'The Fifth Head of Cerberus'' - - Algys Budrys, review of 'Shadow of the Torturer', The Magazine of Fantasy and Science Fiction vol.58 no. 5 May 1980.

As I became bored of de Sade (!), am ignorant of Plato, and just plain confused by Ripperology, where are these references? Wouldn't it be fun if we teased them out?

Jonathan

1999-07-22 Alex David Groce #

Proposes that Plato is the easiest source to find in Wolfe, linking Severian's 'we invent symbols' passage to Platonic Ideals. Argues de Sade and Jack the Ripper fit The Fifth Head of Cerberus better than The Shadow of the Torturer, since Urth is more Byzantine decadent than Victorian London; replies to Peter Stephenson.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Severian's symbols passage echoes Plato's Ideals; de Sade and Ripper elements belong to The Fifth Head of Cerberus

I think Plato is definitely the easy one of these to find. My guess would be Severian's "We believe that we invent symbols" passage--which I showed a friend as an example of Wolfe's writing, and he said "Nice writing, but it's just Plato's old stuff about Ideals." Maybe not exactly, but certainly related.

Jack the Ripper and de Sade somehow seem more likely to be 5HC elements than in Shadow. . . Urth is more Byzantine decadent than Victorian London/Justine decadent.

1999-07-22 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Agrees with Budrys that Greek-style terms like agathodaemon point toward Plato, and proposes faint de Sade and Jack the Ripper traces in The Shadow of the Torturer. Lists possible hooks: torturers' algophilia, beatings of prostitutes, the evil twins' killings and Agilus's talking skull, and answers Alex Groce's Plato guess.
supports · source-and-allusion · theories: Greek terminology evokes Plato's Ideals; Torturers' algophilia echoes de Sade; Brothel and cab scenes echo Jack the Ripper; Evil twins' killings echo the Ripper

Re: the Budrys line in review of THE SHADOW OF THE TORTURER.

I'd agree with that. Add details like Thecla's mention of "agathodaemon." Perhaps all the Hellenic/Hellenistic terms, even the names of weapons, add to a "Greek-ness" which then led Budrys to "Plato."

For traces of de Sade, I'd say offhand: "Algedonic" and any issues of algophilia among the torturers, as discussed by Severian (although I'm not sure how much of this is done in SHADOW itself).

For traces of Jack the Ripper, I'd guess: issues of beating prostitutes, which comes up first in the cab ride and later at the brothel itself (of course, this has de Sadean applications as well). Maybe the casual mention of the murdered body near the cafe, even though this seems to be more the victim of a thugee attack than that of a knifeman. Maybe the serial killings perpetrated by the evil twins. Maybe even the hallucinatory talking skull trick (that Agilus uses in the shop) seems like the visions of or by a ripper?

Since Budrys was only writing a brief review, it is probably nothing more than a few details such as these, which stuck in his mind as possible hooks for other readers.

=mantis=

1999-07-22 Peter Stephenson #

Argues that the de Sade, Plato and Jack the Ripper references Budrys cited in The Shadow of the Torturer are generic, not specific, and offers a guess that they evoke a decadent aristocratic world. Grounds this in Plato's philosopher-ruler resembling the autarch, the concubines Thecla and her relatives, and the 1979 film Murder by Decree, which may have prompted Budrys's association.
proposes · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion · theories: Ripper and de Sade references are generic decadence markers; Autarch resembles Plato's philosopher ruler; Budrys associated Shadow with Murder by Decree

Yes, it would, although maybe he's just saying Wolfe refers to anything and everything and has picked some extreme examples, like Polonius ends his list of things the players in Hamlet can perform with `scene individable and poem unlimited', whatever that means. But it's fun to make dodgy hypotheses based on tenuous connections.

Plato, I suppose, means the ideal republic of the Republic, with its philosopher ruler, which isn't so far off what the autarch is (in practice, I mean, when we actually meet him --- pedants will want to wreck this by noting that we don't in Shadow, apart from an appearance in the House Azure which isn't explained till later). You could probably make an argument that the New Sun was the Form of the Good, but that would be a bit vague even for my taste. Nor does it seem particularly outr'e to suggest the Commonwealth is less than ideal. Was he thinking of cave dwellers, looking at the world in shadow? Or (I may well be doing Budrys a disservice) does Plato mean `some Greek guy with old-fashioned ideas about society'?

As for the others --- neither aristocratic debauchery and perversion, nor the systematic murder of prostitutes are major themes in Wolfe, although I hesitate in case anyone has a piece of fan fiction ready. Here's my guess: both de Sade and Jack the Ripper are generic references to a decadent (in Wolfe's case `decayed' would seem more appropriate) world of aristocratic (i.e. exultant) vice --- we already know in `Shadow', for example, that Thecla and her relatives are concubines.

There was a 1979 film, `Murder by Decree', in which Jack the Ripper turned out to be the Duke of Clarence, mad son of Victoria and Albert (there, I've spoilt it for you, sorry); the case was investigated by Sherlock Holmes (Christopher Plummer) and Dr Watson (James Mason). They managed to cram the freemasons, and even werewolves if the quote below is anything to go by (though I can't remember that bit), into the story, too. `Oliver Stone would love this plot' is a comment in the IMDB review (see http://us.imdb.com/Title?0079592). Coming a year or so before Shadow of the Torturer, with a strange city (they always do that to Victorian London, but I suppose Dickens got there first), and a proto-Wolfean mixing of themes, it might have been enough to suggest to Budrys that `Shadow' had Jack-the-Ripper connotations.

Holmes: We've unmasked madmen, Watson, wielding sceptres. Reason run riot. Justice howling at the moon.

corncrake
