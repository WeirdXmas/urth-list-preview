More on Silk-Horn's eating habits

Urth list · 6 messages · 2002-02-19 to 2002-02-19

2002urthThe Book of the Short SunThe Book of the Long SunTom FosterAndy RobertsonJames JordanMichael Straight
The thread: Readers debated whether Silk-Horn, the narrator of Wolfe's Long Sun and Short Sun stories, is an inhumi, prompted by his eating habits, a sinister line at the end of On Blue's Waters, and John Clute's claim. Andy Robertson proposed that an inhumu drained Horn in the green jungle and left him believing he was human, so the inhumu's soul now inhabits Silk. Michael Straight argued against this, noting inhumi do not take on victims' memories, that the Neighbor ring retrieval makes a dead inhumu body implausible, and that the 'girl' is the Rajan's concubine. James Jordan offered a mundane reading of the old-feeling line, and Tom Foster raised the question without settling it. The thread ended with the positions still apart and no consensus reached.
  • Andy Robertson: Horn was drained by an inhumu and came to think he was human, so Silk carries the inhumu's soul
  • Michael Straight: inhumi don't take on victims' memories, and a dead inhumu body would have been noticed; the 'girl' is the Rajan's concubine
  • James Jordan: the old-feeling line is innocent, since an old man near young company naturally feels younger
  • Tom Foster: Silk's eating and the sinister closing line may support John Clute's inhumi reading

Themes: Robots, androids and artificial life, Beasts and the animal in us, Ghosts, dreams and visions

2002-02-19 Michael Straight #

Disputes Andy Robertson's idea that Horn was drained by an inhumu and came to think he was human, arguing inhumi do not take on victims' memories or personality. Cites Fava's behavior after drinking from Mora's grandmother, Krait's bond with Horn through his mother's draining of Sinew, and the expectation that an inhumu in Silk's body would eat more, not less.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Horn was drained by an inhumu and became Silk; Silk is used to fasting, Horn is not

I don't think the inhumu soul-stealing works that way.

For one thing, there's no other evidence of an inhumu gaining memories (or even much personality) from the person he/she's drained. Fava feels the urge to appear like an old lady after she drinks from Mora's grandmother, but she doesn't show any signs of taking on the grandmother's memories or personality.

Secondly, if there's any sense in which an inhumu gains personality from someone who has been drained of blood, it is in the second generation. Krait is drawn to Horn, and is so much like a son to him--extending to the love/hate relationship, because his mother drained Sinew. I guess you could say that Horn was drained by an inhumu who subsequently had children and that one of those children then had adventures on Green thinking he was Horn (and somehow getting hold of Horn's neighbor ring) and then sent his hybrid soul into Silk, but that seems to be wandering pretty far into fan fiction territory.

The strongest objection to this idea is that, based on the actions of inhumi when they spirit travel and gain human bodies, an inhumu spirit in Silk's body would revel in eating and having sex and doing all the other human things it was unable to do in its reptilian body. If there were an inhumu in there, Silk would be eating more, not less, so you'd need some other explanation for why he's not eating.

To me, the "Silk is used to fasting, but Horn isn't" explanation is the simplest.

-Rostrum

2002-02-19 Michael Straight #

Disputes Michael Straight's theory that Horn's body was a drained dead inhumu, arguing the theory conflicts with later events. Cites the scene where the narrator, Hoof and Jahlee retrieve the Neighbor ring from Horn's lander and leave it with Sinew, reasoning that people would have noticed a dead inhumu.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Horn's body was a dead inhumu drained of blood

Yet another problem with this theory. When the narrator and Hoof and Jahlee travel to Green and visit Sinew's city, they go out to the lander where Horn died and retrieve the Neighbor ring and leave it with Sinew. Surely people would have noticed and made a fuss if Horn's body had actually been a dead inhumu.

-Rostrum

2002-02-19 Michael Straight #

Argues that the 'girl' in Horn's closing line of On Blue's Waters is the concubine the Rajan escaped with, not Seawrack. Agrees with Tom Foster that the line reads sinisterly, but rejects the idea that the narrator is an inhumu, suggesting Wolfe teases readers with that suspicion.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn's 'girl' is the Rajan's concubine; Horn may be an inhumu, teased by Wolfe

I think "the girl" there is the concubine the Rajan escaped with. I agree, that sentence does lend itself to sinister interpretation.

I don't think the narrator is an inhumu, but I'm starting to think Wolfe meant to tease us with the suspicion that he might be.

-Rostrum

2002-02-19 James Jordan #

Argues that the narrator's line about feeling old after the girl's company in Out of Blue's Waters has a simple, non-sinister reading: an old man near a young concubine or children naturally feels younger, and mood can shift this. Responds to Tom Foster's suggestion that the 'girl' is a concubine the Rajan escaped with, and jokingly notes Wolfe's own age and grandchildren.

2002-02-19 Tom Foster #

Asks whether Silk-Horn's enthusiastic eating and drinking in Return to the Whorl, along with his neat writing, supports or undercuts John Clute's claim that the protagonist is an inhumi. Also flags a sinister line at the end of On Blue's Waters, where the narrator says he felt old instead of younger after Brother and Sister, citing the girl as a possible reference.
questions · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Silk-Horn is an inhumi

Following on from my question about this the other day, I have now read the first 150 pages or so of RTTW. Two passages stood out with respect to the narrator's eating habits, both of which concern him eating/drinking enthusiastically. These are just about the only times I can remember that the narrator feels this way. The two instances occur at the beginning of the chapter 'Haunting Dorp', in which he enthusiastically describes all the food that he is brought as "a feast", and in the chapter 'Dark Empty Rooms', in which he drinks wine several times. One of these instances occurs in his present, and one in his past. I'm not sure what to make of it all, but it does seem strange.

I've been reading a lot of conversations in the archives and was interested to read about John Clute's review of IGJ, in which he apparently stated that it was fairly evident that the protagonist of the books is an inhumi. There was some debate about it, but what do people make of it now? I'm not at all convinced, though I can see several arguments to support it, as well as several to dispute it (the obvious ones being the eating/drinking and the ability to write neatly, though perhaps this could be achieved through being an inhumi trapped in a human's body?) One thing that struck me as slightly sinister is a sentence at the end of OBW. The narrator writes "Brother and Sister should have made me feel younger, as the girl did. I felt old instead". It isn't clear who the 'girl' he is referring to might be (Seawrack?), but I think that one could interpret this statement in several ways...

Tom

2002-02-19 Andy Robertson #

Proposes that Horn was drained by an inhumu in the green jungle and afterward only believed himself human, so Silk carries the inhumu's soul and the inhumu carries Horn's. Grounds the idea in Silk's reluctance to eat and in his physical humanity, shown by fathering children at Gaon, and responds to John Clute's review of In Green's Jungles.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Horn was drained by an inhumu on Green; Silk is a triple hybrid with inhumu soul; Silk-on-Blue is physically human

I think it is possible that when Horn went to Green he was sucked dry by an inhumu and thereafter merely thought he was human.

I cannot pin down the spot but I think it might be after he leaves the City and is lost in the jungle. The rest of his striving on Green is the striving of an inhumu who thinks he is a man.

Silk-on-Blue is therefore a triple hybrid: Silk has the inhumu's soul: the inhumu has Horn's soul. Hence Silk carries many inhumu characteristics, including reluctance to eat.

Silk-on-blue is of course *physically* human, since he begets children at Gaon.

hartshorn
