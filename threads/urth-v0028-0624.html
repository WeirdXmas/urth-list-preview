Typhon and Pas

Urth list · 3 messages · 1999-12-13 to 1999-12-13

1999urthThe Book of the New SunMitchell A. BaileyGreene, CarltonMichael Andre-Driussi
The thread: Readers debated whether Typhon and Pas, the two-headed figures in Wolfe's Urth and Whorl episodes, are the same being and whether the books share one milieu. Michael Andre-Driussi partly supported Mitchell Bailey's observation but argued the timing of the Whorl scanning means Severian meets Typhon after the head removal, and that Typhon's tampering with boarding minds explains the continuity gap. Mitchell Bailey disputed the identification, contrasting Typhon's concealed postoperative state with Pas's openly displayed two heads and citing the chiliarch and curtained monarch scenes. Carlton Greene countered that Typhon's two-headed state was a deliberate, god-like display, citing Janus, fairy-tale giants, and a practical need to wait for the spare head to atrophy. The thread ends in impasse, with no shared resolution of the continuity question.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: Typhon and Pas are linked; timing and Typhon's tampering with minds explain the continuity gap
  • Mitchell A. Bailey: Typhon's concealed condition contradicts Pas's displayed two heads, so Urth and Whorl may not share one milieu
  • Carlton Greene: Typhon's two heads were a deliberate god-like display, linking him to Pas's bicephalic simulation

Themes: Doubles, twins and clones, Gods, angels and the Increate, Robots, androids and artificial life

1999-12-13 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Agrees with Mitchell Bailey's observation about Typhon and Pas but rejects his final paragraph, arguing the scanning for the Whorl happened before the slave's head was removed, so Severian meets Typhon afterward. Adds that a god fittingly has two heads and that Typhon's tampering with the minds of people boarding the Whorl explains away any continuity problem, while Urth's palace guards would notice the change.
supports · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon's scanning preceded the slave's decapitation; Typhon tampered with the minds of Whorl passengers

I agree with everything but the last paragraph.

I took the situation as meaning that the "scanning" for the Whorl had happened before the slave's head had been removed; thus sometime before Severian met Typhon in Typhon's era; which is obvious and self-evident.

I would add to your notions that it would be more fitting for a god to have two heads. That is true and I agree. Here's the addition: we know that Typhon was seriously messing with the heads of all the people (and embryoes) going onboard the Whorl, thus there is no "continuity" problem for them; whereas on Urth, the palace guards are saying, hey, what's the deal--bossman had only one head yesterday!

=mantis=

1999-12-13 Mitchell A. Bailey #

Contrasts Typhon's concealed postoperative condition in the Urth books with the bicephalic Pas of the Whorl, who openly displays his disfigurement. Cites Severian's scenes with the chiliarch and the curtained monarch, and argues this contradiction calls into question the theory that Urth and Whorl share one milieu.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Urth and Whorl do not share one milieu; Typhon hides his surgery from his subjects

One curious contrast I noticed between the postoperative recuperating Typhon of Urth and the bicephalic simulation Pas of the Whorl.

Typhon of Urth was rather reticent about his, er, condition when the Conciliator showed up. The monarch had retired to his work camp in the mountains where his monument was being carved while he recovered (that is, until Piaton should outlive his usefulness and be amputated like a tumour). The chiliarch threatened Sev with death if he should speak of anything he might see or hear, and ensured the troopers could not see the monarch when the curtain was drawn. I think we can guess what it is Typhon did not want spoken of, and it seems clear that he does not wish his subjects generally to see or know about his surgery or the means by which his intended rejuvenation was supposed to occur.

He appeared readily enough to Sev in both _Sword_ and UNS, but those were special cases out of sight of his ordinary subjects.

On the other hand, the Typhon who was simulated as Pas seemed to have no problem whatsoever with the people on the Whorl seeing his disfigurement, and in fact flaunted it as a sort of trademark.

Other than to say that the Cargo of _Whorl_ were permanently removed from Urth and what is an acceptable appearance for a god might be unsuitable for a monarch, it seems to be a bit of a contradiction in attitudes. This could call into question the theory that Urth and Whorl occupy the same milieu after all.

1999-12-13 Greene, Carlton #

Argues that Typhon's two-headed state after surgery reads as a deliberate, god-like display of magical technology rather than mere embarrassment, linking it to Pas's bicephalic simulation on the Whorl. Grounds this in mythic precedents like Janus and fairy-tale giants, a practical need to wait for the spare head to atrophy, and the self-image Typhon left aboard the Whorl.
proposes · myth-and-structure · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon's two heads as deliberate divine display

I always understood that the chiliarch was protecting against news of Typhon's weakened, post-operative condition, and not the fact that he would be sporting two heads for a while. Although we might see Typhon's status as horridly embarrasing, it might also be seen as an impressive, god-like display of magical technology. Such a display is consistent with Typhon's deity status on the Whorl generation ship. It is also a peculiarly symbol-laden technological achievement, and gives Typhon more of the mythological touches that a good deity needs. A two-headed man has a rich grounding in myth (Janus and fairy tale giants spring to mind), much more so than a man who achieves immortality by taking a high-tech multivitamin.

As a practical matter, it might have taken too long for Typhon to wait for his spare head to atrophy before returning to his empire, leading him to make the best of it and exploit his two-heads' symbolism as best he could. Are we told how long it would take for Piaton to atrophy?

Having said all this, it does seem odd that he would choose his two-headed self as the self-image he bequeathed to posterity aboard the whorl, given that he planned a return to one-headedness. Maybe the mythological power of that symbol was more important than the man he would become.

C/
