2010urthThe Book of the New SunGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffLee Berman
The thread: Readers debated whether Christ, Jesus, or Christian concepts appear in The Book of the New Sun, with the Outsider cast as a creator god. Gerry Quinn argued that Jesus and Christian analogues are present despite gnostic names, citing a lion-and-lamb Eucharist and Wolfe calling Severian a Christian figure. Lee Berman countered that the Gnostic references like Abraxas and Demiurge dominate, while Jesus is not clearly present and placing Christ there is an unjustified leap. David Stockhoff disputed both sides, saying Christ is absent, proposing an iterated-cycle reading, and treating Dionysus as a strong allusion. The thread ended in impasse with no shared conclusion.
  • Gerry Quinn: Jesus and Christian concepts appear in the text despite gnostic names; absence of evidence is no evidence of absence
  • Lee Berman: Christ is not clearly in the Sun books; Gnostic references like Abraxas and Demiurge outweigh the few Jesus-figures
  • David Stockhoff: Christ is plainly absent; Urth is an earlier iteration in a cycle, and Dionysus is a strong allusion read too literally by others

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Gods, angels and the Increate

2010-12-16 Lee Berman #

Argues that the Outsider is one creator god behind Allah and Dionysus, but that Jesus is not clearly in the Sun books, so placing Christ in the story is an unjustified leap. Responds to Gerry Quinn by citing the text's explicit Gnostic references like Abraxas and Demiurge, contrasting them with the single mention of Allah and the few Jesus-figures.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: the Outsider is the creator behind all religions; Christ is absent from the Sun books

So if the Outsider is our God, the creator of all universes, then Wolfe is saying that worshippers of Allah and Dionysus are all worshipping the same God that Catholics worship. Just differently named. No quarrel from me, but there might be from others.

Well, first you need to distinguish Jesus from Christ. With two universes there is an argument that there can be one without the other.

Allah is mentioned once. Jesus-figures a couple times. Dionysus as Wine God..and other gnostic references like "Abraxas" and "Demiurge" are mentioned a lot more and a lot more explicitly than those other two in the Sun series. Methinks your elevation of Jesus above the other two derives not from the text but from your society/culture.

Given your society/culture it may be difficult for you to realize that you are making the great interpretational leap by assuming Christ is in the story even though neither he nor salvation is mentioned. Others who do not insert Christ into the Sun series are being conservative, parsimonious and more true to the text.

2010-12-16 David Stockhoff replying to Gerry Quinn #

Disputes Gerry Quinn's claim that Jesus is present in The Book of the New Sun, arguing instead that Christ is plainly absent from the text. Proposes that Urth belongs to an earlier cycle of iterations, so Severian's world is a far-future past in which signs of Christ's future coming appear, citing the cycle idea and the light from Apollo's face.
disputes · theology-and-religion · myth-and-structure · theories: Urth is in a previous iteration of the cycle; Christ will come in a future iteration

I thought you wanted to argue from the text. You're arguing from Gerry's gut now.

Christ is plainly NOT in the story. And this makes sense if we accept, as we have been told, that Urth is in a previous iteration, and Severian lives in the far future of that iteration (from our perspective).

Christ will come in a future iteration, and these are all signs of his coming, just as the light from Apollo's face presaged it in our own part of the cycle.

2010-12-16 Gerry Quinn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Outsider as creator God is worshipped in Allah and Dionysus, and that Jesus and Christian concepts appear in the text despite gnostic names. Answers a quoted claim by citing a lion-and-lamb Eucharist, the Conciliator's Christian analogues and Wolfe calling Severian a Christian figure, and holds that absence of evidence is no evidence of absence.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: The Outsider is the god worshipped in all faiths; Jesus is present in the text; The Conciliator has obvious Christian analogues; Unnamed concepts are fundamental and named ones misleading

From: "Lee Berman" <[email]>

Insofar as they are worshipping the god of gods, yes. I don't think this is especially theologically controversial, certainly not in the case of Allah.

I don't see any grounds in the text for such a distinction. We do see evidence for Jesus in the text, and we also see evidence for an aspect of the Outsider that matches the teachings of Jesus.

We have a number of clear Jesus references - culminating in a sort of Eucharist in which the lion lays down with the lamb. Gene Wolfe certainly likes to throw in gnostic names and concepts (and names and concepts from many other sources) - but Christian concepts are everywhere too, even if not always mentioned by name. The whole Conciliator concept, for example - this has obvious Christian analogues.

[One could even formulate a hypothesis that it is the unnamed concepts that are fundamental; the named ones always misleading or incomplete.]

Wolfe has himself called Severian "a Christian figure". But it was Jesus I said was in the story, which quite clearly *is* the case. That is beside the point; it is always a leap to assume that absence of evidence constitutes evidence of absence.

But certainly, let us assess these ideas in terms of what the text says.

- Gerry Quinn

What you seem to be saying here is that this iteration features a Jesus who 
is not Christ, although he acts and spoke as Christ did.  And since we have 
heard of Allah, presumably there is there is a Mohammed, and he has pointed 
to the Increate. Mohammed may have been functionally identical to that of 
our iteration, just like the Apollo astronauts.  In fact most things seem to 
have been identical.  The Increate is present, and rules over all 
iterations.

The fundamental difference is that Jesus was not Christ, and therefore the 
inhabitants of this universe, stuck with a dud Jesus, must make do with 
Dionysus, who somehow prefigures Christ better than other deities - is that 
a valid summary of what you are saying?

Here are some reasons that spring to mind for doubting it:

(1) Why should there not be an Incarnation in more than one iteration of the 
universe?

(2) Who is Jesus in this universe - and what happens him in ours?

(3) What's the big deal about Dionysus?  He has a few symbols in common with 
Christ, but not much else. He doesn't really seem to be any kind of 
proto-Christ.

(4) It's a SF seriese, not the foundational text of a new religion.

- Gerry Quinn
