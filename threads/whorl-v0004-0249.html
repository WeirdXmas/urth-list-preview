Vodalus sez Urth flipt?

Whorl list · 2 messages · 1997-03-17 to 1997-03-18

1997whorlThe Book of the New SunMichael Andre-DriussiJames Jordan

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

1997-03-17 James Jordan #

Admits he cannot locate the passage where Vodalus says the Urth flipped on its axis, recalling only a possible alzabo-feast scene with Thecla. Argues that Smith's influence on Wolfe, shown in Fifth Head's focus on humanity and in sacramental themes, comes from shared religious beliefs, and calls the Norstrilia-Dune parallels coincidence.
answers · source-and-allusion · theories: Smith influenced Wolfe through shared religious beliefs

Aw, man! I don't know! Gimmeabreak!
I've read the Urth novels thrice, but I did not take notes. I dimly recall it from the alzabo feast when Severian eats Thecla and brings her back to life inside of himself -- but that might not be the place. Maybe someone else can remember. Or maybe I'm offbase.

There's a bit more. Fifth Head explores what it means to be human, and that is a large part of Smith's work also. I started with Fifth Head, and that is why I thought there was a good deal of Smith influence. Moving to the Severian books, I saw the same kind of sacramental/symbolic concerns that also run through Smith. Also, both have a real interest in psychoanalysis (as in the stories in Island of Dr Death &c.). These, to me, were more striking than the things you mention. At any rate, the coincidences seem to arise from similar religious beliefs, and some similar interests (religion, sacrament, political theory, war, human psychology). Curiously, there are lots of coincidences between Smith's Norstrilia and Herbert's Dune, but both were written at the same time, so they are just coincidences!

Nutria

1997-03-18 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Asks Nutria (aka JBJordan) where in the text Vodalus says Urth has flipped on its axis, and then compares Wolfe with Cordwainer Smith. Lists five shared traits, including fairy-tale style over an SF core, beastmen borrowed from Wells, and pagan elements used to explore religion, citing Smith's axis mundi as like Severian's vision on Yesod.
questions · source-and-allusion · theology-and-religion · theories: Wolfe's beastmen derive from Wells and Frankenstein; Smith's axis mundi resembles Severian's Yesod vision

Reply: Item #7558985 from [email]@INET02#

Nutria (aka JBJordan),

I'm curious as to the text wherein Vodalus said that the Urth has flipped on its axis--where would that be located?

Re: Cordwainer Smith and Wolfe, well sure, the similarities come down mainly to the coincidences of: 1) a fairy-tale style over a science fictional core; 2) some beastmen appropriated from H.G. Wells (who in turn took them from animal fables via FRANKENSTEIN); 3) a sense of Big History, lifted from Stapledon, or Toynbee, et al.; 4) a sense of Religion as a positive force; 5) a use of pagan elements to explore the mysteries of Religion (for example: the axis mundi part of Smith's QUEST OF THREE WORLDS is remarkably similar to what Severian sees on Yesod).

Which I guess is a round about way of saying that there is more than one way to make banana nut bread, but certain key elements will distinguish said loaf from zuccini bread or pound cake.

=mantis=
