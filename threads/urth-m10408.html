Sham War

Urth list · 10 messages · 2010-08-08 to 2010-08-09

2010urthThe Book of the New SunLee BermanDave LeblingPaul BJames WynnJeff Wilson
The thread: Lee Berman opened by arguing the Ascian war in The Book of the New Sun is a sham staged by one power to test Severian, citing Inire, the Cumaean and Vodalus's chess remarks. Paul B. countered with Inire's letter in Citadel urging the Hierogrammates to side with "our side," and Dave Lebling argued the war is a real struggle comparable to the Korean War, with Erebus-side hedging explaining the ambiguous remarks. Berman answered that Inire's "cousins" need not be Hierogrammates, and James Wynn argued the Hierogrammates could end the war if they chose, so it must serve their aims. The thread drifted into whether Wolfe, a Korean War veteran and Catholic, saw the war as God's purpose, with Lebling citing theodicy and Long Sun and noting the Hierogrammates are not God; it ended without agreement.
  • Lee Berman: the Ascian war is a sham staged by one power to test Severian, later that it is significant to combatants but cosmically insignificant
  • Paul B.: Inire's letter contradicts the sham reading and he asks why Inire would lie so bluntly
  • Dave Lebling: the war is a real struggle like Korea, and Wolfe as a Catholic treats God's plan as the book's central theodicy
  • James Wynn: the Hierogrammates could have ended the war, so it serves their purposes
  • Berman (later): Wolfe was troubled by the Cold War stalemate and the book shows war serving a higher divine purpose

Themes: War and soldiers, Gods, angels and the Increate, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

2010-08-08 Lee Berman #

Argues that the war with Ascia in The Book of the New Sun is a sham, with a single power playing both sides like a chess game to test Severian. Grounds this on Father Inire, the old Autarch, and the Cumaean playing both sides, the old Autarch's offer to let Severian command both armies, and Vodalus and Tzadkiel's chess remarks.
proposes · puzzle-solving · myth-and-structure · theories: The Ascian war is a sham staged by one power playing both sides

Not sure if I've posted on it in the past. I know some others have alluded to similar ideas.

The reason for thinking it, in small part, is because Father Inire, the old Autarch and even the Cumaean describe how they are playing for both sides of the conflict.

But the larger part comes from the comment the old Autarch makes to Severian before he hands over the reins of power. He asks if Severian would prefer to command the Ascian army. Or both armies. I take this, if not a sincere offer, as a truly possible suggestion of something that could happen. A way of hinting to Severian that not all is as it seems.

Both Vodalus and Tzadkiel make reference to Severian's life being like a chess game, Vodalus discussing the dark and light sides, Tzadkiel mentioning the limitations of pawns. One of the Pelerines suggests to severian that much more is going on than a battle between good and evil.

My interpretation is that the on- and off-screen powers are playing a chess game here. Perhaps the same power is playing both sides. The outcome doesn't matter. The purpose of the game is to test the players under game conditions...one player in particular. I see a similar situation in regard to the conflicts on Tzadkiel's ship and Yesod.

2010-08-08 Dave Lebling replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Ascian war in The Book of the New Sun is not a sham but part of a larger struggle, citing the Korean War analogy. Responds to Lee Berman's claim by explaining the Cumaean's and the old Autarch's remarks as Erebus-side hedging, and lists parallels such as multinational forces and a distant backer.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: The Ascian war is an analog of the Korean War; The Ascian war is a sham

I'm not all that certain which specific remarks you are referring to, but the Cumaean (during the scene in the stone town) talks about how service to Fr. Inire is a way of keeping a finger in both pies, as it were. The old Autarch talks about how Erebus would have traded him Ascia for the Commonweath, if it included Severian. I think his comment about "you could have both" means, "if you joined Erebus's side."

I don't see a lot that says the war is a sham, just that it's part of a larger war. Given that the war with Ascia is an analog of the Korean War, that's hardly surprising. Many details are similar. "We've driven them back half as far as they drove us south" (paraphrased). The Ascians are backed by a larger Power that really doesn't care about them. The war is barely noticed day-to-day at home in the Commonwealth. The Commonwealth forces are multi-national. Etc. I'm sure none of these details are new to you, but they all hammer home the Ascian War/Korean War analogy, in which there is a larger struggle outside that war, but that war is in itself significant, not a sham.

-- Dave Lebling

Argues that Wolfe's Ascian war in The Book of the New Sun is significant to those fighting it but insignificant from a cosmic or divine viewpoint, paralleling the Korean War. Responds to Lee Berman's quotation of Dave Lebling, reasoning from Wolfe's apparent religious outlook and the way all wartime sacrifice is eventually forgotten over the centuries.
disputes · author-and-biography · theology-and-religion · theories: Ascian war is insignificant from a divine viewpoint; Ascian war parallels the Korean War

I certainly agree with the autobiographical nature of the Korean/Ascian war. And I certainly agree that Wolfe thinks wars are very significant to the people who are fighting in them. Perhaps Wolfe is suggesting they are not so significant to the greater powers which put the war into motion and even less significant to the higher power above that.

Regardless of Mr. Wolfe's personal political leanings, I have a difficult time thinking (based on his writing) that he feels God and Jesus wanted the USA and democracy to win and N. Korea and communism to lose and that this was God's significant battlefield where He would vanquish His enemies, etc.

Commonwealth/Ascia war can be seen as significant when everyone who won the war, lost the war, all their families and friends and fellow citizens and comrades would all be dead and submerged within a few decades. A long, bitterly fought war with so much resources spent, so many dead, so many wounded and...for what?

I can imagine Gene Wolfe reading The Bible and, at the end, thinking the same about the Korean War.

2010-08-09 James Wynn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Hierogrammates could have decided the outcome of the war in The Book of the New Sun and The Urth of the New Sun if they had wished, so the conflict must serve their purposes. Responds to Lee Berman's claim that Wolfe presents the Korean War as a battle God wanted won, citing the Hierogrammates' time travel, mirrors and power over death and matter as grounds.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Hierogrammates allow the Ascian war to continue for their own purposes

I'm not so sure he would be so cosmopolitan about the Korean War as this implies. Seeing what North Korea has become, knowing what the Soviet Union was like and what happened 25 years later under Mao, I'm not so sure _I_ feel this way and I have far less skin invested in that war than Mr. Wolfe.

However, the one word that describes the meta-plot of the BotNS and UotNS is "uber-determined". There is no doubt in my mind that if Hierogrammates wanted the Commonwealth to liberate Ascia--or if they had wanted to undermine their government--they could have done so. They had Time travel, mirrors, and other near-divine technology at their disposal. They had conquered death and matter. The war continued only because it was a minor annoyance to them (at least), or it served their purpose in some way.

2010-08-09 Dave Lebling replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Gene Wolfe, as a well-read Catholic, would not be troubled by questions of God's plan, since the Book of the New Sun is effectively five volumes on theodicy and divine purpose. Responds to Lee Berman's question about who God wanted to win the Korean and Cold Wars by citing The Book of the Long Sun and noting that the hieros, hierogrammates and Tzadziel are not God.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Tzadziel is not God; The Book of the New Sun is about theodicy

Gene Wolfe seems well-acquainted with his religion, and therefore would have no difficulty answering the question. He answers it himself in TBotLS. There are many events in life and history which would trouble a believer, and I doubt that he is unsophisticated enough to be overcome by them, although he may ruminate now and then on the question of God's Plan, or on theodicy. TBotNS is, looked at the right way, five volumes on those topics.

In any case, it is quite clear that the hieros, hierogrammates and even Tzadziel are not God, although they fill some of the roles God fills in our universe. The question "who would Tzadziel want to win the Ascian war?" is not at all equivalent to "who would God want to win the Korean War?"

-- Dave Lebling, aka vizcacha
