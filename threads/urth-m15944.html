The thread: Posters debated whether Typhon's resurrection and the Short Sun's biology and healing powers are scientifically plausible, and whether Severian or Typhon's own account explains the revival. Lee Berman, Jeff Wilson and David Stockhoff were most active, arguing over whether non-carbon life, time-reversal physics, or supernatural power explain the events, while others argued Typhon's origin and the Flood-versus-Christ reading of the Solar Cycle. The thread ended in drift into jokes about the boots and the Pope, with the central disputes left unresolved.
  • Lee Berman: Short Sun biology and healing are natural advanced science, not supernatural, and shared biochemistry explains the creatures
  • Jeff Wilson: the Short Sun's time-bending and Increate-given powers exceed ordinary biology, so resurrections are believable within the books
  • David Stockhoff: Wolfe avoids hard-SF biology, so the books cannot reliably be judged natural or supernatural
  • Roy C. Lackey: Typhon was revived before Little Sev died and Severian's healing is divine rather than biological
  • Gerry Quinn: Typhon's origin matters little and he was probably not born on Urth
  • Daniel Petersen: the Solar Cycle is organized around Christology and the Flood versus Jesus question

Themes: Death and resurrection, Time loops and causality, Robots, androids and artificial life

2010-09-26 Lee Berman #

Argues that Typhon in The Book of the Short Sun is not plausibly of nonhuman origin, pointing to the biological feats shown in Typhon's and Tzadkiel's revivals and the many man-beasts elsewhere in the Whorl. Answers Jane Delawney's point about transplants staying within the human species by claiming that a gut feeling against an alien Typhon is enough to reject the idea.
disputes · theology-and-religion · theories: Typhon is not of alien origin

In Typhon we see a dried out, thousand year old human body, cells destroyed proteins degraded, somehow able to come to life, rehydrate, re-establish neural connections, organ integration etc. all within hours. In Tzadkiel we see the cells of a finger manage to re-organize themselves into heart, lung, brain, liver and bone cells and become a fully organized and integrated person within seconds. We have man-apes and dogmen and lord knows what else going on in the oceans and Baldanders labs and the witches tower.

All these fantastic biological processes are casually accepted yet a slight protein readjustment in the histamines of a human-appearing alien by the best doctors of an interplanetary empire to suppress organ rejection is deemed impossible?

I have to guess that Typhon-as-alien-in-origin just doesn't feel right to some people. In my view, such a feeling is sufficent to reject it as a working concept. No need for post hoc biological or other sorts of objections, especially those from outside the text. It works for my view of BotNS but it isn't meant as a foreign contaminant or monkey wrench in the working interpretations of others.

2010-09-26 Jeff Wilson replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the biological implausibility of Typhon's resurrection and Tzadkiel's transformations is no worse than the time-bending powers and alien origins that make other resurrections and changes believable in Wolfe's books. Responds to Lee Berman's list of implausible biological events by citing Briah and Yesod time mechanics, and rejects a space-vampire reading of Typhon as retroactive continuity from later books.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Typhon's resurrection explained by Severian's time-bending powers; Typhon is a space vampire by retroactive continuity

Typhon's resurrection by Severian is not reasonably believable as a biological process, and I certainly did not casually accept it as one when I first read the books, or the third time. But it is just as believable as the result of Severian's time-bending superpowers as any other resurrection in the books.

Tzadkiel's transmogrifications are likewise accompanied by an alien origin and a sublime command of time and space - it looks like seconds, but but it could be equally exempt from the plodding pace and irreversability of Briah's temporal arrow, as is spelled out for other processes in Yesod.

The man-apes and dog-men are just as believable as any other genetic engineering writ large. Baldanders's gadgets and self-modifications are pretty outre, but there is the requisite appeal to the authority of science for suspension of disbelief in those matters.

A nonhuman origin for Typhon or anyone else who appears basically human however, is without precedent or suggestion of possibility in the first five volumes, at least remotely close to the Urthly ages. If Typhon's "really" a space vampire, it's by retroactive continuity from the later books.

2010-09-27 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to David Stockhoff #

Posts a Wikipedia excerpt describing hemocyanins, copper-containing respiratory proteins that turn blue when oxygenated and are found in molluscs and horseshoe crabs. Responds to David Stockhoff's remark that copper-based life seems to prove the rule by not existing here, supplying real-world biology on copper-based respiration without drawing an explicit conclusion about the Short Sun books.
informs

Hemocyanins (also spelled haemocyanins) are respiratory proteins in the form of metalloproteins containing two copper atoms that reversibly bind a single oxygen molecule (O2). Oxygenation causes a color change between the colorless Cu(I) deoxygenated form and the blue Cu(II) oxygenated form. Hemocyanins carry oxygen in the hemolymph of most molluscs, and some arthropods, including the horseshoe crab, Limulus polyphemus. They are second only to hemoglobin in biological popularity of use in oxygen transport.[1] Unlike the hemoglobin in red blood cells found in vertebrates, hemocyanins are not bound to blood cells but are instead suspended directly in the hemolymph.

(from Wikipedia)

2010-09-27 Lee Berman replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the creatures of the Sun books share biochemistry and can eat and absorb one another, through either human dispersal and mutation or a parasitic gene-sharing race. Frames The Book of the New Sun as an exploration of pagan gods against Judeo-Christianity, set in a world where the Flood and Jesus never happened.
proposes · myth-and-structure · theology-and-religion · theories: Creatures of the Sun books share biochemistry through gene-sharing; BotNS explores pagan gods against Judeo-Christianity

Yep. And The Mother and Seawrack are down there eating drowned human sailors and, I think, Neighbor sailors before that. That's the picture I get from the Sun series- a galaxy of creatures able to eat each other and share their physical and mental being and existence.

Is this because Urthly humans spread out across the galaxy and mutated into a myriad of creatures? Or is it because a parasitic race feeding on multiple species serve as a vector for genetic sharing and splicing, like a virus for bacteria cells or even oncogenes in humans. Perhaps some combination of these processes?

I think the suggestions for this stuff are clear to see in the story. Identifying exactly which process created each superhuman character is probably not so important. Only the possibilities matter, which is all I've ever argued for.

For me, the underpinnings of BotNS are as an exploration of the interaction between pagan gods and Judeo-Christianity. Wolfe did this by creating an Earth twin where The Flood and Jesus never happened, allowing the pagan gods to have continued dominion far into the future of where we on Earth are now.

I'm glad Wolfe gave us various possibilities for the origin of such creatures as Erebus, Zeus, Typhon, Gabriel, Odin, Arioch, Abaia, Tzadkiel, etc. But at the end of the day, for the purpose of this story, I think it is more important to understand what they are rather than where they came from.

2010-09-27 David Stockhoff replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe chose to skip biological complications in the Short Sun and related books, since he is not a hard-SF writer and avoids non-carbon life or non-iron blood. Responds to Lee Berman's post on inhumi and alzabo feeding on humans and Neighbors, and on the cross-species gene sharing behind creatures like the Mother and Seawrack.
disputes · genre-and-craft

Another possibility is that Wolfe simply chose to ignore those complications. As an engineer, he is sometimes very technical---see the remarkably elegant explanation presented in this list for the Long Sun. But he is not really a hard-SF kind of guy, not when details might get in the way, and maybe especially in terms of biology. And he's never proposed non-carbon life or non-iron blood in any story, as far as I know. Even if he considered one of those possibilities (we do know gene transfer is possible), he wouldn't put them out front. I suppose the inhumi do make it impossible to rule out any theory like that.

Hemocyanins (also spelled haemocyanins) are respiratory proteins in the form of metalloproteins containing two copper atoms that reversibly bind a single oxygen molecule (O2). Oxygenation causes a color change between the colorless Cu(I) deoxygenated form and the blue Cu(II) oxygenated form. Hemocyanins carry oxygen in the hemolymph of most molluscs, and some arthropods, including the horseshoe crab, Limulus polyphemus. They are second only to hemoglobin in biological popularity of use in oxygen transport.[1] Unlike the hemoglobin in red blood cells found in vertebrates, hemocyanins are not bound to blood cells but are instead suspended directly in the hemolymph.
