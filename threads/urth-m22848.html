The thread: The thread asks whether Wolfe's Lupiverse iterations must repeat the Christ figure and whether later Urths can improve on the Incarnation, drawing on the La Befana note. Andrew Mason argues Wolfe treats multiple Christs on other planets as a joke, while entonio holds large works harmonize with their author's beliefs without being allegorical. Gerry Quinn disputes the logic of a single Incarnation across improving universes and cites the Urth cosmology passage, while António Pedro Marques argues iterations need not be linearly comparable. Jeff Wilson and entonio then drift into debate about whether humanity's far future is bleak and whether people or planetary histories can be judged, with no clear resolution and no Wolfe text cited in the final exchanges.
  • Andrew Mason: Wolfe rejects Christ reborn on other planets, though he may accept it for universes
  • entonio: Large works almost always harmonize with the author's beliefs, but iterations need not improve on one another
  • Gerry Quinn: A single Incarnation across improving universes is logically problematic; the Urth cosmology text says each iteration reaches the same stage
  • António Pedro Marques: Iterations are not linearly comparable since our world has only just reached globalised religious knowledge
  • Jeff Wilson: Humanity's far-future survival is less likely than now, and moral judgment on likely conduct shows humanity is not young
  • entonio: Planetary histories cannot be judged while unfinished, and age speeds up rather than running linearly

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Deep time and ruins, Death and resurrection

2012-03-12 Andrew Mason #

Argues that Wolfe rejects the idea that Christ must be reborn on other planets, treating it as a joke in 'La Befana', though he may accept it for universes. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes by noting C.S. Lewis accepts the principle for universes but not planets, and says an orthodox author could reasonably hold either view.
disputes · theology-and-religion · author-and-biography · theories: Wolfe rejects Christ reborn on other planets as a joke; Wolfe may accept Christ reborn across universes

In any case, Wolfe fairly clearly rejects this view in the author's note to 'La Befana' - while the story itself does turn on the idea that Christ must be born anew on different planets, it's made clear that this is a joke. On the other hand, the way he puts it suggests that he _may_ actually think the principle is true for universes.

C.S. Lewis, who accepts it - or at least is willing to run with it as a fantasy postulate - for universes, definitely rejects it for planets. But while I agree with you about the actual theology, I don't think this position is so _obviously_ right that an author who seeks to be orthodox could not think otherwise.

2012-03-13 Gerry Quinn replying to Andrew Mason #

Disputes the idea from La Befana that Christ must be reborn in each iterated universe, arguing that a single Incarnation across improving universes is logically problematic. Asks what Wolfe's author's note actually says and rejects interpreting an SF saga through religious dogma, noting the failed Jesus of earlier iterations and the puzzle of later ones.
disputes · theology-and-religion · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Christ must be born anew in each universe

From: Andrew Mason

What does he say, exactly? I read ?La Befana? online but it had no author?s intro. [My thought was that the mistake of La Befana might be to be looking among the human colonists rather than among Zozz?s people.] Aside from the issue of looking in dogma -either of one?s own religion or the dogmas assumed to be asserted by the author ? for interpretations of an SF saga, which I believe to be a questionable approach to literary analysis, the idea of a single Incarnation in iterated, slightly improving universes seems rather problematic on logical grounds. The ?Failed Jesus? of every previous iteration is just one problem. What about iterations *subsequent* to the one with the Incarnation? Either that is the last iteration (which is nowhere suggested) or the subsequent iterations are better than that one... and yet they also must have some sort of Failed Jesus or Post Jesus rather than the one true Jesus Christ. How can that be? Also, if ours is the final, perfect iteration... well, it seems like it ought to be a little bit better, doesn?t it? Really, the universe of Urth despite various SF horrors doesn?t seem especially terrible compared to our imaginings of this one, or even our history. - Gerry Quinn
