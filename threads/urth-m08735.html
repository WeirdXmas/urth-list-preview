Seawrack's Name

Urth list · 13 messages · 2009-03-14 to 2009-03-17

2009urthThe Book of the Short SunJordon FlatoAdam ThorntonDavid StockhoffJames WynnScott WowraSon of WitzMatthew GrovesStuart Hammb sharpJohn Smith
The thread: Posters debated the real name behind Seawrack, the Long Sun character, with proposals including Sycorax, Sirenia, Shipwreck, an Irish silkie or seaweed origin, East Asian myth, and Syrinx. John Smith and b sharp floated early candidates, and David Stockhoff and Scott Wowra argued for Irish and Greek sources respectively. Jordon Flato and Stuart Hamm argued the name is a non-human word from an alien tongue, and James Wynn later argued that Syrinx is a transcription of an alien word pronounced roughly 'Surigx'. The thread shows no consensus and ends with the disagreement over whether Wolfe's names are human-derived or alien.
  • John Smith: Sycorax from The Tempest, admits it is a stretch
  • b sharp: Sirenia or Shipwreck, reasoning from hair colour and the New Sun water creatures
  • David Stockhoff: Irish silkie origin, Wolfe follows his own rules
  • Jordon Flato: an unfamiliar non-human word, as she passes from non-human world to humanity
  • Scott Wowra: Syrinx, Wolfe always leaves clues in names
  • Stuart Hamm: an unpronounceable word from a totally alien language

Themes: Names and their meanings, Robots, androids and artificial life, Myth and folklore sources

2009-03-14 b sharp #

Proposes two candidate real names for Seawrack in the Short Sun books, Sirenia and Shipwreck, reasoning from her green-tinged hair, the undines and manatees of the New Sun, and the silent w. Responds to R. Borski's earlier archive post suggesting an Irish seaweed name, which the poster finds unsatisfying.
proposes · source-and-allusion · puzzle-solving · theories: Seawrack's real name is Sirenia; Seawrack's real name is Shipwreck; Seawrack's name hints at her siren function

I was reading the Short Sun and thinking that we are invited to figure out two things when we are first introduced to Seawrack. One is her real name which is supposed to sound somthing like Seawrack. I did an archive search and could only find
a 2000 post on the topic, from R. Borski, who (typically?) went overboard with name analysis I think and came up with an Irish seaweed called "screadhbhuidhe". This just isn't a satisfying answer for me. Not sure if my attempts are much better but, FWIW:

One guess I had is Sirenia. It came to me because Seawrack is described as having some green algae tint in her hair and this reminded me of the undines from BotNS. The undines were several times confused with manatees and Sirenia is the scientific order name for manatees. The Mother seems to invoke an Abaia-like presence. Of course this ties in with the siren and mermaid allusions which seem to tie all these characters together. Sirenia is an animal name anyway and perhaps this is partly why it was incomprehensible to Horn for a girl's name. Perhaps his mind changed it to the plant name Seawrack, as a name he could comprehend for her.

My other guess is "Shipwreck". It would qualify as wordplay meant to go directly between author and reader, somewhat by-passing the characters. I wondered if Wolfe might be hinting this her real name (or more likely her job title or duty or something) since it accurately describes her function as a Siren. She does seem embarassed when Horn first tries to prounounce her name. Perhaps because of what she was supposed to do to Horn's ship? Horn couldn't see and match the silent w found in both words but we can. Moreover, shipwreck and seawrack also do sound quite alike.

-bsharp

2009-03-15 James Wynn replying to John Smith #

Speculates about the real name of Seawrack, a character in the Long Sun books, suggesting it might derive from East Asian mythology given her tilted eyes and high cheekbones. Weighs this against her gold hair and pale skin, and proposes she may be built from the corpse of a pirate woman Horn killed, while noting the golden-green colour recurs.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Seawrack's real name derives from Japanese, Chinese or Mongolian mythology; Seawrack is built from the pirate woman Horn killed

I've always presumed her actual name would be from the mythology of Japan, China, or Mongolia, due to her features: "sharp chin, very high cheekbones, and tilted eyes".

But then her hair is gold, her skin is fairest white, and her eyes are "midnight-blue". On the OTHER other hand, if she were built from the corpse of the pirate-woman Horn killed a couple days previously, it is no surprise she is so pale, but that would not explain her golden-green hair. (but there's that golden-green color again.

J

2009-03-16 David Stockhoff replying to b sharp #

Suggests that the name Seawrack may come from the Irish silkie, a shape-changing seal-or-woman fairy, while conceding it is far from the original meaning. Builds on b sharp's idea that Horn rejected Sirenia and chose Seawrack, arguing that Wolfe always follows the rules he invents.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Seawrack's name derives from the Irish silkie

There is also the silkie, an Irish shape-changing fairy who is either seal or woman (I think).

It's nothing like seawrack, but then people often end up far from the original when they are forced to pick a native word to stand for a foreign one. (Technically a "calque," I believe, although that's usually a place name, like Leghorn.)

But I like bsharp's suggestion that Horn balked at Sirenia and went with Seawrack. Wolfe always follows the rules he invents.

2009-03-16 Jordon Flato replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that Seawrack's name is an unfamiliar, non-human word rather than a human one, since she passes from the mother's non-human world into humanity. Grounds this in Horn's fluency with complicated names in his tale and in the parallel with the false mother the Mother creates to speak to Horn, both of which approximate humanity.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Seawrack's name is an alien non-human word

Hmmmm. I can't really get on board any of that. Although I can understand wanting to figure it out, as rarely is something laid before us like that without some purposful reasonance.

I assumed the word was in a language completely unfamiliar, and non-human. Seawrack being a bridge of sorts between to worlds, that of the mother and that of humanity. She is being passed from the mother, the non-human world, where she had a non-human name, into the world of humanity, where she has to have a human name.

Why would she have a name of the language of men? I can't imagine Horn having a hard time saying any of the words people have said here. He 'pronounces' an awfuly lot of complicated nomenclature throughout his tale, so Serineia or other others don't strike me as holding any special difficulty.

An alien word though, in a tongue not related to the world of man, that would be tough. That also has resonance for me. Mirrors the false mother the Mother creates to speak to Horn. It has to approximate humanity to interact with Horn, and Horn has to 'approximate' Seawracks name to relate to Seawrack.

2009-03-16 Scott Wowra replying to b sharp #

Proposes that the name Seawrack is drawn from Syrinx, the nymph who escaped Pan by turning into reeds, citing the matching sound, Horn hearing Seawrack sing, and the nymph's seductive quality. Argues Wolfe always leaves clues in his names, so Seawrack should not be wholly alien, and answers b sharp's objection that the name is too human-sounding for Horn.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Seawrack is derived from Syrinx

My 2 cents:

Seawrack = Syrinx

Syrinx was a Naiad nympth who escaped a horny Pan through a transformation into reeds, which he cut off to make his pipes.

Names sound the same, the story connects to music as Horn is always hearing Seawrack singing, and Syrinx has the nymph quality to seduce males.

?
That seems like cheating to me, something Mr. Wolfe doesn't do. Mr. Wolfe always leaves clues in his names, and I don't think that Seawrack is based off of something totally alien.

2009-03-17 James Wynn replying to Stuart Hamm #

Argues that the name Syrinx, as used for a Short Sun character, is a literal transcription of a word whose pronunciation would be "Surigx", not a made-up alien name. Supports this by comparing it to the goddess Sphigx and by appealing to Wolfe's usual method, responding to Stuart Hamm's claim that the name is unpronounceable in an alien language.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Syrinx name transcribes Sphigx-like word

For record, (although I like the choice of Syrinx on a lot of levels) a literal transcription an pronunciation of Syrinx's name would be "Surigx". Not all that different from the pronunciation of the name of the goddess Sphigx.

I doubt the name, whatever it is, was intended to some made-up alien name. Its just not Wolfe's way.

J
