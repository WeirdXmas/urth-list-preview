free will and piracy

Urth list · 4 messages · 2007-12-17 to 2007-12-18

2007urthThe Book of the New SunMarc AraminiDave Leblingpaul witcover
The thread: Marc Aramini argued that the elderly Father Chris never tries to deter his younger self from piracy and murder, so his refusal to curtail that free will carries the redemptive meaning of the faux confession, citing his tears and the Christmas homily on gold as talents. Paul Witcover disputed this, reading the old man's tears as relief at returning to Novia rather than empathy, and calling Chris a cog. Aramini replied that later Wolfe characters lack the sinister edge of earlier figures and that Ignacio is coldly culpable, citing specific episodes. Dave Lebling agreed the tears mark returning to Novia, but argued the fixed monastic life is a prison rather than freedom, contrasting Severian's shaped life. The thread ended in divergent readings rather than agreement.

Themes: Free will and choice, Suffering and sacrifice, Justice, torture and mercy

2007-12-17 Marc Aramini #

Argues that Father Chris's older self never tries to deter his younger self from a path toward murder and piracy, so that the older man's refusal to curtail that free will is central to the redemptive meaning of Chris's faux confession. Grounds this in the older Chris weeping as the novitiate leaves, and in the Christmas homily about gold as talents given by God.
proposes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Older Chris's refusal to curtail younger self's freedom is central to redemption

On an unrelated note, I do think that any discussion
of the moral ambiguity inherent in Father Chris must
confront the older version breaking down in tears when
the young novitiate leaves and falls into the past -
an older version that never once tries to deter the
younger man from the path that will lead to murder,
debauchery, and other piratical pass times.

There is the Christmas homily of Chris that speaks of
gold - the talents given by God and the manner in
which we use them. It is unclear to me whether the
younger or older versions of Chris are using those
talents, or "gold", in the manner most stereotypically
considered as good, and I think somehow the older
versions refusal to curtail the free will, or the
"pirate freedom" of his younger self is somehow
central to any discussion of the redemptive meaning or
catharsis of Chris' psuedo confession.

Certainly free will and the existence of evil must be
discussed when the text's tangential relationship to
Catholicism is considered.

Good discussions on this repetitive cycle and faux
confession of Fr. Chris so far in the list.

2007-12-18 paul witcover replying to Marc Aramini #

Offers a reading of why the elderly Ignacio weeps as his younger self leaves, arguing the tears come from relief at finally returning to Novia rather than empathy for the young man's coming ordeal. Responds to Marc Aramini's point that the older version never tries to deter the younger from a path toward murder and piracy, using the scene where the novitiate departs.
disputes · close-textual-reading · author-and-biography · theories: Ignacio weeps from relief at returning to Novia

Marc Aramini <[email]> wrote: On an unrelated note, I do think that any discussion of the moral ambiguity inherent in Father Chris must
confront the older version breaking down in tears when
the young novitiate leaves and falls into the past -
an older version that never once tries to deter the
younger man from the path that will lead to murder,
debauchery, and other piratical pass times.

###

The question is, why does Ignacio cry at this point? My reading was that his tears were not provoked by any sense of empathy for what his younger self is about to experience, but rather the overwhelming emotional release of finally reaching the moment for which he has labored his entire adult life. He's crying because he is finally going back to Novia. Chris is nothing to Ignacio beyond a necessary cog in the mechanism that will take him back and allow him to reunite with her.

2007-12-18 Marc Aramini replying to paul witcover #

Argues that Wolfe's later characters, Chris, Ignacio, Silk and Able, lack the sinister quality of earlier figures in Fifth Head and Peace, and that Ignacio is coldly culpable for his own past. Grounds this in specific episodes: Chris's piracy, Silk killing the inhuma, Ignacio buying chickens and letting his younger self steal them. Replies to Paul Witcover's remark about Ignacio's tears and Chris as a mere cog.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Ignacio is cold and complicit in his worst moments

In the last decade and a half I have noticed an
increasingly sentimental nature in Wolfe's characters,
an absence of the sinister silences we see in 5HOC and
Peace and New Sun or his shorter work that makes us
say, jeez, this guy is creepy. Chris might be an
ineffectual apologist for the "environment" of the
brutal past forcing his hand, but if he elides facts
it just doesn't seem overtly sinister, despite his
embraced piracy.

The worst thing Silk ever did was right at the end of
the Sun cycle, when he kicked the inhuma to death.
Able was at worst a bully and at best a hero. Ignacio
is one cold dude if he could contribute to his own
worst moments while still doing things like buying
chickens and letting his younger self steal them.

I need to read it again, I guess. As always.

2007-12-18 Dave Lebling replying to paul witcover #

Argues that Ignacio, the older Chris of Pirate Freedom, builds a fixed life for his younger self, so the monastic path is a prison rather than freedom, and suggests his tears come from finally returning to Novia. Grounds this in the contrast with Severian's shaped life and the cloned narrator of The Fifth Head of Cerberus, and answers Paul Witcover's cog-in-a-machine reading.
disputes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology · theories: Ignacio's plan makes Chris's monastic life a prison; Ignacio cries because he will return to Novia; another older Chris pulls the strings

Ignacio as an older Chris, laboring diligently to create exactly the Chris he was as a young man, makes an interesting and creepy contrast to the life of Severian, molded by the hierodules, possibly more than once, to be a better man (or perhaps a worse - he is a mass murderer, after all) than the rough torturer he started out as.

Chris is a child of privilege (in a Mafia family, of course) whose life, looked at from his own selfish point of view, works out nearly perfectly until he loses Novia. His older self works for years to recreate his younger self, so that the younger self will not deviate from the life he previously led, thereby recreating the older self that creates the younger self.

Contrast this with the narrator of 5HoC, who is trying to recreate himself in a cloned body, and has been working on this project for generations with debatable success. Clearly Ignacio/Chris is aware that his project is risky and easily knocked off course (for example, he worries about the repercussions that might ensue if he visited himself when Chris still lived in "Jersey").

The perfectly choreographed life Chris leads from the time he enters the monastery is not "pirate freedom," it is a prison he puts himself into and can only break out of if his plan succeeds (or as Paul Witcover wrote, a machine in which Chris is just a cog). So, "pirate freedom" isn't freedom at all. Maybe that's why Ignacio is crying: after all that work, he thinks he is finally going to be free. Of course, there may be another still older Chris/Ignacio tugging the puppet strings.

-- Dave Lebling, aka vizcacha
