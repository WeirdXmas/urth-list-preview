The thread: Readers discussed whether Wolfe's characters are best when archaic and flat, prompted by remarks about Julian Jaynes's claim on Homeric introspection. Alice Turner agreed a general argument could hold and argued Wolfe excels with flat archaic figures but is weaker at nuanced modern characters. Kevin J. Maroney disputed Jaynes's categorical claim with a single Odyssey counterexample and said Alden Weir's characters are seen at a distance in his best stories. Alex David Groce defended Severian, Latro, and Weer as working by a memory-based, sacramental psychology rather than modern inner agony, and read Silk as a Moses figure. The thread ends with the disagreement over modern characters unresolved, as the posts did not converge.
  • Alice Turner: Wolfe succeeds with flat archaic figures and is weaker at nuanced modern characters; Jaynes's general claim could be sustained
  • Kevin J. Maroney: Jaynes's categorical claim is refuted by one Odyssey counterexample; Weir's characters are distant in his best stories
  • Alex David Groce: Severian, Latro, and Weer follow a memory-based sacramental psychology; Silk is a Moses figure compared to Myshkin and Aloysha

Themes: Memory and forgetting, Beasts and the animal in us, Myth and folklore sources

1998-11-03 Alice Turner #

Agrees that a general argument about Julian Jaynes's claim on Homeric introspection could be sustained, while noting Samuel Butler's translation and his belief that The Odyssey was written by a woman. Restates the poster's view that Wolfe succeeds best with flat archaic figures and is weaker at nuanced modern characters, responding to alga's quoted remarks on Jaynes and the Iliad.

Oh, I agree, and could easily find other examples, even from Gilgamesh. (Why are you reading Butler, btw, did you download it? There are some splendid more modern translations. Did you know that Butler, and Graves after him, was convinced that The Odyssey was written by a woman?) 1998-11-03 Kevin J. Maroney #

Argues that Jaynes's categorical claim about the mind is undone by a single counterexample, citing the Odyssey scene he first met in Auden's translation. Responds to alga's point about Jaynes, and adds that Alden Weir's characters are seen at a distance in his best stories, though Weir himself is vividly drawn.
disputes

The Butler was available online. I actually can't remember what translation I was reading when I first encountered this scene; I read it in WH Auden's _The Greek Classics_ (Viking Portable Library).

I certainly understand; but Jaynes was making an absolute categorical statement, and like any absolute categorical statement, a single counterexample damages it. (Harold Bloom seems to make a similarly broad and indefensible statement in his newest book on Shakespeare.)

Interesting observation, and one that I simultaneously find quite correct and want to argue with. Alden Weir is a brilliant 3-dimensional figure, but other than that, the characters in his best stories do tend to be viewed at a remove.

1998-11-03 Alex David Groce #

Argues that Wolfe's best characters, Severian, Latro, and Weer, work by a different psychology than modern fiction, with memory and a sacramental view replacing inner agonizing. Compares Silk to Myshkin and Aloysha, and suggests Silk is a Moses figure whose loyalty to Hy keeps him from the promised land, responding to Wombat's claim that Wolfe's modern characters are flat.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Silk is a Moses figure denied the promised land; Silk's loyalty to Hy prevents his arrival

Perhaps a better way to put it would be that a lot of Wolfe's best characters have a very different psychology than you generally find in modern literature--Severian comes to mind, of course, but Latro fits the bill as well. I don't think Severian is so much "flat" as extended in a different manner--where a modern character has depth, Severian does indeed seem flat. However, Severian's memory and ability to tune in to what I guess I would call a sacramental view of reality replaces this; he does not agonize over decisions because he is doing something else. Latro works the same way (his lack of memory serving the same kind of function as Sev's abundance thereof).
Weer is, to my mind, the most complex of Wolfe's characters--with both the psychological depth of a "modern man" and the labyrinthine memory of a Severian (Weer's being, presumably, less "true" but equally involved.) I'd say Weer's closest parallel is the narrator in Remembrance of Things Past--completely different kinds of books, with very different concerns, but a similar unique kind of character.
Of course, I'd say that it's more that Wolfe emphasizes certain kinds of psychology that aren't prevalent in modern literature (or recognized by us moderns in general, although still extant) than that he can't handle the kinds usually seen--I think Weer's proof of that.
Silk I see as more "modern" than Severian, but also akin to Myshkin or (what I thought of when I started the book) Aloysha in The Brothers Karamazov... An Aloysha, a Father Brown, a "little Christ", and a bit of Moses (so is Auk Aaron?). Hey, that's actually a nice point. Moses never got to the promised land either! The book is "Exodus," after all. Of course, Blue & Green don't seem to be the land of milk and honey, but then the Israelites were being given the one nation in the region without oil supplies. So, if Silk is Moses-like, what's his disobedience that prevents arrival in the promised land? Or is this an intentional contrast, where it's his loyalty (to Hy) that prevents it, making him both Moses-like and Christic in self-sacrifice for (a less than Godlike but still touching) love?
