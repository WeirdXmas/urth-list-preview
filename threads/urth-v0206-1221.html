Christians and Christs: A new RPG?

Urth list · 4 messages · 2002-06-12 to 2002-06-17

2002urthThe Book of the New SunJeff WilsonDan'l Danehy-OakesJames WynnJames Jordan
The thread: Participants debated whether Severian is a Christian rather than a Christ, and whether the Urth setting holds Christianity in a meaningful way. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes invoked the Catholic idea of the anonymous Christian, saying it offers a fair chance rather than universal salvation. James Jordan argued Christianity appears only indirectly through Byzantine terms, the Pelerines, and Severian's prayer, making him a weak believer, and called Briah a passing idea except for the flood. Jeff Wilson questioned how the flood fits the Noah promise and whether it was local or caused by something other than the White Fountain. James Wynn proposed the Ushas deluge mythologically echoes Noah's flood, presenting God's beneficial intent, and argued Wolfe's books are myth rather than speculative science fiction. The thread ends with the positions stated rather than resolved.
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Severian fits the anonymous Christian idea, offering a fair chance at salvation without promising universal salvation
  • James Jordan: Christianity is present only indirectly in Urth, so Severian is a weak believer rather than an anonymous Christian
  • Jeff Wilson: Asks how the flood squares with the Noah promise and whether it was local or caused by something else
  • James Wynn: The Ushas deluge mythologically echoes Noah's flood and shows God's beneficial intent; the books are myth, not science fiction

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Gods, angels and the Increate

2002-06-12 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Responds to a quoted claim that Severian is a Christian rather than a Christ by invoking the Catholic idea of the anonymous Christian, which holds that people without explicit faith can still be offered salvation. The post separates this from syncretic universalism, saying it does not promise everyone is saved, only that God gives everyone a fair chance. A joke about the hit points of a Jesus closes it.
disputes · theology-and-religion

Crush declaimed:

Well, here you fall afair of the common Catholic theological concept of the "anonymous Christian" -- the person who, for whatever reason, is not given the grace to know and believe in Christ in this life, but believes in what the closest conception available to him/her. This is a hair, but a critical hair, separater from syncretic universalism: it doesn't say that everyone will be saved; rather, it says that God plays fair and that everyone has the opportunity to be saved.

--Blattid

2002-06-14 James Jordan #

Argues that the Urth world contains Christianity present only indirectly, pointing to Byzantine theological terms, the Pelerines' faithful but confused nuns, and Severian's prayer at the stellar winds, so Severian is a weak believer rather than an anonymous Christian. Responds to a post by Crush about Severian being a Christian rather than a Christ, and adds that Briah as a prior universe may be a passing idea that matters little to interpretation except for the flood.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian is an undisciplined, weak Christian believer; Urth world contains Christianity seen only indirectly; Briah is a pre-Noah universe allowing a flood

I'm inclined to think that there is Christianity in the Urth world, but that we don't see it except very indirectly. Wolfe has left it in the background, but hinted at it with his Byzantine setting, the Byzantine theological terminology (Increate, Pancreator, Theoanthropos), the confused but still somewhat faithful nuns of the Pelerines (sp?) with their saint-relic shrine, etc. Severian utters a prayer as he commits his first book to the stellar winds. So I don't think we're supposed to think of Sev as an "anonymous Christian" but as a person living in a confused and compromised Christian world (as all Christian worlds are, because of sin, and Byzantium certainly was) and as having a background that makes him a very "undisciplined" Christian of some sort. He's a believer, but a weak one. He shares the common faith of his society.
BTW, when Wolfe told me that he was thinking of Briah as a previous universe, I took it at face value. Maybe all this means is that this is what he was thinking when he started out. He was "playing" with the notion of a cyclical universe. I certainly don't have anything invested in it, and the 11 or 12 volume super-novel is in a lot of ways simpler if we assume it is "our" universe. I don't think it matters much, for I have not been able to see that it is some kind of major interpretive clue -- except that it does allow him to have a flood over the earth, which in our universe would be problematic because of the promise to Noah. That aspect of the narrative is "cleaner" if this a pre-Noah universe. FWIW.

Nutria

Responds to James Jordan's quoted remark about Wolfe's Briah being a previous universe and disputes the transcript's clarity about what Jordan originally asked Wolfe.
questions · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Flood was local rather than global

The transcript does not render your question clearly at all; what exactly =did= you ask him, if you can recall at 11 years' remove?

Is it possible to dodge the promise some other way? Was Urth ever abandoned by humanity? Could the flood be local in nature, or due to some other cause, perhaps Abaia? The flood lasts far too long to be due to the White Fountain alone.

2002-06-17 James Wynn #

Proposes that the Deluge of Ushas in Wolfe's Long Sun and Short Sun books may mythologically echo or prefigure Noah's flood, reading Wolfe's flood as showing God's beneficial intent rather than a historical event. Grounds this in comparisons with Tolkien's refusal to build Middle-earth's religion and C. S. Lewis's Space Trilogy links, and argues Wolfe's books are mythological rather than speculative science fiction.
proposes · theology-and-religion · myth-and-structure · theories: Ushas Deluge mythologically echoes Noah's flood; Wolfe's flood shows God's beneficial punishment; Long Sun is mythological not speculative sci-fi

The thought occurs to me that the Deluge of Ushas *could* be the Deluge of Noah - that the Story of Noah could be the a mythological telling of the Ushas Deluge...OR the Noah story could be a PROPHECY of the Ushas Deluge!!!

...or (as I'm inclined to believe) Wolfe was toying with the idea of the ultimate beneficial intent of even G-d's most punitive actions - and took up the icon of the world-wide flood to show it (I'm pretty certain that Wolfe does not believe in a historical Deluge).

Seen this last way, Wolfe's NS, LS, and SS books stories are "mythological" sci-fi rather than "speculative" sci-fi. That is, one cannot expect to draw a line from the Messiah's Earth to the Conciliator's Urth. You can do it technologically, linguistically, geographically, and in literature. But Biblically? No. That's just too much to ask even from Wolfe. Even Tolkien balked at attempting to create such a world - insisting that there is NO religion in Middle Earth (except the false worship of Sauron by the Swarthy Men).

C. S. Lewis DID draw such a connection in both his Space Trilogy and the Narnia series. But when he attempted to add a tenuous connection (in the space trilogy) to Middle-Earth, Tolkien deeply resented it.

If Wolfe IS bringing into the Long Sun and the Short Sun books the actual G-d he worships at Mass, then I'll bet a dollar he does not see Silk's world and history as any more potentially "actual" than Tolkien saw Middle-Earth when he included in it the origin of "Hey-diddle-diddle".

I'm not sure I'm making myself clear. Sorry about that.

-- Crush
