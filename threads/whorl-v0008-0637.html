1998-05-16 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Responds to a Lexicon Urthus-style draft by reviewing several entries, arguing that "A card in a cart" in Orchid's offer to Silk is a fragment of a whorlish saying about a free sexual favour. Corrects the volume numbers in the "Pure keg" citations and glosses both senses from the Long Sun books, citing Auk and Scylla's and Bustard's dialogue.
disputes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: "A card in a cart" is a fragment of a whorlish saying

Brain,

Yay! Stuff looks =great=! "Scrape out" and "Sweatin' ken," excellent!

Re: "Card in a cart," this is used in regard to Orchid's proposal for Silk to have recreational sex "on the house" (er, free of charge):

"We're closed Hieraxday, so not then either. But come in any other day and I'll give you a pass. How's that? . . . You know what I mean, right, Patera? Not me [whom you will dally with]. I mean with any of the girls, whoever you want. If you'd like to give her a little something for herself, that's all right. But you don't have to, and there won't be anything [paid] to the house." Orchid considered again. "Well, a card in a cart, huh? All right, that's a lay [i.e., a shag <g>] a month for a year."

So maybe all she is saying is that the total value of the gift is one card? "A card in a cart" being a fragment of some whorlish phrase like "A card in a cart is worth two in a bush" (if you know what I mean and I think you do, nudge-nudge, wink-wink)?

Re: Dimberdamber nanny nipper. Okay! "Adroit coat-cutter thief" looks very good, especially as a coat-cutter thief must be pretty adroit to begin with.

Re: "Pure keg." Agg! The volume numbers are switched! The two citations should be (III, 115.7) and (II, 213.3).

"All right, she [Scylla] said it [about looting the wealthy quarter] and I believed it, and now I [Auk] got to let her know I'm the pure keg, too" (II, 213.3).

In this case the sense seems to be "one who acknowleges the favor of another with a favor in kind."

Bustard speaking in Auk's mind: "Give me the pure keg, sprat. You want me to winnow you out? If I'm going to help, I got to know" (III, 115.7).

In this case the sense seems to be "the absolute truth."

Brain good!

=mantis=

1998-05-17 Peter Westlake #

Proposes that "pure keg" in The Book of the Long Sun means something faithful or true, suggesting it may derive from beer drawn straight from the keg, unwatered and unadulterated. Responds to mantis's correction of the volume citations and the Auk-Scylla exchange by weighing the meaning against the Brain dictionary entries for "keg".
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: "Pure keg" means faithful or true; "Pure keg" derives from unadulterated beer from the keg

...

Or just "faithful", i.e. "true": that would make it the
same sense as below.

Brain gave several meanings for "keg", but didn't list "barrel" among them. How about "pure keg" being derived from beer straight from the keg, unwatered and unadulterated?

Spectaced bear.
