Sightings at Twin Mounds

Urth list · 7 messages · 2014-05-27 to 2014-05-28

2014urthAntónio Pedro MarquesMarc AraminiDavid DuffyMo Holkar / UKG
The thread: The thread asked whether the narrator of "Sightings at Twin Mounds" committed murder and cannibalism, and whether a proposed wiki reading of the story is trustworthy. Marc Aramini argued the narrator may be a murderer, citing ambiguous phrases and bloodstains, while calling the cannibalism reading unproven without explicit textual support and treating Robakowski as half outside time, so his death is not a crime. Mo Holkar agreed the reading is internally consistent but found the evidence insufficient to require it. António Pedro Marques argued the narrator may be unreliable only in his own voice, so he could omit incriminating facts while objective description stays trustworthy, and pointed to the confession and the odd detail of seeking random men to eat. The debate stayed unresolved, with the sides disagreeing on how far the text supports the murder reading.
  • Marc Aramini: narrator may have murdered Robakowski, but cannibalism needs explicit textual support
  • Mo Holkar: proposed wiki reading is internally consistent but not required by the evidence
  • António Pedro Marques: narrator is unreliable only in his own voice, so he may omit incriminating facts while objective events stay trustworthy

Themes: Unreliable narration, Justice, torture and mercy, Beasts and the animal in us

2014-05-27 Marc Aramini replying to Mo Holkar #

Argues that unreliable narrators in Wolfe have legitimate narrative reasons for deceiving readers, not simply pathological lying, and doubts that a wiki explanation of Sightings at Twin Mounds can be trusted. Responds to Mo Holkar's note about a new wiki theory by citing examples from Short Sun, Latro, and VRT, and says the third-person stories give less motive than first-person ones.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft · theories: Sightings at Twin Mounds narrator is almost entirely untruthful and murderous

I will keep it in mind when I read it again but usually the unreliable narrators in Wolfe have legitimate narrative reasons beyond being pathological liars to be so - they are ignorant in short sun, amnesiac in latro, a parasitic child in VRT ( ie- the death scene of the abo boy victor Trenchard is not just a made up "lie")... This analysis indicates our narrator is almost entirely untruthful and murderous ...

Castleview and some of the other third person stories are Wolfe's hardest to feel confident of adding inferred analysis to because there is little narrative bias with motive besides the author's need to be cryptic. (This made Free Live Free an easy work to analyze compared to, say, 7 American nights with the possibility of hallucinogen AND forgery) If the narrator is a cannibal there should be some subtext that indicates his desires. Will look at it soon.

Cites the wendigo origin story as the only cannibalism reference, and finds the idea that a UFO-clipping collector would eat a random man a stretch.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Narrator of Sightings at Twin Mounds committed murder

Okay I read it through twice ... The ambiguous subtext is " I had a day to kill", "murder would appear to have taken place" and "no doubt I should not have done what I did" talking about entering the house and finding the blood stains. The only mention of cannibalism is the origin story of the wendigo.

Someone collecting newspaper clippings on ufos and deciding to eat a random guy still seems a little bit of a stretch.

2014-05-28 Marc Aramini replying to António Pedro Marques #

Argues that the murder of Robakowski in "Sightings at Twin Mounds" is not a crime, since he was half in and half out of time, and that his apartment is linked to the mound only through the narrator's testimony. Responds to António Pedro Marques's unreliable-narrator point, and rejects a cannibalism reading unless a text naming cannibalism or the narrator enacting what he reads can be shown.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Robakowski was half in and half out of time; Apartment connected to the mound only through narrator testimony; Story is not a cannibal story

Well, there isn't a crime if the Indian Tribe is "following him" after the contact ... the part that we have to trust the narrator on is that the Indian girl and Robakowski, when they flee the murderous tribe, somehow wind up back at his apartment embracing, as he sees through the windows. The journal from the psychiatric doctor indicates Robakowski/Roland (or whatever name he uses) returned from the past half in and half out of it ... so his apartment is connected to the mound primarily through the testimony of our narrator. Robakowski did experience being with the girl at one point, and our narrators observations show that this extended to his apartment.

So there is no crime if the Indians killed him because he was half in and half out of time (ie he never got away), and somehow the mound and its displacement is connected to his apartment.

For this to be a cannibal story, I would at least like a name from a text referencing cannibalism or some sign that our narrator tries to enact everything that he reads.

2014-05-28 António Pedro Marques replying to Marc Aramini #

Argues that the narrator of the Twin Mounds story may be unreliable only in his own voice, while objective description of events stays trustworthy, so he could legally omit incriminating facts about killing Robakowski. Responds to Mo Holkar's and Marc Aramini's remarks about the story's internal consistency by citing the narrator's murder confession and the odd detail of him seeking random men to eat.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Narrator killed Robakowski and omits incriminating facts

I had just reread the story the day before Mo posted this. Someone once said that 'Wolfe doesn't lie in direct discourse'. I think what was meant is that all the 'unreliability' stuff is due to most of the stories being told by a flesh-and-bone narrator, not the 'omniscient' kind that we've strangely become accustomed to (and whose qualities even the flesh and bone narrators of other writers seem to have acquired!), but there are still times when the voice isn't some narrator's, but an objective description of events. That's the one exception I see to the general unreliability, and as such I don't think there ought to be some special conditions to be met so that the narrator is able to lie or omit stuff. In fact, it could be said that if the narrator killed Robakowski then he's doing a fine account of events leaving out those that may incriminate him (as a defendant may legally do in many legal systems). Yes, it's strange that he'd be looking for random guys to eat. Though what I personally like the least is that the amount of story that needs to be introduced is so big. But at the end of the day, there's a crime to solve.
