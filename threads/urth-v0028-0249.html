Borges and Vance

Urth list · 8 messages · 1999-10-26 to 1999-10-28

1999urthThe Book of the New SunJim HenleyDave LeblingJames JordanMichael Straight
The thread: The thread asked whether Borges or Jack Vance was a stronger influence on Gene Wolfe. Jim Jordan and Jim Henley mostly offered admiring or joking remarks, with Henley's posts lacking textual argument. David Lebling proposed Borges's 'The Approach to al'Mutasim' and argued Vance was the central SF influence, contrasting Vance's amoral heroes with Wolfe's seekers. Michael Straight argued 'The Immortal' resembles Wolfe's cool narration, comparing Severian and Latro, and Henley disputed that Latro shares this tone, offering amnesia as the reason for Latro's understated reporting. Straight and Henley ended in a narrow disagreement over narrator style rather than a resolved conclusion.
  • Jim Jordan: Wolfe is unique and resists reduction to Borges, Chesterton and Lewis influences
  • David_Lebling: Borges's al'Mutasim and Jack Vance are the less obvious key influences
  • Michael Straight: Borges's The Immortal and Wolfe share a cool, distanced narrator style
  • Jim Henley: Severian fits the cool tone but Latro's casualness comes from amnesia
  • Alex David Groce: claimed Borges influenced Wolfe (cited, not posting in thread)

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Books, libraries and writing

1999-10-26 James Jordan #

Notes that Borges was a strong admirer of Chesterton, as Wolfe is, and adds C.S. Lewis as a religious influence who was himself shaped by Chesterton. Concludes that Wolfe is unique and cannot be reduced to these influences. Responds to a remark that all Wolfe fans are Borges fans, agreeing and extending the list of influences without offering textual evidence.

Ultimately, however, Wolfe is ... Wolfe. Sui generis.

1999-10-26 Dave Lebling #

Proposes Borges's story "The Approach to al'Mutasim" as a less obvious influence on Wolfe, citing its mystery-novel frame, messiah figure and revised edition that reveals all mysteries. Also argues Jack Vance is the central SF influence, contrasting Vance's amoral heroes with Wolfe's seekers after God and comparing Severian to a Vance hero.
proposes · source-and-allusion · genre-and-craft · theories: Borges's al'Mutasim is an influence on Wolfe; Severian behaves like a Vance hero

Alex David Groce writes of Borges' influence on Wolfe:
<<Borges is also always looming over Wolfe's work ... "Funes the Memorius" and "The Circular Ruins" are obvious ones.>>

I'd like to add, as a less obvious one, also from _Ficciones_, "The Approach to al'Mutasim." This is a story about a pulp genre (mystery) novel which eventually concerns the search for a messiah-like figure. It is a work with many levels, but one of them is a meditation on the importance of mystery (or perhaps the unattainability of truth) in fiction. One part of the story concerns a revised edition of the book in question, in which all the mysteries are revealed, producing a much inferior work. At another level it is about religion, and subtle hints, and many other bits and pieces (including a planetary, um, Indian adventure) which are quite lupine.

But then, all Wolfe fans are Borges fans already, right?

Also,
<<Jack Vance is the real center of science fiction's literary influence of Wolfe.>>

Couldn't agree more. However (as mantis has pointed out elsewhere), there is quite a contrast between Vance's picaresque, areligious, sometimes amoral heroes and Wolfe's tormented, sometimes unknowing seekers after God. To be honest, Sev's behavior is not unlike that of a Vance hero; he just agonizes over it more.

But then, all Wolfe fans are Vance fans already, right?

(A sad parallel between Borges and Vance is that Vance is now blind or nearly so.)

Dave Lebling
(aka vizcacha)

1999-10-27 Michael Straight #

Argues that Borges's story 'The Immortal' resembles Wolfe's style more than any other Borges story, since its narrator's offhand remarks reveal cruelty as routine practice. Responds to Alex David Groce's claim of Borges's influence on Wolfe by pointing to the cool, distanced narrator and comparing it to Severian and Latro.
supports · source-and-allusion · genre-and-craft · theories: Borges's 'The Immortal' is a model for Wolfe's narrators

I'd also like to add "The Immortal," a story which reminds me more of Wolfe's style than any other. For instance, the narrator gives offhanded comments from which one can infer his cruelty, not because he's trying to hide anything, but because torturing slaves for information or crucifying seditious soldiers is such standard operating procedure that it hardly bears mentioning.

Also the cool, distanced tone of the narrator reminds me of one of Wolfe's narrators (Severian, Latro).

-Rostrum

1999-10-27 Michael Straight #

Argues that Severian and Latro share a habit of reporting extraordinary events in a casual manner, often skipping the details a reader would find most striking. Responds to Jim Henley's comparison of narrators, which he partly disputes by noting Latro's tone is not cool or distanced.
disputes · close-textual-reading · genre-and-craft

What I'm thinking of is that both narrators have a tendency to report extraordinary events in an almost casual way sometimes neglecting to go into detail about what we would consider the most interesting or unusual aspect of events.

-Rostrum

1999-10-27 Jim Henley #

Argues that Latro's casual reports of extraordinary events, such as meeting the river god, read as ordinary because he has no baseline for what is unusual. Responds to Michael Straight's comparison of Borges and Vance narrators by offering a reason for their understated reporting, drawing on Latro's amnesia-driven lack of context.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Latro's amnesia explains his casual tone toward marvels

Ah, but so much of the time, Latro has no standard by which to tell the events are unusual, is the impression I get. He describes meeting, say, the river god casually because for all he knows this sort of thing happens all the time.

1999-10-28 Michael Straight #

Clarifies that the earlier complaint about cool, distanced narration concerns the narrative style of Latro rather than his personality, and compares it to Severian's perfect memory as the source of a similar effect. Responds to Jim Henley's quoted point that Latro reports extraordinary events, such as meeting the river god, casually because he has no standard of strangeness.
supports · genre-and-craft

Well sure, I didn't mean to malign Latro and say that he personally was cool and distanced, it's his narrative style I was talking about.

You could make a similar argument that it's Severian's perfect memory rather than his actual temperment that creates that effect in his narrative.

-Rostrum
