The thread: Craig Brewer asked a fellow poster to put a question to Wolfe about whether magic really happens in The Sorcerer's House or whether the epistolary narrative is an elaborate con. Lee Berman then argued that Wolfe subverts science fiction by explaining scientific worlds through religion, seeking harmony between science and faith and comparing Wolfe to Milton and the author-as-demiurge idea. David Stockhoff disputed this, arguing Wolfe subverts SF more by bringing fantasy into it, noting that many fantasy writers were openly Christian, and offering a qualified counter-suggestion. The thread ends with the disagreement unresolved and no clear consensus recorded.
  • Craig Brewer: asks whether magic really happens in The Sorcerer's House or the epistolary narrative is a con
  • Lee Berman: Wolfe subverts SF by explaining science through religion, seeking harmony between science and faith
  • David Stockhoff: Wolfe subverts SF by bringing in fantasy, not by explaining science away with religion

Themes: Unreliable narration, Catholic faith and sacraments, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

2012-03-15 Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe's fiction subverts science fiction by explaining scientific worlds through religion, while seeking a harmony between science and faith. Responds to Craig Brewer's point that an author cannot control how readers take a work, drawing on the author-as-demiurge idea and comparing Wolfe to Milton's Paradise Lost. The post grounds this in SF's history of using science to explain away religion and in the Dr. Crane figure.
proposes · author-and-biography · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe uses religion to subvert science fiction

Heh, yep. Reminds me of David and my discussions on author as demiurge. You can create

everything in a world but you can't control the free will of the residents/audience.

Hm, I think WOlfe's work resembles this. SF is a genre which has been a historic breeding

ground for atheistic thought. Good portions of it have been devoted to explaining away

religious beliefs and God's creation using science. Wolfe's work might be called profoundly

SF (he has certain collected enough SF awards) but I think he uses his work to secretly

subvert SF by explaining it away with religion.

That overstates it of course. Wolfe seems to want science and religion to coexist in some

sort of simultaneous harmony. But in a literary world populated by Dr. Cranes, that could be

seen as a form of subversion.

Thanks for this answer. It seems like a response which required some self-contemplation and

is a realistic (as opposed to idealistic) prediction of your reaction to my hypothetical

scenario.

2012-03-15 David Stockhoff replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe's fiction subverts science fiction less by explaining it away with religion than by bringing fantasy into it, noting that many fantasy writers were openly Christian. Responds to Lee Berman's quoted claim that SF has a history of atheistic thought and that Wolfe uses religion to explain away science, offering a qualified counter-suggestion instead of a flat rejection.
disputes · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe subverts SF by bringing in fantasy

Possibly. It's true that many of the Golden Age SF writers were Jewish. But look at how many fantasy writers were overtly Christian.

Perhaps there is more of an argument to be made that Wolfe subverts SF by bringing fantasy into it.
