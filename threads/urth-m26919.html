Wolfe & Women

Urth list · 24 messages · 2020-01-29 to 2020-01-31

The thread: The thread began with Gem Caccetta reading Barnes's remark in Free Live Free that he wants women but does not like them as a possible clue to Wolfe's own attitude toward women. Gerry Quinn and Ab de Vos read it as general misanthropy or a sour-grapes fable, while David Stockhoff argued authors do not simply adopt their characters' views. Frederick Norwood defended Wolfe's love for his wife, and Ryan Dunn cited a personal anecdote to deny misogyny. The discussion then drifted into political correctness and civility debates led by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Charles Gillingham, with no textual resolution. Norwood finally asked the list to drop the thread and return to Wolfe's fiction, and Eric Bourland offered a brief text-based observation about Crisoforo and wistful endings.
  • Gem Caccetta: Barnes's line may reflect Wolfe's own attitude toward women
  • Gerry Quinn: Barnes's remark is misanthropic rather than specifically misogynist
  • Ab de Vos: Barnes disparages what he cannot have, as in the Fox and the Grapes
  • David Stockhoff: Authors do not simply adopt characters' views; Wolfe may have studied the question
  • Frederick Norwood: Wolfe loved his wife, and PC complainers just want permission to be rude
  • Ryan Dunn: Wolfe was not misogynistic, citing his emotion over writing Cassie in An Evil Guest

Themes: Women, sex and gender, Unreliable narration, Love and loss

disputes

But sure if you want to be polite when someone tells you to come with them, go ahead be as nice as you want.

2020-01-30 David Stockhoff replying to Norwood, Frederick Hudson #

Argues that there is no reason to attribute a character's attitude toward women to Wolfe himself, since authors do not simply adopt their characters' views. Suggests instead that Wolfe may have found the question of whether he liked women or merely found them necessary interesting enough to use in building a character's interior life.
disputes · author-and-biography · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe's character attitudes are not Wolfe's own

I see no reason whatsoever to ascribe Barnes' attitude to Wolfe. That's not how authorship works. It's far more likely that the thought occasionally occurred to Wolfe as a question---do I actually like women, or do I merely find them necessary?---and he found it interesting enough to use for building a character's interiority.

2020-01-30 James Wynn replying to Norwood, Frederick Hudson #

Argues that the label "politically correct" is an ad hominem and that the PC culture of the late 1980s, with its callout and cancel offshoots, has not changed in substance. Responds to Frederick Hudson Norwood's claim that PC complainers just want to be rude, offering personal experience and advocating accurate, unemotional rebuttal instead.
disputes

Calling proponents of a position "politically correct" is itself a form of ad hominem. But if it is going to be *defended* as a category, then...

In my experience, unironic "political correctness" gained a very bad reputation quite early -- by the late 80s -- because the people advocating for it demonstrated a very low bar for the definition of rudeness when encountering ideas they disagreed with and a very permissive bar for the punishment that should be meted out for perceived rudeness. They also demonstrated marked blindness for rudeness perpetuated in the name of "political correctness" (sometimes euphemized "the common good"). Nothing about the culture of PC has changed since then, but what has changed is an increased ability of some of its advocates to sequester themselves from the critiques of it and the various subcultures it spawned (callout culture, cancel culture, social credit scores).

Rather than engaging inaccurate statements with ad hominem (see above), I think it is more effective to engage them with accurate speech untainted by personal emotion.

~ James

I also got sad when I reached the end of Evil Guest, and the end of Cassie?s voice, b/c I knew I would never hear that voice again. Wolfe knew it too. You could tell, reading the end of EG, he got emotional. Who wouldn?t?

On the Gene Wolfe page on Facebook, someone invited people to read Pirate Freedom, for the purpose of convening later to discuss. Later meant about four weeks or so in the future, and that was a couple of weeks ago I guess. Anyway a bunch of folks hopped on, and I grabbed PF and read it in a couple of nights and it was as wistful and lovely (and violent and brutal) as ever. It?s been noted that Crisoforo?s voice seems at times too boyish ? he is an educated Catholic priest, after all ! ? but his voice changes appropriately I think; he is a pirate boy in lost history; a troubled priest in the modern world. Different person / voice in different times. He is different when his Novia is near, and marauding with Capt Burt. Anyway my point, if I have one, and maybe I don?t, is, I think many times in his heart Wolfe wrote as a young person filled with wonder and a yearning for romantic adventure. And so we get wistful moments like the poignant endings of Evil Guest and Pirate Freedom. And Wizard Knight, and so on. Severian, even when old and care-worn, was still a boy in his heart. A boy with his dog, a little lost, slightly thoughtless, the way young people can be.

That?s more of a mere observation, and less of a point, but it was something I was thinking about as I scudded again through Pirate Freedom.
