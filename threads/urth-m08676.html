PEACE: the source story

Urth list · 9 messages · 2009-06-01 to 2009-06-10

2009urthThe Book of the New SunMatthew KingJosh GellerAdam ThorntonGwern BranwenChristopher Keiser
The thread: Christopher Keiser proposed that Ambrose Bierce's "An Inhabitant of Carcosa" is the source story for Wolfe's Peace, supporting a reading that Weer is dead and his ghost lives in the far future, and listed parallels to other Bierce works. Gwern Branwen disputed this, noting that The King in Yellow borrowed names from Bierce and that the ghostly narrator who learns he is dead is a generic device. Matthew King asked whether Silk and Horn map onto the two kinds of death in Bierce's opening paragraph. brunians argued the metaphysical ideas predated Bierce, though he may have read him, and later agreed Bierce could be a seed without offering textual evidence. Keiser conceded the ideas were not new but kept the seed claim; the thread ended without resolution, with the source question still open and little textual support offered.
  • Christopher Keiser: Bierce's Carcosa story is the source of Peace, implying Weer is a dead ghost, with claimed parallels and an unverified reread promise
  • Gwern Branwen: the ghost-realization device is generic and Bierce's influence is shown only through shared names from The King in Yellow
  • Matthew King: asks whether Silk and Horn map to the two kinds of death in Bierce's opening
  • brunians: metaphysical ideas predate Bierce so he need not be the source, though Wolfe may have read him and Bierce's combat writing may have influenced Peace

Themes: Ghosts, dreams and visions, Stories within stories, War and soldiers

2009-06-01 Christopher Keiser #

Proposes that Ambrose Bierce's story "An Inhabitant of Carcosa" is the source story for Peace, and that this supports the theory that Weer is dead and his ghost lives in the far future. Grounds the claim in Wolfe's interviews naming a root source story for his works, and in the poster's own rereading that revealed the allusion.
proposes · source-and-allusion · author-and-biography · theories: Carcosa is the source story for Peace; Weer is dead and his ghost is in the far future

Hello there?Urth.net, long time lurker, first time poster.

I am suffering, at the moment, a specific mental ailment, Eireneomania.

During my third consecutive read, an allusion that had been nibbling away in my brain finally chewed its way free.

"An Inhabitant of Carcosa", by Ambrose Bierce, is the the source story for _PEACE_.

http://www.ambrosebierce.org/carcosa.html

In interviews, Gene Wolfe always points to one or another story as the root source of his story, the seed of the idea.

Never would I say anything is 100% with Wolfe, but, I will say it now: this story is the seed of _PEACE_.

I believe this will also support the theory that Weer is dead, though I am not sure anyone at this point believed otherwise. My favorite interpretation of _PEACE_ places Weer's ghost in the far future, after the fall of our civilization. "An Inhabitant of Carcosa" would support that hypotheses.

I hope this is not a repeat of something previously posted to Urth. I did a search on Urth, and the Internet in general, and found nothing similar.

Have a read, any feedback would be appreciated. Please tell me I am not crazy.

Thanks,
Chris Keiser

2009-06-01 Christopher Keiser replying to Christopher Keiser #

Lists supposed parallels between Wolfe's PEACE and Bierce's An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge and The King in Yellow, including a dead narrator unaware of his death, the repeated 'all is gone' motif, and a wolves-at-Carcosa passage. Jokingly proposes Wolfe as his own grandfather who time-traveled to influence Bierce, and promises a reread for textual proof.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: PEACE draws on Bierce's Owl Creek Bridge; Hoseib Alar Robardin's narrator is dead; Wolf imagery in PEACE derives from Carcosa

gwern

That is certainly an honest request, considering Gene Wolfe.

1. Hoseib Alar Robardin finds his grave entangled in the roots of a tree.

2. The narrator does not know that he is dead.

3. Obviously, The inclusion of the _Morryster's Marvells of Science_ shows that Wolfe is familar with Bierce (of course, is there anything with which he isn't familiar?). The inclusion of the three mythos tomes, (Bierce, Lovecraft and Bloch), another substrata of the novel, displays more than a passing fancy regarding authors borrowing fictional books from each other, and thereby strengthening their 'reality'.

4. The motif "all are dead, all is gone, I am alone" is repeated many times in _PEACE_.

5. Savages now inhabit the world ... Iroquois inherit Cassionsville after Blaine dies

6. "I continued, 'I am ill and lost. Direct me, I beseech you, to Carcosa.' ... very similar to Weer's conversations with many characters.

7. And the smoking gun ...

"A chorus of howling wolves saluted the dawn. I saw them sitting on their haunches, singly and in groups, on the summits of irregular mounds and tumuli filling a half of my desert prospect and extending to the horizon. And then I knew that these were ruins of the ancient and famous city of Carcosa."

Gene Wolfe is obviously his own grandfather, and has traveled back in time, influencing Ambrose to include the image of Wolf in his short story, so that a young Wolf may appropriate it for his first novel. ;-)

Seriously, I think I will reread _PEACE_ again, find perhaps some textual proof.

Thanks,

2009-06-10 Matthew King replying to Christopher Keiser #

Asks whether Silk and Horn in Peace correspond to the two kinds of death described in the opening paragraph of Bierce's source story. Quotes the passage about a spirit dying with or before the body and being raised again, and offers the mapping Silk to the first kind and Horn to the second as a question.
questions · source-and-allusion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Silk and Horn match the two kinds of death

"In one kind of death the spirit also dieth, and this it hath been known to do while yet the body was in vigor for many years. Sometimes, as is veritably attested, it dieth with the body, but after a season is raised up again in that place where the body did decay."

Silk died the first kind, Horn the second?
