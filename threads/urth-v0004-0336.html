Themes: Love and loss

1997-10-10 Derek Bell #

Proposes that Holly Hollander's novel Pandora is an allegory about love, each family relationship and the Holly–Alladin Blue bond expressing a different kind of love. Supports this with the grandfather's jealousy, the father's resignation, and Alladin's broken leg as a fall from grace, while finding Holly unconvincing.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · close-textual-reading · theories: Pandora is an allegory about love

Warning, spoilers ahead!!

Last warning!

Disclaimer, this is from memory, so take it with a few
shovelfuls of sal if you like.

OK, my opinion is it is an allegory about love: consider the various relationships in the story: Holly's granparents; Holly's parents; Holly's mother and her lover; Holly and Alladin Blue. Each relationship is based on on different kinds of love, at least from the men's side. Holly's grandfather is jealous and possessive, her father loves her mother, though he is resigned about her being unfaithful and is even willing to take the blame for the bombing to prevent his wife being jailed. Holly's mother and her lover recognise in each other that they are similar: they are both very selfish people.

I have a problem describing Holly and Alladin's relationship; Alladin's character seems strongly outlined by the incident where his leg was broken - his motives are noble, though he, like anyone else, can only do so much before he was intimidated into breaking the law. (In religious terms, I supposed it was his "fall from grace".) Holly's character didn't convince me and I find it hard to describe her, other than she clearly loved her father, wanted to clear his name and discover the real bomber. Maybe it's just the amount of time that's elapsed since I read it.

Tercel

1997-10-10 Derek Bell #

Speculates that Wolfe's childhood polio, with its leg paralysis, explains his recurring religious symbolism, then jokingly asks whether he played electric guitar. Notes Chicago references in Holly Hollander, arguing that "Muddy Brooks" is a disguised Muddy Waters, citing Blue's house and Tercel's humming of "Mannish Boy" as support, in a reply to an earlier post that read the novel as a semi-allegorical juvenile.
proposes · author-and-biography · source-and-allusion · theories: Wolfe's polio explains his religious symbolism; Muddy Brooks is a disguised Muddy Waters

It struck me that Wolfe had infantile paralysis, otherwise known as poliomyelitis. I think one common symptom is paralysis of the legs; perhaps that's why Wolfe chooses this religious symbol so often - it relates to his own life.

^^^^^^^^^^^^ Say, did he play electric guitar? :-) This book's got loads of Chicago references! I wouldn't put it past Wolfe to deliberately choose a character to have many types of references/connotations: e.g. both religious and Chicago-related.

For instance, "Muddy Brooks" sounds like a thinly disguised variation of "Muddy Waters". And he lives in this house of Blue's!

Tercel, humming "Mannish Boy"
