Hyacinth and Seawrack

Urth list · 8 messages · 2010-10-21 to 2010-10-22

2010urthThe Book of the Long SunLee BermanAntónio Pedro MarquesJames WynnDave TallmanRoy C. Lackey
The thread: Lee Berman asked whether James Wynn had linked Hyacinth and Seawrack, and proposed that Horn's naming of Seawrack in the Ayuntamiento passage of Return to the Whorl is an unreliable-narrator slip caused by parallel lover pairs. Wynn confirmed the idea and said the Rajan confuses the two women, but gave no exact location for the passage. António Pedro Marques suspected an authorial error, then proposed that Silk's memories surfacing in Horn explain the mix-up. Dave Tallman read it as the narrator's split identity, and Berman agreed while wishing the point were developed further. Roy Lackey extended the confusion to Hyacinth and Nettle, and the thread ends with the reading of a deliberate narratorial slip as the main view.
  • Lee Berman: repeated Hyacinth/Seawrack confusion is an unreliable-narrator slip tied to parallel lover pairs
  • James Wynn: the Rajan confuses Hyacinth with Seawrack more than once
  • António Pedro Marques: possibly an authorial error, or Silk's memories surfacing in Horn
  • Dave Tallman: the mix-ups reflect the narrator's split identity, not a real identification
  • Roy C. Lackey: the narrator also confuses Hyacinth with Nettle

Themes: Unreliable narration, Doubles, twins and clones, Memory and forgetting

2010-10-21 Lee Berman #

Asks whether a James Wynn post once suggested a link between Hyacinth and Seawrack, and proposes that Horn's mention of Seawrack in Return to the Whorl is an unreliable-narrator error caused by parallel lover pairs (Horn/Seawrack, Pas/Kypris) bridged by Silk and Hyacinth. Grounds this in the passage where Horn describes the overthrow of the Ayuntamiento with Maytera Mint, Patera Silk and Seawrack, and asks whether an in-world connection exists.
questions · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Seawrack and Hyacinth confused through relationship parallels; Silk and Hyacinth bridge the lover pairs

I've been reading Return to the Whorl and had a question. I think it is for James Wynn. Wasn't it you who suggested some sort of connection or shared identity between Hyacinth and Seawrack? I tried looking/searching but couldn't find the post.

I thought of it because I ran across a section where Horn is describing to Vadsig how he and Maytera Mint and Patera Silk and Seawrack overthrew the Ayuntamiento.

If the inclusion of Seawrack was an auctorial error I'd find that pretty interesting, as I am more interested than most in the autobiographical aspects of Gene Wolfe's work.

More likely it is an example of unreliable narrator error I suppose. As such my first interpretation would be that the error occurs due to relationship parallels. Horn's paramour is Seawrack. Pas/Typhon's paramour is Kypris. Silk and Hyacinth, possessing aspects of the gods, provide the bridge between these two pairs, thus Seawrack and Hyacinth are confused.

Am I missing some some real world (within the story) way that Seawrack and Hyacinth can be connected in the way that Silk and Horn are connected?

Responds to Lee Berman's quoted remark about the Rajan's confusion and adds a grandmother's habit of calling her husband 'your father' unnoticed as a parallel.

2010-10-21 Dave Tallman replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Rajan's mix-ups between Hyacinth and Seawrack in In Green's Jungles reflect the narrator's split identity rather than a real identification of the two women. Supports this by quoting the passage where he hears Hyacinth singing and the one where he recalls lying with both women, and notes his calling a son Horn.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Rajan's confusions reflect the narrator being two men

I'm pretty sure that is not the only time the Rajan confuses Hy with Seawrack. I couldn't tell you exactly where. I thought there was a time where he talks about Hyacinth singing.

There is, in IGJ, p. 121. "Here I stopped to listen, for I heard Hyacinth singing to her waves."

I believe this is more about the Narrator's identity confusion than any real identification of Hyacinth with Seawrack. He exchanges one love for another because he is really two men. A similar mistake is when he addresses one of his sons as "Horn" in the last book.

The following quote shows how his memories of the women are doubled: IGJ p. 170: "I am going to pray and go to bed and (if I cannot sleep) daydream about the first time I lay with Seawrack on the clumsy little sloop I built with my own hands, and loved so much, and of lying with Hyacinth, too, in Ermine's on the night of our marriage."

2010-10-21 Lee Berman replying to Lee Berman #

Agrees that repeated confusion of Hyacinth and Seawrack is the simplest explanation for Horn mixing up the two names, but wishes Wolfe had developed the point further. Notes that Wolfe seems unusually heavy-handed in showing that Silk and Horn have merged, and suggests this name slip is part of that display.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk and Horn are merged in Horn's narrative

Yes, if the mistake is repeated then I suppose this is the most parsimonious answer. Would have been nice (for me anyway) if there was more to it. For some reason Wolfe is uncharacteristically heavy handed in his demonstration that Silk and Horn have been merged. This could just be a part of that.

2010-10-22 Roy C. Lackey replying to Dave Tallman #

Supports James Wynn's point that the narrator confuses his wives, Hyacinth and Nettle, and adds further instances. Cites a passage in On Blue's Waters where Silk tells Chandi he called Nettle Hyacinth, and notes the mention of Seawrack's singing on the same page.
supports · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk deliberately substitutes Hyacinth's name for Nettle's

I agree. IIRC, there are also other places where Silkhorn confuses one wife with the other, when he writes Hyacinth when he means Nettle. He also consciously substitutes Hyacinth's name for Nettle's at least once, when he spoke to Chandi: "As for me, I did not weep; but I was at least as homesick as she, and when she was calmer I told her about you, Nettle, calling you Hyacinth." (OBW, 222)

He also mentions Seawrack's singing on the same page.

-Roy
