Leagues and gravity

Urth list · 8 messages · 2002-03-11 to 2002-03-12

2002urthThe Book of the New SunThe Book of the Short SunMatthew MalthouseJerry FriedmanAndy RobertsonGrant PeacockDan'l Danehy-OakesRoy C. LackeyMichael Straight
The thread: Posters tested whether the orbital mechanics of Green and Blue in The Book of the Long Sun, and of Lune around Urth, support the Blue=Ushas theory. Jerry Friedman calculated that Green's gravity would destabilize the system and asked whether Wolfe's figures were a deliberate clue or science yielding to story. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued the 35,000-league figure is likely a mistake and that Wolfe rarely builds star-system mechanics into his plots. Grant Peacock raised the Whorl's low gravity and a tidal comparison with the Moon, and Roy C. Lackey cited the Urth passage to argue Lune never left orbit. Michael Straight and matthew.malthouse then argued that any consistent history of the Neighbours needs an orbital system stable over long spans, which no proposed solution provides. The thread ended in impasse, with most posters rejecting the mechanical case for Blue=Ushas.
  • Jerry Friedman: Green's orbit would be gravitationally unstable, so the figures are either a clue or a story-driven error
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: the 35,000-league figure is probably a mistake and Wolfe rarely plots star-system mechanics
  • Grant Peacock: the Whorl's low gravity may explain the sensations, and the fountain may have captured Lune
  • Roy C. Lackey: Lune did not leave orbit, since it rises brighter after the flood and behaves normally afterward
  • Michael Straight: any Blue=Ushas version leaves too long a gap for unstable orbits to survive
  • matthew.malthouse: any explanation needs a stable enough system for the Neighbours' long history, and none yet meets that

Themes: The dying sun and the cosmos, Deep time and ruins, Myth and folklore sources

2002-03-11 Jerry Friedman #

Works through the orbital mechanics of Green and Blue in The Book of the Long Sun, estimating Green's mass and closest approach in leagues and miles, and concludes the gravitational forces would destabilize the system. Asks whether Wolfe's figures are a deliberate clue or simply a case of science yielding to story, linking the question to the Blue/Ushas conjecture.
questions · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Green is a second planet, not a moon; Wolfe's astronomical figures are internally inconsistent

...

Where did you get 150,000 miles?

Anyway, you got me thinking about Green-Blue celestial mechanics. Stop me if this has all been done.

We're pretty sure Green is not a satellite but rather another planet with a slightly smaller orbit, so that it comes into conjunction every six years (if I remember correctly), right? Also, Green has higher gravity than the moon--otherwise the narrator would have told us at least once how the first thing he as Horn noticed was floating every time he tried to take a step. I can hardly imagine Green has less than half the gravity of Earth and the same density. Using those assumptions gives it 1/8 the Earth's mass. (Calculations available on request.)

Let's also believe Incanto quoting one Gagliardo when he says the closest approach of Green to Blue is 35,000 leagues. If these are the same leagues in the afterword to _The Sword of Lictor_, namely about three miles, then that's 105,000 miles. For comparison, somebody on the first visit to the Red Sun Whorl says a league is 7000 steps. If these are half the Roman pace of which the 5000-foot Roman mile was a thousand, then that's 7000*2.5 = 17,500 feet ~ 3.31 miles. Using that latter number, Green's closest approach is about 1.87 x 10^8 meters.

We can now determine that the maximum force that Green exerts on Blue at conjunction is about 8.5 x 10^21 N. For comparison, the force the sun exerts on the Earth is about 35.3 * 10^21 N. In other words, Green is going to mess up Blue's orbit something fierce--and much more if it has more than half the Earth's gravity. Mere storms and tides are nothing. Blue is going to mess up Green's orbit even more. I doubt the system would be stable for more than one or two conjunctions.

So did I make a mistake? Are we supposed to infer something from this discrepancy? Or is this another case of Wolfe's not letting science get in the way of the story? The problem with the latter is that if he can get away with anything, the kind of deductions he apparently expects readers to make become problematic--which is the difficulty we're having with the Blue/Ushas conjecture.

Jerry Friedman

2002-03-11 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that Wolfe's Short Sun colonists probably have primitive astronomy, so the 35,000-league closest approach of Green to Blue quoted from Gagliardo is likely a mistake, and that the Blue/Ushas theory fails despite its appeal. Responds to Jerry Friedman's gravity and distance calculations by noting Wolfe rarely builds star-system mechanics into his plots and the St Croix system is not germane to The Fifth Head of Cerberus.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · author-and-biography · theories: Blue is Ushas; Gagliardo's distance figure is an error

Jerry Friedman wrote, and I'm going to snip and cut mercilessly to avoid a 90% quoted posting...

Not, so far as I can tell, in your math.

Possibly. My immediate guess would be that Gagliardo was actually clueless about the actual distance between the two worlds at, uh, perigreen. (No falcon around about this.) It's probably safe to guess that the astronomy and astrophysics of the Short Sun colonists is pretty primitive.

This is probably heretical, given his engineering background and almost fanatical attention to detail, but ... I have never really thought of Wolfe as the kind of Clement-ish worldbuilder who sits down and calculates all the astrophysics of a system before he starts writing. In fact, I could be wrong, but I'm pretty sure that this is the first Lupine text where the actual mechanics of a star system really affected the plot in any significant way - certainly the mechanics of the St Croix system isn't very germane to the plot of _Fifth Head_!)

The problem we're having with the Blue/Ushas conjecture is that some people on this list, who shall go nameless, like the idea so much that they will hang on to it in the face of any amount of contradictory evidence. Heck, _I_ like this theory. It would resolve a number of irritating problems in the Sun books. But in fact it simply does not work for eighty-'leven reasons.

--Blattidio

2002-03-11 Grant Peacock #

Argues that the Whorl's gravity may suit people accustomed to less than one g, questioning the calculations behind the Green-Blue orbital argument, and asks whether the white fountain physically moves into the system. Responds to an earlier post on Green's gravity and orbit by offering the tidal comparison with the Moon and the Blue=Ushas idea that the fountain captured Lune.
questions · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Narrator is accustomed to less than one g; Blue=Ushas with fountain capturing Lune

Yeah I once posted some similar thoughts, although I didn't do any math.

Have you considered the possibility that the narrator is accustomed to gravity less than 1 g? I'm assuming the whorl was designed to prepare people for Blue. The fliers in the whorl are perhaps more realistic in a .5g environment.

Our moon exerts a stronger force on Earth than our Sun does. I say this because tides are only a bit more extreme during a full moon. (I forget the terminology. What are neap tides??) So I would guess there must be something wrong in your calculations.

I think you are surely right that the orbits are unstable, even if the force exerted by Green on Blue is a small percentage of that exerted on Earth by the moon. This means that the arrangement is a new one. For those who believe the Blue=Ushas theory, what must have happened was that the white fountain grabbed Lune out of orbit as it came lumbering in and made into a planet.

Does the white fountain literally move through space into the system, or does it just magically appear?

Argues against Grant Peacock's suggestion that the Whorl's sub-Earth gravity explains the narrator's sensations, reasoning that the narrator and Hoof would notice heavy local gravity. Supports the point with tidal-force arithmetic comparing the Sun, Moon and the Green-Blue system in The Book of the Long Sun setting.

Reasonable, though we can hang-glide on Earth and the Fliers' propulsion modules could easily be far in advance of our technology. Maybe you don't feel the local gravity in astral projection, but if so, they should notice that the Urth folk walk as if they were nailed to the ground (if nothing else).

Tidal force decreases as the cube of the distance, not the square, so the gravitational force of the sun on us is almost 200 times that of the moon, even though the tidal force of the moon is something like three or four times stronger than that of the sun.
