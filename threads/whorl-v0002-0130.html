1997-02-11 Dave Lebling #

Argues that Silk's readings of his enlightenment are self-serving, since he narrows the Outsider's charge to save the Whorl down to saving his manteion before later reinterpreting it. Cites Pike's Ghost, where Silk instantly assumes the black shape is Oreb, and asks who else saved the rest of the Whorl and how Auk's lander fits in.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Silk's explanations of his enlightenment are self-serving

mantis,

I'll have to do some more research if I'm to come up with a list. I think what I was referring to was more Silk's habit, whenever anything happens, of coming up with an immediate and facile explanation. Sometimes, as you say, he is corrected, or corrects himself, but sometimes it just goes on by without comment. One example with immediate correction is his experience that we've been calling "Pike's Ghost," where he immediately assumes the black shape is Oreb.

One case that hit me on rereading is Silk's interpretation of his enlightenment. It seemed clear to me from the wording and the content of his vision that his charge from the Outsider was to save the _Whorl_, but he almost obsessively whittles that down to a charge to save his manteion. Only much, much later does he, in an almost surprised way, decide that saving his manteion means saving the people of the quarter the manteion serves, which is of course equivalent to saving the _Whorl_. The explicit religious aspect of this is obvious; we are all charged by the Outsider with saving the world, and such saving is to be accomplished by saving ourselves and our fellow humans, and through them the world.

In fact, the more I think of it, the more I think that Silk's explanations are often self-serving in a sense; the same way peoples responses to religious challenges are often self-serving, and deflect them from what was really wanted.

Another question (so this won't all be philosophy and ethics): Who saves the rest of the _Whorl_? Horn's appendix makes it clear that people from cities other than Viron made it to Blue. Who got them there? Did the Outsider enlighten someone in each city? Auk's lander is the first to leave the _Whorl_. What's happening elsewhere? Why did Tartaros pick Auk of Viron, of all the Cargo? (That at least has a plausible explanation.)

1997-02-11 James Jordan #

Argues that Silk in The Book of the Long Sun is slowly freed from idolatry as he learns the Outsider is the only real God. Compares this with Severian's arc in the Quintet and, replying to a quoted reading that Silk's charge is to save his manteion, says that his deliverance from bondage is the Outsider's command.
proposes · theology-and-religion · theories: Outsider is the only real God; Silk's arc overcomes idolatry; False gods carry fragments of truth

In both the Severian Quintet and in the Long Sun Quartet Wolfe gives us a central character who is slowly working his way out of a bad beginning. In Silk's case, it is idolatry. Slowly he comes to understand that the Outsider is the only real God, and all the rest are false. After that, he comes to appreciate that even these false gods carry fragments of truth with them.
Idolatry is a major interest in many of Wolfe's works, but never more than here.
Silk's gradual understanding of the Outsider's command to him is part of that process of illumination and deliverance from bondage.

James B. Jordan
