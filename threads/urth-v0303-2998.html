2003-03-02 jismulkstis #

Recommends Iain M. Banks's Culture novels, naming Consider Phlebas, Use of Weapons, Excession, Inversions and Look to Windward, and praises their moral ambiguity, sense of wonder and detailed society. Notes stylistic differences from Wolfe and asks what other readers think of Banks, while doubting this forum suits the discussion.
proposes

I strongly recommend Iain M. Banks Culture novels:

Consider Phlebas
Use of Weapons
Excession
Inversions
Look to Windward

While stylistically unlike Gene Wolfe, Iain M. Banks writes novels about the moral ambiguity that comes with wielding vast power. He can also be very dry, sarcastic, and funny, but these are in no way humorous novels. The Culture is a detailed, well thought out society and his books are better at creating the old "sense of wonder" (especially Excession) than anything I've read in many years.

You have to like a world where the spaceships are far more intelligent than their passengers and give themselves names like "Honest Mistake" and "Ethics Gradient".

Those who are hooked on a more medieval setting should
try "Inversions".

I'd like to know what others think of Banks, but this
may not be the appropriate forum.

Muskrat

2003-03-03 Thomas Bitterman #

Argues that Iain M. Banks's Excession borrows from Wolfe's Long Sun books, since its hemispherical ship interior with a rotating bright line for day and night mirrors the Whorl. Offers the ship's design and its rotating light as the evidence, while noting that Banks's worldview is opposed to Wolfe's.
proposes · source-and-allusion · theories: Banks's Excession alludes to the Long Sun

[...]

He's not just stylistically unlike Wolfe, their worldviews are diametrically opposed. Banks and Wolfe probably shouldn't shake hands, lest some sort of literary explosion take place.

Obligatory Wolfe note: I'm convinced that Banks is making an allusion to Wolfe in _Excession_. (My copy is at home, so I'll just draw an outline.) The ship's interior is hemispherical, and there is a bright line that rotates around to make day and night. Banks describes it as a giant jump rope, with the ship at play forever. It's such a brilliant twist to the Long Sun it's hard to believe Banks didn't have it in mind.
