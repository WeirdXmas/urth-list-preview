The thread: The thread began as brief congratulations on The Book of the New Sun winning a Gollancz contest, then drifted into a long dispute about the contest list's lack of women writers. Posters including James Wynn, Jerry Friedman, Marc Aramini and Lane Haygood debated whether Atwood, Le Guin and other women authors are dated or current, and whether 1970s SF is didactic. The discussion moved on to politics, preachiness in fiction, and Marc Aramini's circus anecdote, which led to socialism and scripture debates. It ended in general drift with no sustained engagement with Wolfe's texts.
  • James Wynn: women authors are dated and absent from lists, and Wolfe's politics are radical libertarian
  • Jerry Friedman: Atwood and Le Guin remain current, and many admired pre-1970s SF authors were didactic
  • Marc Aramini: much 1970s SF is dated and female writers rely on tired conventions, with a far-right Catholic outlook
  • Daniel Petersen: prefers science fantasists like Lafferty and Wolfe, and rejects sorting fiction by gender
  • David Duffy: Wolfe's politics are unobtrusive, and allegory is enjoyed by suspending the urge to detect it
  • Jane Delawney: Ringworld and The Dispossessed deserved omission because of Niven's sexism and Le Guin's preachiness

Themes: Power, rulers and politics, Books, libraries and writing, Genre: science fiction, fantasy, both

2011-08-02 James Wynn replying to Lane Haygood #

Argues that few women authors would make a list of books that inspired young readers, since the leading female names he cites, Rowling, Meyer and Lackey, are absent, and that feminist-era writers like LeGuin and Atwood now feel dated. Responds to Lane Haygood's remark that Le Guin's The Left Hand of Darkness should have made a contest list that Book of the New Sun won, mainly by discussing the gender balance and lasting appeal of the authors.
disputes

1) Since this is a democratically selected list, you would probably have to limit the voting to exclusively to women to hope that a woman author would make the top 10. We're talking about authors whose work would inspire someone in high school or early college--at an age when a writer helps set the concrete of a reader's tastes. Stil, I wouldn't have been surprised to see "Frankenstein" on this list. But the most successful female authors among females in my experience are Rowling, Meyer, and Lackey. None of which are going to make this list. I haven't read "The Time Traveler's Wife". Is it as deserving of being on this list as "The Name of the Wind"?

2) The big names are Feminist authors whose work simply feels dated now (imagine a Pro-Suffrage SF writer). LeGuin, Atwood, Yarboro...These are all skilled writers but their Great Works are essentially museum pieces to certain themes that were current in the 1970s. An exception IMO is Joanna Russ, whose novels are still worthwhile for study by budding writers -- but I'm not sure I'd call them pleasurable.

3) The woman authors whose work still carries today, do their best work in short story format: Tiptree, Willis, and Nancy Kress (yeah! I said it!).

2011-08-02 Jerry Friedman replying to James Wynn #

Disputes James Wynn's claim that women authors on a best-of-genre list are dated or unlikely to rank, arguing that Atwood and Le Guin remain current. Supports the point by citing Atwood's The Year of the Flood and The Handmaid's Tale and Le Guin's The Dispossessed, and noting that Tiptree's stories are more museum-like.
disputes

This comment strikes me as very strange. Atwood is still quite successful. I just finished /The Year of the Flood/ (set at the same time as /Oryx and Crake/), which there's nothing particularly feminist about. Even /The Handmaid's Tale/ seems no less current now than it would have in the '70s.

And Le Guin? What about her Great Works is stuck in the '70s? Unless you consider "The Word for World is Forest" to be one of them, which I don't. Incidentally, /The Dispossessed/ was the only novel by a woman on the science-fiction shortlist. I can hardly see that kind of anarchism as being to the 1970s what women's suffrage was to the 1910s.

Tiptree is the one some of whose stories I might have called museum pieces, such as the excellent "The Women Men Don't See".

Jerry Friedman

2011-08-02 Marc Aramini replying to James Wynn #

Argues that much 1970s SF feels dated and that female writers like Atwood and Le Guin rely on tired dystopian and didactic conventions, preferring female fantasy authors and Gothic writers. Responds to James Wynn's remark about a contest and women authors, defending The Book of the New Sun's winning position against recent selections.
disputes

A whole lot of 70s SF feels dated, from the artistic efforts of Samuel Delany on down, in the same way modernism seems a bit musty lately, or those socially conscious novels of the late 19th and early 20th century that are many things, but are probably not artistic in the final analysis.

I don't know, I always felt like the previously mentioned Atwood was not doing exciting things in the way that more mainstream female writers like Flannery O'Connor or Acker or even Byatt were, I felt like I'd read 1984 and other similar dystopian stuff over and over and over before when I read some of her stuff, or LeGuin's for that matter, so tired of that weak lowest common denominator left slanting. Their SF in general IS too closely tied to a didactic, pragmatic, realistic purpose, instead of the quite possibly teenage boy aimed wow rocket ships explosions impossible science human villains cool plot twist kind of stuff men seem to write in SF ... .

I think there is a better case for quality fantasy female authors than actual SF for some reason (Hobb, Tanith lee, Susanah Clark, some of the gothic (southern or otherwise) stuff, etc).

but some of the works that were selected seem like they are just recent and in people's memories now, something that is not true of New Sun, which I would like to think deserved to win.

And I don't think women writers had or have any kind of monopoly on weak lowest-common-denominator left, right, or libertarian slanting (though this does bring up Ayn Rand...), certainly not considering the shoot-em-ups that you rightly note are aimed at teenage boys.

And, of course, Le Guin, author of the best YA fantasy ever (and I say this without having read Harry Potter).

Lee's Flat Earth books are one of my guilty pleasures.

I'll go along with that, unsurprisingly.

Jerry Friedman
