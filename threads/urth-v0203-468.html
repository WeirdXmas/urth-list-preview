eponyms as saints

Urth list · 4 messages · 2002-03-26 to 2002-03-27

2002urthThe Book of the New SunJerry FriedmanMichael Andre-Driussi
The thread: Michael Andre-Driussi argued that the eponyms behind Nessus's busts are known figures such as guillotine or gerrymander rather than saints, conceding only St. Amand, and tied this to the short-lived city quarters that imply recent rather than ancient busts. Jerry Friedman countered playfully that Commonwealth names echo Catholic saints like Baldanders, Foila, Katharine and Barbara, and compared the pattern to Lynch, Boycott and jerry-built. Andre-Driussi replied that saints are not usually eponyms in the sense of naming countless people, offering awards and lodges as better parallels, and rejected the idea that Baldanders is a bad guy by name. Friedman apologized for an obnoxious remark and asked whether Commonwealth names come from a broader canonical list of admired people, citing Agilus, Agia, Severian and Severa. The thread ends without resolution, with the disagreement left open.
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: busts honor known eponyms like guillotine or gerrymander, not saints, except possibly St. Amand; quarters are recent
  • Jerry Friedman: Commonwealth names echo Catholic saints, so eponyms are obvious; real-world eponyms show the same pattern
  • Michael Andre-Driussi: saints aren't really eponyms in the usual sense; awards and lodges are closer parallels; Baldanders isn't a bad name
  • Jerry Friedman: names may come from a canonical list of admired people, not only saints

Themes: Names and their meanings, Myth and folklore sources, Catholic faith and sacraments

2002-03-26 Michael Andre-Driussi #

Argues that the eponyms of Nessus's busts are known rather than unknown figures, possibly eponyms like guillotine or gerrymander, not saints, while conceding one saint, St. Amand. Grounds this in the city's quarters being short-lived and abandoned as the city moves north, so the busts are recent rather than ancient.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: Nessus busts depict known eponyms, not saints

That's funny, because I don't normally think of the people of the Commonwealth having saints (granted that they are all named after Catholic saints, but I have never seen this as a literal sign of the saints being resurrected, as James Jordan did in his early interview with Wolfe, but rather as a clever trick to get big history and alien-ness in one coherent, convenient package), so I used non-saint eponyms.

But of course you are right, they =do= have saints (well one: "St. Amand"), so the busts become perhaps a sequence of saints and commoners. From high to low, altitude-wise at least.

Still, another point I wanted to transmit was that while Nessus is "eternal," the quarters of the city are definitely not: they have a rather brief lifespan, in fact, and then they are abandoned to ruin as the city moves North. So the busts, despite their seeming antiquity, are not all that old, really, and were placed there by recent architects for recent tastes. (I mean, it isn't like they are as alien in culture/distant in time as Etruscan busts in medieval Italy.) With this, I would guess that the eponyms are known rather than unknown; and since Severian's time seems to be at a low-tide for churches, I suspect the eponyms to be more like guillotine, gerrymander, and shyster than St. Agia (although that would've made a neat moment!).

=mantis=

No, forgive me, it is not. I have never heard of saints categorized as eponyms for giving their names to countless individuals--as opposed to giving their name "to a tribe, nation, process, product, etc." (Name for a city, which you gave before, seemed close to tribe/nation, athough greatly diluted.) If it is clear to you then that is enough.

=Saint Austregildis=, du-uh!
