Sev's parents

Urth list · 3 messages · 1999-11-08 to 1999-12-01

1999urthThe Book of the New SunJim HenleyRoy C. LackeyJon Camfield
The thread: Jeremy Crampton's claim that Severian lied about never knowing his parents was defended by Roy Lackey, who cited a breastfeeding memory and Ouen's recognition as evidence, and argued that Severian's perfect memory and self-deception explain why he disowns that knowledge. Jim Henley disputed that the narration is a lie merely because the narrator is flawed, arguing the full truth about the tomb emerges across the whole work, and illustrated pacing with a mock opening chapter. Jon Camfield proposed that the memory is too perfect to be literal, so that Severian gets lost in boyhood recollections and omits known facts, and suggested checking whether his meta-comments thin out where he errs. The thread ends without resolution, with the positions left standing side by side.
  • Roy C. Lackey: Severian lied about his parents; his self-deception and memory explain the disowning
  • Jim Henley: Flawed narrator is not a lie; full truth emerges over the whole work
  • Jon Camfield: Memory is too perfect to be literal; he omits facts while lost in recollection

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Fathers, mothers and children

1999-11-08 Roy C. Lackey #

Defends Jeremy Crampton's claim that Severian lied about never knowing his parents, citing a memory in Claw of breastfeeding in a gray cell and Citadel's recognition that Ouen is his father. Argues that Severian's perfect memory and self-deception explain why he disowns that knowledge, and asks whether his childhood mausoleum visit came before the events of URTH.
supports · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Severian lied about never knowing his parents; Ouen is Severian's father

In defense of Jeremy Crampton's argument that Sev lied about "I have never known my father or mother", I offer this: In CLAW, third page of chapter XXVII, Sev does remember his mother, remembers being breast fed in a gray cell. Whether that constitutes "knowing" her or not is problematic. He can't raise his eyes to look into her face, yet can see the gray of her cell wall. He never spells out whether he ever equated "Katharine" with "Catherine" or not. Sometimes having a perfect memory has its drawbacks, so that you have to lie to yourself to keep your sanity. It wouldn't be pleasant to see your mother's face once a year, particularly when a child too young to distinguish between ritual and reality, on a bloody severed head.

Also, he met Ouen twice and on the second occasion, in CITADEL, figured out that Ouen was his father. If he didn't get to know Ouen any better after that, it was his own fault.

the mausoleum before the events of URTH? This is a genuine rather than rhetorical question.<<

Chapter XXXVIII of CITADEL: "I know too in whose mausoleum I tarried as a child, that little building of stone with its rose, its fountain, and its flying ship all graven. I have disturbed my own tomb, and now I go to lie in it."

Roy

1999-11-11 Jim Henley #

Argues that Severian's narration is not a lie just because he has character flaws, and that the complete truth about the tomb emerges over the whole work. Makes the point with a mock opening chapter about a boy who plays in his tomb and later learns he is a time traveller, illustrating the book's pacing.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Severian does not lie, only has flaws

So it was his own fault. The question is whether Severian lies, not whether he has character flaws.

This is another case where someone seems to confuse the specific case (Sev lies) with the general (Sev is a Bad Guy). Evidence of the latter does not constitute evidence of the former. When people point this out it is suggested that they are somehow unwilling to see Sev as having flaws.

Thank you. It's established. It doesn't make Sev a liar though, just shows that for a novice writer he has an instinctive sense of pacing. I mean, that would be a good book, wouldn't it? Chapter Two: "So there was this tomb I used to play in as a boy. It was my tomb, and I was some kind of time traveller. Not that I knew that right at that moment. See, time isn't like a river, it's like an ocean with ripples. That's what these space guys told me years later. Their names were . . . "

Note that over the course of the work as a whole, you get the complete truth about the tomb as Sev understands it.

Best,

Jim

1999-12-01 Jon Camfield #

Proposes that Severian's perfect memory is too perfect to be literal, arguing that as he writes he gets lost in memories of boyhood and so omits facts he knows, which explains contradictions. Responds to a Roy Lackey post quoting the torturers'-apprentice passage about never knowing parents, and suggests checking whether Severian's meta-comments thin out where he errs.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian's memory is too perfect; he forgets while writing

my part, I had already adopted as my own the device

May I offer a third possibility? Sev's memory is too perfect. As he meta-commented on a few times, he would oftentimes get lost in memories, and find himself crying or laughing in his Autarchial chambers. I propose that as he's churning these books out, he gets lost in his memories to do so--lost enough such that after he gets into the flow, he neglects to include facts which he knows, but does not treat at the time because he, for all he knows as he is writing, is back at the citadel as a boy. If you wanted to get really complicated, you could analyze Sev's initial meta-comments (when he speaks as the writer/as the adult) and if they get scarce around the times he speak erroneously, though this is a decidedly bad case to try that out on ;)
