The thread: Readers of the Centipede Press newsletter debated whether Wolfe's Urth had a Centipede Press map and what its geography and compass implied. Gerry Quinn doubted any published map would be definitive and asked what became of Europe, Asia and Africa under the popular South America hypothesis, while António Pedro Marques argued a map is either crucial or decorative and that the Gyoll cannot be the Amazon. Rick Norwood proposed a six-point compass and south-at-top as evidence Wolfe did everything differently, and David Stockhoff asked him to justify it, floating a hexagonal map and a reversed-globe theory. Mark Millman argued the compass is spherical, with up and down marked, and that south-at-top is a typo, while Stockhoff and Millman traded doubts about whether the text mentions six points at all. The thread drifted into citation quibbles and ended with no agreement and no text settled.
  • Gerry Quinn: a published Urth map would not be definitive and the popular South America hypothesis leaves other continents unexplained
  • António Pedro Marques: a map is crucial or decorative, and the Gyoll cannot be the Amazon if the Commonwealth is South America
  • Rick Norwood: the six-point compass and south-at-top show Wolfe deliberately departed from our conventions
  • Mark Millman: the compass is spherical with up and down marked, and south-at-top is probably a typo
  • David Stockhoff: asks for the textual basis of six points and suggests a reversed globe with Buenos Aires beyond the Andes
  • Fernando Gouvea: a flipped South America is possible in the Plan(e)t Engineering map but does not fit as south-up

Themes: Stories within stories, The dying sun and the cosmos, Power, rulers and politics

2020-12-06 António Pedro Marques replying to Fernando Gouvea #

Argues that in Wolfe's books a map is either crucial and needs closer study or merely decorative, and that if the Commonwealth is South America with Nessus as Buenos Aires, the Gyoll cannot be the Amazon. Responds to a newsletter-quoted signature block from Fernando Gouvea, whose message consists mostly of a quoted Centipede Press newsletter and a joke about dads in movies.
disputes · source-and-allusion · theories: Commonwealth is South America, Nessus is Buenos Aires

In a Gene Wolfe book, either the map is awfully important and nobody has studied it well enough, and presenting a half-accurate version is counterproductive, or the map is a mere detail and worrying too much about it is counterproductive.

But if the Comminwealth is supposed to be South America and Nessus is Buenos Aires, then certainly the Gyoll cannot be the Amazonas.

2020-12-06 Norwood, Frederick Hudson replying to Fernando Gouvea #

Proposes that the New Sun's world departs from ours in its compass, with six cardinal points rather than four and south at the top of maps, as evidence that Wolfe deliberately did everything differently. Replies to Fernando Gouvea's post, doubting that people from Earth would carry the convention over to the new sun.
proposes · genre-and-craft · theories: Wolfe deliberately inverted Earth's compass conventions on Urth

The most important thing is to have six cardinal points of the compass, not four. Second, to have south be up. I'm not certain that the people from earth carry this over to the new sun, but it would indicate an awareness that, in this series, Wolfe deliberately did everything different from the way we do things.

Best,
Rick Norwood

2020-12-07 Mark Millman replying to Norwood, Frederick Hudson #

Argues that the six-pointed compass in The Urth of the New Sun is most likely a spherical compass marking up and down as well as the horizontal directions, not six coplanar points. Supports this by citing the classical Chinese and medieval European map orientations to suggest south-at-top is a typo for top, and by calling coplanar points too arbitrary for Wolfe's engineering mind.
disputes · close-textual-reading · source-and-allusion · theories: Six compass points are a spherical compass marking up and down

Folks,

And on Monday 7 December 2020, David Stockhoff asked:

To which Rick replied:

I imagine this is a typo for "top". You're probably aware that maps with south at the top are the classical Chinese standard, which was influential in east Asia (and remember that there's evidence that Korea may have been a dominant power on Urth for a while, including having launched Jonas' ship). For that matter, medieval European maps often put east at the top, taking their orientation from Jerusalem and Eden.

But my impression of the six-pointed compass is that it's a spherical compass that indicates up and down as well as south, north, west, and east. Six coplanar cardinal points seems too arbitrary to appeal to Wolfe's engineering brain.

Best,

Mark Millman

I imagine this is a typo for "top".  You're probably aware that maps
with south at the top are the classical Chinese standard, which was
influential in east Asia (and remember that there's evidence that
Korea may have been a dominant power on Urth for a while, including
having launched Jonas' ship).  For that matter, medieval European maps
often put east at the top, taking their orientation from Jerusalem and
Eden.

But my impression of the six-pointed compass is that it's a spherical
compass that indicates up and down as well as south, north, west, and
east.  Six coplanar cardinal points seems too arbitrary to appeal to
Wolfe's engineering brain.

Lists several ways The Urth of the New Sun departs from expectations, including a six-point compass, ending the first book by leaving the starting city, and the man-master inversion, and asks whether this carries into the Long Sun and Short Sun books. Also notes that The Book of the New Sun is a trilogy in four or five volumes, responding to David Stockhoff's newsletter-related post.
questions · genre-and-craft

In Urth of the New Son, Wolfe did almost everything differently from what the reading expected, including a compass having six points and south being at the south. Another example is that, in the end end the first book, instead of reaching their destination, the characters are just leaving the city they started in. Another is that, at one point, we find out that the man is the master and the master is the man. It is one of the book's charms.

I do not know if this carries over into Book of the Long Sun and Book of the Short Sun.

Also Book of the New Sun is a trilogy in four (or five) volumes.

Best,
Rick
