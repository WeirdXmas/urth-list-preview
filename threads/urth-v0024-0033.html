Themes: Humour in Wolfe, Mirrors, pictures and art

1999-03-02 John Bishop #

Argues that the small asides in the Shadow of the Torturer passage about Robert and Marie, a naked native and an airplane, are references to some famous source. Asks whether anyone has identified the two unexplained paintings in Severian's visit to the Library, and notes that Valeria mistranslates the stand-in Latin, possibly as a humorous hint that things are not what they seem.
questions · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Valeria's mistranslation is a deliberate joke

I think all or most of the little asides are a reference to something; the airplane is a clue which I can't decipher, but if there isn't a (reasonably) famous Robert and Marie in a jungle in the 1920s-1970s time frame, I'll be surprised. The naked "native" is a clue; and the dialog might well be lifted/approximated.

Along those lines, there are three paintings we see described in Severian's visit to the Library: one is explained (the astronaut on the Moon), but the other two are a mystery to me. Has some other person figured them out?

I did catch that when Valeria translates the "Latin" (which Wolfe tells us is used to stand in for another langauge), she gets it wrong. I _think_ this is meant to be humorous, and a hint that she's trying to impress Severian; it may be also a hint to us that some things _aren't_ what they seem.

-John
