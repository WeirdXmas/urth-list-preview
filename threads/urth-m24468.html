The thread: Readers debated whether the gods in the Latro books are literally real and whether Soldier of Sidon ends the series. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Fernando Gouvea doubted a further Latro book, with Gouvea citing Wolfe's own indication and the central role of the gods. Jeff Wilson, Christopher Simon and Marc Aramini argued the gods are real, while Simon offered the head-injury reading as the alternative to dismiss. Marc Aramini and Lee Berman then moved to a theological reading of pre-Christian zeitgeist and divine recombination, which David Stockhoff extended speculatively. The thread drifted from the series' ending into broader theology without resolution.
  • Marc Aramini: pagan gods are real and syncretic, a pre-Christian zeitgeist leading to the Incarnation
  • Christopher Simon: gods are real, not hallucinations from Latro's head trauma
  • Jeff Wilson: pagan gods are meant to be literally real, per Wolfe's stated beliefs
  • Fernando Gouvea: another Latro book is unlikely since the gods are central to the series
  • Lee Berman: divine identities recombine across the Latro and Sun books; humanity may be unready for revelation beyond Christ
  • Sergei SOLOVIEV: Soldier of Sidon left him depressed, contrasting Latro's victory in Arete with his decline

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Gods, angels and the Increate, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

2013-07-29 Antonin Scriabin #

Argues that Soldier of the Mist's amnesiac hero Latro parallels the Long Sun books, with apparent gods, blood sacrifice, and a threat from non-humans, and resembles a prototype of The Book of the New Sun. Asks whether the Latro series ends with Soldier of Sidon, avoiding spoilers.
proposes · author-and-biography · theories: Latro books are a prototype of The Book of the New Sun; Unknown God and Outsider are the same figure

I had been putting off the Latro books for a while so that I would always have an unread Wolfe series to look forward to, but my curiosity has finally gotten the better of me. I am nearly done with *Soldier of the Mist, *and have been enjoying it. It has a number of parallels with the *Long Sun *books, such as the (apparent) reality of gods, detailed discussions of blood sacrifice, a just-outside-of-view threat from non-humans like vampires and werewolves, etc. It actually seems like a prototype version of what became *The Book of the New Sun*; a central male hero suffers some kind of mental "event" (on the one hand, severe brain trauma, and on the other, enlightenment) that opens the door to gods (or to what appear as gods). In both works, these gods are petty, prone to infighting, and in many ways offer more questions than answers to the hero. He has only been mentioned twice so far, but I also feel that the Unknown God / Outsider similarity can't be a coincidence. Anyways, I know that you guys have all probably been over these topics plenty, I just wanted to air a few thoughts. I do have one question: does the series conclude with *Soldier of Sidon*? I would, of course, like to avoid spoilers, I am mostly interested in the possibility of more Latro novels. Thanks!

2013-07-29 Marc Aramini replying to Christopher Simon #

Argues that the gods in the Latro books are real and that the religious scheme is syncretic, citing the triple goddess and the merging of Pleistorus into Ahura Mazda as signs of divine identities recrystallizing into something more uniform. Responds to Christopher Simon's remark that Latro's head trauma could explain the gods, dismissing that reading as only a superficial realistic touch.
proposes · theology-and-religion · theories: Gods in Latro are real; Latro's religion is syncretic; Pleistorus merges into Ahura Mazda

Yeah the trauma is only the most superficial touch of realism- gods and the wellsprings of human faith are as real as they can be in those books, but the religious scheme is syncretic. The triple goddess, the sublimation of Pleistorus into Ahura Mazda- these are all pointing to a recrystallization and refocus of the divine identities into something more uniform once their ideological differences are resolved. Wonderful books.

2013-07-29 Sergei SOLOVIEV replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Shares an overall impression of the Latro books, saying the first two volumes impressed him while the third, Soldier of Sidon, left him depressed. Cites the ending of Soldier of Arete, where Latro seems to win against the petty gods despite his injured memory and returns home, and contrasts it with Latro's failure to find a cure and his slide into chaos in Egypt and Africa.
supports · close-textual-reading

Dear Antonin, and All -

just to share my impressions, I was greatly impressed by first two books (Soldier of the Mist and Soldier of Arete), and was
waiting with impatience for Soldier of Sidon, but in the end was rather depressed by it - my overall impression
is that the ending of Soldier of Arete is great, I think that Latro in some sense wins against these "petty" gods in spite
of his injured memory - and he goes home! There are also other great pages in the first two books (e.g., the
meeting with Proserpina, the falanga's attacks in the end of the "Soldier of the Mist"). But the third book was a sad conclusion, because we see that Latro failed with his plan to find a cure at home, and his adventures in Egypt and in black Africa look as a slow
drowning into chaos - his meeting with the Seven Lions tribe is a sad story etc -

Regards -

Sergei Soloviev

2013-07-31 Marc Aramini replying to Lee Berman #

Proposes that the Latro books explore the pre-Christian zeitgeist leading to the Incarnation, where an immutable God takes human nature, with Wolfe's gnostic play central. Replies to Lee Berman's remarks on Wolfe's view of pagan gods, calling their reality a paradox for Christians and ending with e pluribus unum applied to humankind.
proposes · theology-and-religion · theories: Latro books explore pre-Christian zeitgeist leading to Incarnation; Wolfe's gnostic play contrasts Creator and human God; Pagan gods real within Christian worldview is paradox

"Given Jeff's consideration of Wolfe's assessment of the reality of pagan gods, this re- combining theme makes Wolfe's personal theology very interesting with regard to pagan gods, the Trinity and Christianity.?"?
?
????????????????????????

I actually fancy that the Latro books are an exploration of the pre-Christian zeitgeist leading up to the time of the Pax Roman that becomes something of the ultimate philosophical moment of Christian history - mankind is at last ready for divine revision, and that readiness creates a scenario were immutable God takes on a mutable human nature.? Wolfe's gnostic play is here in full force - it is impossible to ignore the ideological differences between the Creator God and the God who is fully human and fully divine; the relationship between what people believe in and their own character no doubt shapes their perception of the world of the divine.

The paradox of pagan gods being "real" in a Christian world view is something that bears thinking about, but I think that trite coin motto works: e pluribus unum ... but mankind must be unified, too.

I agree

gods, the Trinity and Christianity.
