the points of it all

Urth list · 3 messages · 1999-11-09 to 1999-11-10

1999urthThe Book of the New SunAlex David GroceClifford DraneDaniel Fusch
The thread: Readers debated the storytelling contest in The Book of the New Sun: whether the attack cut it short so that chance decides the outcome, and whether Severian, an executioner, would have judged it. Daniel Fusch argued torturers are bound to obedience and that Severian dislikes judging, citing his own statements and Foila's postponements, and held that the recorded stories keep the contestants' dreams and deaths from being meaningless. Clifford Drane stressed chance or fate over the characters' investment and agreed that his reading and Cliff Judge's immortality reading coexist, noting the series is itself storytelling. Alex David Groce argued that the personal loves and hates in the series carry cosmic importance, citing Wolfe's personalized cosmos and answering Drane that the promise to record was more than a reaction to events. The thread ended in disagreement rather than resolution.
  • Daniel Fusch: Severian is bound to obey and dislikes judging, so he would not have judged the contest; the recorded stories give the dead meaning
  • Clifford Drane: the attack cuts the contest short, so chance or fate decides; the immortality and storytelling readings can coexist
  • Alex David Groce: personal loves and hates carry cosmic weight in Wolfe's personalized cosmos, so Severian's acts gain universal significance

Themes: Stories within stories, Books, libraries and writing, Free will and choice

1999-11-09 Daniel Fusch #

Disputes Cliff Judge's claim that Severian would have judged the storytelling contest, arguing instead that torturers are bound to obedience and Severian dislikes judging. Cites Severian's own statements about his discomfort as judge and Foila's repeated postponements, and argues that the recorded stories keep the contestants' dreams and deaths from being meaningless.
disputes · close-textual-reading

Hi, Cliff!

I just need to disagree with you about ONE more thing. I think you're reading Severian wrong.

(snip)
"I can't imagine Sev *not* judging the contest, had they lived. He's an executioner - very used to making decisions and not getting emotionally invested in the consequences of his judgments. I think he would have judged, if the attack had not occured."

Severian tells us, however, that torturers are not supposed to make decisions or judgements. Their task is blind, unquestioning obedience to the decisions passed down to them by the proper authorities. Severian even tells us that he feels extremely uncomfortable when placed in the position of judge, is that he has developed an extreme aversion to judging, so that he is glad when Foila keeps postponing the judgement.

And I would also argue that the death of the contestants does not render their dreams and their desires meaningless at all. Their dreams are preserved in the stories, and their love is preserved through the stories (the stories, after all, were told in order to win Foila's hand--except for Foila's story, which was set against the others). By recording the stories, Severian ensures that their lives--and their deaths--are NOT meaningless.

But I agree with most of your other points.

--Daniel

1999-11-10 Alex David Groce #

Argues that the judgment scene in The Urth of the New Sun shows the personal loves and hates of The Book of the New Sun carry cosmic importance, so Severian's acts gain universal significance. Grounds this in Wolfe's personalized cosmos, citing Tzad's ship, Yesod and "The Death of the Island Doctor", and answers Clifford Drane's view that Severian's promise to record the stories was only a reaction to events.
disputes · theology-and-religion · close-textual-reading · theories: Judgment in Urth shows personal acts carry cosmic importance

Ah, but whether this is true or not, I think it's not at all consistent with the theme of BOTNS--the judgement in URTH shows that all of the "loves, hates, etc." we've seen in NEW SUN are, in fact, of cosmic importance--I think Wolfe is one of the few science-fiction writers who personalizes the universe while maintaining grandness of scale. Wolfe has few Big Dumb Objects, but some amazing Big Smart Objects (Tzad's ship, Yesod itself). Severian is special, but he's also something of an Everyman character--the fact that his actions are truly significant is, I suspect, meant to grant all human actions significance. Wolfe's stories tend to take place in an almost frighteningly "caring" cosmos--"The Death of the Island Doctor" is a good example of this. It is impossible for the characters in Wolfe's work to escape responsibility and judgement by claiming indifference on the part of fate/the universe.
