> What are your favourite works of SF and Why?
>
> Someone once quipped: ?the Golden Age of Science Fiction is twelve?, meaning that what you read when you are twelve impresses itself upon an area of the brain that will seek out more like it. It follows that as your reading level and experience becomes greater, your favourites will continually alter. Readers in their seventies may have developed the kind of sophistication that would allow them to enjoy the sequence of four novels known as ?The Book of the New Sun? (1980-83) by Gene Wolfe. Unusually, the book masquerades as a fantasy about an ancient and depleted civilisation of the future, but readers will sooner or later notice that all this fantasy has an astonishingly firm ? if elusive ? underpinning of SF. Younger readers may, however, be more likely to enjoy ?Snow Crash? (1992) by Neal Stephenson, set in a much closer future than the one Wolfe creates, starring among others a high speed pizza delivery girl, and some very clever, very young hackers creating a cyberworld.

2012-01-17 David Duffy replying to Gwern Branwen #

Relays a podcast episode in which Jonathan Strahan and Gary K. Wolfe discuss Gene Wolfe, noting BotNS's lack of Hugo nomination, the Wizard Knight as a starting point, and Shields of Mars as readable without deep analysis. Shares the link and summarises the episode's points, including Wolfe's prose compared to origami and Wolfe-as-chess and Ulysses comparisons.
informs

http://www.jonathanstrahan.com.au/wp/2012/01/07/episode-83-live-with-gary-k-wolfe/

The other Wolfe on the Chicago Literary Hall of Fame event, and Strahan on BotNS, _The Map_, _A Cabin on the Coast_.

Strahan discusses the fact that BotNS was not Hugo nominated, compared to other awards it picked up (more popular with other authors than SF fans). Strahan didn't finished BotNS ;), though he has read Long. He thinks the short stories more accessible. GKW thinks _Wizard Knight_ a more start for beginners at "how to read Gene Wolfe". Comments Wolfe writing has been compared to origami.

Gary K Wolfe also recorded an interview with Wolfe for the Chicago public libraries system around the time. He also cited a spoiler warning at a convention (they are about to talk about _Moby Dick_).

Strahan mentions _Shields of Mars_, introducing the idea that Wolfe can be read pleasurably without plumbing the depths. He doesn't think there are any depths to that story. GKW then compares hermeneutics of Wolfe as studying chess, and the Sun series to _Ulysses_.

They will discuss Wolfe in subsequent podcasts.

CHeers, David Duffy.

2012-02-22 Marc Aramini replying to James Wynn #

Proposes that the tree-and-river mating in The Fifth Head of Cerberus relates to the new species on Blue or Green being hybrids with tree stock, linking this to chromosome doubling in asexual reproduction. Grounds the idea in two quoted passages, one about drowned children and one about the narrator's frog-egg experiments, and in the eucharist scene in the forest.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Trees are vanished gods mated with humans; New species on Blue or Green are hybrids with tree stock

I was re-reading Fifth Head of Cerberus a long time ago and there were two things that really struck me in terms of how I think the dynamic between trees / Silkhorn / life on Blue or Green works.

"They mated with trees and drowned the children to honor their rivers" (pg 11 of old ace copy) and "I told her about my experiments (I was stimulating unfertilized frogs' eggs to asexual development and then DOUBLING THE CHROMOSOMES by a chemical treatment so that a further asexual generation could be produced)" (pg 23)

As you know, I thought the eucharist scene in which "they" are watching in the forest involves the trees (vanished gods), and that the new species on Blue or Green are "hybrids" or old ones with that tree stock, which would double the genetic information before sex segregation in the hybrids. I felt good reading that in Fifth Head, as it at least indicates to me that Gene has thought about this process for asexual reproduction before, especially since hybridization DOES double the chromosomes.

I am aware Fifth Head is about cloning, but interesting to me nonetheless.
