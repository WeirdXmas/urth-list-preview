2008-11-06 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to Stephen Case #

Disputes Stephen Case's claim that Heinlein's Starship Troopers bars non-veterans from voting, saying the book requires only a government service tour, not military service. Notes that volunteers could be assigned to the military and that veteran status was also required for public office, a point with no bearing on Wolfe.
disputes

This is often repeated, and NOT true. You had to do a tour of government service, but it did not have to be military. However, once you volunteered, you went where they put you, so you could easily wind up in the military without intending to.

It wasn't so much "earning the right," as proving that you were willing to put society ahead of your own personal concerns. And it extended beyond voting; you also had to be a "veteran" to hold public office.

2008-11-06 Stephen Case #

Argues that voting should be limited to those who have served in the military, citing Heinlein's Starship Troopers, where citizenship and voting require a military tour. Responds to earlier posts doubting that everyone should vote, offering the novel as a precedent and noting the protagonist's enlistment motive, with uncertainty about whether the film keeps it.
informs

In response to the comments on it not being a good idea that everyone should vote, this was a main premise in Heinlein's Starship Troopers. In that society, you weren't considered a citizen (and thus allowed to vote) unless you had served a tour in the military. It wasn't mandatory that you served (like in certain contemporary European countries), but you weren't allowed to vote-- you weren't considered to have "earned the right"-- unless you had served. This was a primary motivation (if I recall correctly) of the main character joining up. I'm not sure how much of this was kept in the movie . . .

-Steve

2008-11-06 Russ Allbery replying to Adam Thornton #

Argues that the film works as a parody of the Heinlein novel rather than an adaptation, since its events differ from the book while its portrayed society shows what the book's ideas would produce in practice. Responds to Stephen Case's claim that the movie was an excellent adaptation of a Heinlein book, offering a contrasting view without citing evidence from the text.
disputes

I dunno, I found the movie an excellent parody of the book. The events of the movie weren't the events of the book, but the society portrayed by the movie is the society that I think the ideas in the book would have created in practice.
