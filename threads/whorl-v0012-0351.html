Horn Dies

Whorl list · 13 messages · 2001-02-26 to 2001-02-27

The thread: Posters debated whether Horn's death in Horn Dies recapitulates Silk's death and resurrection, read through Eucharistic and Christian imagery such as bread and wine, ascension, and the Whorl as partial heaven. James Jordan and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes offered overlapping Christian readings, while Fernando Gouvea argued Horn exists within Silk and the narrating book is Silk denying his identity. A parallel argument arose over whether Catholic affiliation matters to appreciating Wolfe, with Alex David Groce and others stressing that it does not. Jordan's recounted letter, in which Wolfe calls himself a Christian writer rather than a Christian evangelist, ended the dispute, though the thread drifted off topic and into list-scope reminders.
  • James Jordan: Horn's death recapitulates Silk's death and resurrection, with the Whorl as a partial heaven
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: Eucharistic and Christian resonances coexist, with Horn and Silk becoming one after Horn's death
  • Fernando Q. Gouvea: Horn exists within Silk, so the narrating book is Silk denying he is Silk
  • Alex David Groce: Catholic affiliation is not required to appreciate Wolfe, and Nettle's return to the Whorl is plausible
  • james.kellar: Wolfe's fiction may make readers think more favourably of Catholicism without aiming to convert them

Themes: Death and resurrection, Catholic faith and sacraments, Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies

2001-02-26 James Jordan #

Argues that Horn's death recapitulates Silk's own death and resurrection, linking the shift from animal sacrifice to bread and wine with Horn's offering of bread and wine as Christ's body and blood. Grounds this in Paul's 1 Corinthians reading of bread as both Jesus and the Church, and proposes that both Silk and Horn continue living in Silk's body.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Horn recapitulates Silk's death and resurrection; Horn and Silk both live in Silk's body

THANK YOU! It all makes sense to me now. Yes, Horn has died. Got it. Thank you. I'd missed this.

And it raises more thoughts for those of us sympathetic to Wolfe's Christian standpoint. Silk's own death/resurrection does seem to lead to a shift from animal sacrifices to bread & wine. We don't see this (as I recall) in the Long Sun series, but Olivine is aware of it. But then Horn recapitulates this experience, and comes to be "in union with Silk," and says that the bread and wine he offers with Olivine is "his" body and blood. Recall that according to Paul in 1 Corinthians, the bread represents both Jesus and ALSO the Church. I now suspect that this is part of what is going on.

Finally, I strongly suspect that the answer to the question of how the book ends, one way or another, is that as both Silk and Horn have "died," yet both continue to live IN SILK'S BODY.

2001-02-26 James Jordan #

Argues that Horn's death and passage up to the Whorl can be read as ascension or resurrection, and that the Whorl shifts into a partial image of heaven, with Old Pas becoming a Constantine-like New Pas. Responds to Blattid's suggestion that the Whorl equals resurrection by noting the mixed Egypt and Exodus and Jewish and Roman allegorical frames, and the prayer for daily bread.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: The Whorl shifts into a partial image of heaven; Horn's death is an ascension to heaven; Old Pas resurrected as New Pas

Maybe. Or maybe an ascension, since in some sense "Silk" has just been "resurrected." In a sense, Horn has "gone up to heaven" in answer to the prayer for "daily" bread. Of course, that definitely mixes the metaphor, since the Whorl is originally Egypt under Pharaoh, from which Moses delivers the people, and even more it is the people of God under the Jewish leaders (Ayuntamiento) and Romans (Pas etc.), from which Jesus delivers those who trust Him. At the same time, since Horn goes UP to the Whorl as a result of dying, the Whorl seems to shift into a partial image of heaven. Since Old Pas has himself died and been resurrected into New Pas (Constantine-like?), the Whorl can shift in (one) of its symbolic meanings -- especially since Wolfe is not writing any kind of close allegory anyway.

FWIW: I'm a Reformed (Calvinist) former minister, now theologian; but on the sacramental (real presence, not just symbol) side of that coterie. So, I've got no problem with Wolfe's symbolism.

2001-02-27 James Jordan #

Disputes the suggestion that Wolfe's fiction aims to promote his Christian faith, saying Wolfe is a writer who is a Christian rather than a Christian writer. Supports this by recounting a private letter from Wolfe, and adds a remark that Wolfe calls himself an urban rather than Southern writer.
disputes · author-and-biography

I don't really think so. Long ago I wrote to ask him if he thought of himself as a Christian writer, and he wrote back to say that no, he is a writer who is a Christian. I don't think "evangelization" is part of his intention, though I'm sure he'd be happy if it were part of the result. BTW, I also asked him, in the light of *Peace,* if he thought of himself in any degree as a Southern writer (a la Walker Percy), and he responded by saying he that is more of an urban than a rural writer.

FWIW

Pat. Nut.
