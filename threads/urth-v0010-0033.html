Napoleon

Urth list · 6 messages · 1998-04-30 to 1998-05-04

1998urthRobert KingWilliam H. AnsleyPaul C Duggan
The thread: Readers puzzled over a remark about Napoleon's hand in his coat or waistcoat in Wolfe's Peace, which the narrator calls innocent yet offensive. Robert King joked the gesture was self-fondling and argued a schoolboy's remark was probably innocent, while William H. Ansley disputed that, citing the opening page and arguing the gesture was physically awkward and not meant innocently. Paul C Duggan added an unsourced colostomy trivia claim. Ansley's last post argued Weer kept repeating the story as an adult while still considering it innocent, citing a later exchange with Louis Gold. The thread ended in a split between King's jokey reading and Ansley's textual one, with no agreement.
  • Robert King: the schoolboy's Napoleon remark was probably innocent and the reading is a joke about self-fondling
  • William H. Ansley: the remark was not innocent, the hand gesture is awkward, and Weer repeats the story as an adult while still calling it innocent
  • Paul C Duggan: offers an unsourced colostomy-bag trivia claim linked to Napoleon's hand

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Humour in Wolfe

1998-04-30 Robert King #

Jokingly suggests that Weer's theory about Napoleon's hidden hand in portraits is that he was fondling himself under his clothes. Adds a Seinfeld quip and notes the hand would need to sit much lower than portraits show, then jokes that boys would relish such an explanation to offend older relatives.
chats · theories: Weer's theory: Napoleon fondles himself

Maybe it's my sick mind at work, but I always surmised that Weer's theory held that Napoleon was surreptitiously, uh, fondling himself. (In Seinfeldian terms, the Emperor might have ruled much of Europe but he was definitely not master of his own domain.) Although I suppose the hand would have to be much lower than portraits usually place it.

In any case, that's the type of explanation a certain type of boy at a certain age would take great delight in recounting, especially to older relatives. And it would invariably offend.

-- Bob

1998-05-01 William H. Ansley #

Argues that the Napoleon anecdote about his hand in his coat cannot be the innocent remark described in Peace, because the narrator says the innocent remark offended people. Quotes the opening page of Peace to support this, then adds a remark praising Wolfe's long involuted sentences.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: the Napoleon story is not the innocent remark

Again, I don't think this fits with the fact that this is supposed to be an innocent remark.

"At first I used to tell it in the hope of satisfying curiosity (and so obtaining those real though impalpable satifactions, sweet at any time, but sweetest at thirteen, which accrue when we appear knowledgeable and thus, at least by implication, effective). Later I continued it as a psychological experiment, having observed that the innocent remark invariably offended."

_Peace_, p. 2

BTW, don't you just love the long involuted sentences that abound in _Peace_?

William Ansley

1998-05-01 William H. Ansley #

Argues that the gesture Weer's theory attributes to Napoleon, a hand in the waistcoat, is physically awkward, and that Weer or Wolfe would not have meant the remark as innocent. Responds to Bob (Robert King)'s quoted claim that Napoleon was fondling himself, adding a physical objection to the reading and a judgment about the remark's intent.
disputes · close-textual-reading

Aside from difficulties associated with how Nappy could do this with his hand in his waistcoat (which ends at the waist, at least in front), it seems unlikely to me that Weer (or Wolfe) would consider this an innocent remark.

William Ansley

1998-05-02 Robert King #

Argues that the schoolboy Weer's remark about Napoleon's hand in his waistcoat was probably innocent, since children often make innocent remarks that offend adults. Responds to William H. Ansley's view that the remark was not innocent by questioning whether it would really offend everyone and praising Wolfe's withholding of what Napoleon was doing.
disputes · close-textual-reading

I agree with you on the physical difficulties, and maybe the colostomy bag is more realistic. But I think it's perfectly common for kids (okay, maybe 13 is a little old, but then again these were supposedly more innocent days, weren't they?) to think of their questions or remarks as innocuous and then find that they've offended more-knowing adults. As in, "Hey mommy, what does **** mean?"

To "invariably offend," the remark would have to somewhat salacious, wouldn't it? How likely is it everybody would be offended by young Weer demystifying Napoleon's pose?

Don't you think it's wonderful that by not telling us what Napoleon was supposedly doing, Wolfe has managed to keep us talking about it 23 years later?

-- Bob

1998-05-04 William H. Ansley #

Argues that Weer in Peace kept repeating the Napoleon's hand story as an adult while still considering the remark innocent, citing a later exchange with Louis Gold where Weer says he tells the story rarely. Grounds this reading in the passage on page 2 about the innocent remark that invariably offended, and reasons that Wolfe likely has a specific remark in mind.
proposes · close-textual-reading · theories: Weer repeated the Napoleon story as psychological experiment

This is possible, but my interpretation of the passage in question, which I have
appended to the end of this message, is that Weer continued to repeat the story ("as a psychological experiment") into his adult years but still felt the remark to be innocent. There is some mild support for this later on. When Weer is talking to Louis Gold in his study, after Sherry Gold has persuaded Weer not to tell anyone about about her father's forged books, on p. 226 of the Orb edition, he says:

"I'm afraid I only know the story of Bonaparte's hand, and I don't tell it very often anymore."

It certainly is hard to think of a remark that meets these two criteria. If the author weren't Wolfe, I might think that he didn't have any real remark about Napoleon's hand in mind and was just being annoyingly mysterious. But, since it is Wolfe, I tend to think that (in addition to being annoyingly mysterious) he does have a remark in mind that would make us all say "Oh, of course!"

It certainly is wonderful, but the time of wonders must end. 8-> I think I have beat this thing to death and beyond, so I'll (probably) stop here.

Later I continued it as a psychological experiment, having observed that the innocent remark invariably offended."

_Peace_, p. 2
