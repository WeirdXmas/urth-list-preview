Argues that Catholic theology begins by assuming humans cannot know the mind of God, so speculative theories about divine purpose stay uncertain, and that Wolfe, like Tolkien, builds hypothetical universes that do not violate Christian theology. Well, yes and no ... among other things, this depends on just what Christian theology you're talking about. Catholic theology has, to the best of my knowledge, always begun with with the vital assumption that we do not _and can not_ know the mind of God (Dorothy Sayer's excellent book _The Mind of the Maker_ all to the good). We can attempt to (for example) know why God made the world, but that's speculative theology* and not only are its hypotheses considered uncertain, it's generally considered dangerous to think of them as if they were certain.

* A term which should ought to be familiar to those who participated in the great Pullman Debate of last year.

I would say that Wolfe (like Tolkien before him) has generally gone out of his way to create a hypothetical universe which, while it may not be explicitly Christian, does not actually _violate_ Christian theology in any irreconcilable way.

--Blattid
