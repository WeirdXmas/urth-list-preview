fifth head owlet- wolf

Urth list · 6 messages · 2013-03-25 to 2013-03-28

2013urthJerry FriedmanDavid StockhoffMarc Aramini
The thread: The thread asked what the owlet and wolf imagery in the opening quotation of The Fifth Head of Cerberus means, and whether it links to the pan pipes, to wolves eating their young, and to the Hillmen's moving sleeping place. Aramini proposed links to the pan pipes, the numbered wolves, and Sandwalker's offerings in 'A Story'. Friedman argued for a Christian reading through the hermit, with David rather than the clones as the she-wolf's young, citing the OED and Coleridge. Stockhoff found no evidence that owls prey on wolves or that wolves eat their young, but offered Pan as master of wolves and Victorian beast-lore. The thread never reached agreement and drifted toward Aramini's sleeping-place reading.

2013-03-25 Marc Aramini #

Asks whether the owlet's whooping to a wolf in the opening quote of The Fifth Head of Cerberus links to the pan pipes scene and to the owl-like maitre in The Visitors... no, in V.R.T., and whether the wolf eats its mother's young. Cites the forty-seven pipes, the prisoner forty-seven tapping messages to V.R.T., and the wolf-eating-wolf pattern among the numbered wolfes.
questions · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Owlet's whooping foreshadows wolf-five killing his father

The opening quote, about the ivy tod being heavy with snow and the owlet whooping to a wolf below that eats the she wolf's young, has always seemed a bit ambiguous. The opening scene involves David making noises with the pan pipes. Is this like the owl whooping to the wolfe, number five, who will one day kill another wolfe as his own father has consumed his free life and taken the life of his originator? The only other mention of an owl is when maitre in VRT is called an owl. There are forty seven pan pipes and later the prisoner forty seven taps on the pipes to communicate with VRT. He is a political prisoner.

Does the wolf eat the young of its mother in the quote? (Number four and five et al continually consuming each other- they are wolfes) the quote is certainly not referring to the owl as the consumer of wolves, right?

2013-03-26 David Stockhoff replying to Marc Aramini #

Reports that the poster finds no evidence that owls prey on wolves or that wolves eat their young, and cites a medieval bestiary entry linking she-wolves to prostitutes. Also notes that Pan, the piper, is master of wolves and werewolves, offering a possible link to the pan pipes in the opening scene that Aramini raised.
informs · source-and-allusion

I have no idea, but I'm pretty sure owls don't eat wolves, even wolf pups (though I suppose they could). I did a cursory search and didn't find any discussion of wolves eating their or other wolves' young. But I did find the following in the Medieval Bestiary (http://bestiary.ca):

Prostitutes are called she-wolves (/lupae/) because they lay waste their lover's riches.

Huh. Tuck that away somewhere . . .

Also, Pan (of the pipes) is the master of wolves/werewolves.

2013-03-27 David Stockhoff replying to Jerry Friedman #

Points to a passage in a Victorian beast-lore book describing the owl and wolf as partners in crime, and to the owlet-and-wolf image from Coleridge, then asks whether the old wolf-cannibalism lore fits the wolf-and-bear mystery. Responds to Jerry Friedman's post linking the book and his comments on Coleridge's owlet and Wolfe's clones and the she-wolf's young.

2013-03-28 Marc Aramini replying to David Stockhoff #

Argues that the owl-mouse imagery in the opening of "A Story" in The Fifth Head of Cerberus links to Sandwalker eating the owl-mouse's young and to the Hillmen's sleeping place that moves and takes their sacrifices. Grounds the claim in the scene where Sandwalker leaves little bodies at night that are gone by morning, extending David Stockhoff's earlier point about wolves eating their young.
proposes · close-textual-reading · myth-and-structure · theories: Owl-mouse imagery links Sandwalker's eating of young to Hillmen sacrifice

One more mention at least tangential to owl- at the start of "a Story" Sandwalker hunts the owl mouse and eats its young like shrimp and chews the seeds it has stored there, but more telling in this section is the concept that the sleeping place of the Hillmen moves and hides itself if it is not satisfied with the sacrifice a Hillman brings - thus Sandwalker leaves little bodies at night and sometimes they are gone in the morning. At least in the story, the sleeping place is essential to their long survival and can shun them or occasionally do something with their sacrifices to make it "gone" in the morning.

Proposes that the phrase 'land of shifting stones' in The Fifth Head of Cerberus refers to the Hillmen's sleeping place, which moves and hides itself when a Hillman's offering is unsatisfactory. Builds on the poster's earlier quotation about Sandwalker leaving offerings in 'A Story', noting the passage's statement that the sleeping place vanishes on the third day and dooms the unsuccessful hunter.
