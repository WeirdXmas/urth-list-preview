Base 9 on the Whorl?

Urth list · 11 messages · 2002-05-11 to 2002-05-15

2002urthThe Book of the Long SunKevin J. MaroneyRobert BorskiJerry FriedmanAndy RobertsonMichael Straight
The thread: Robert Borski asked whether the Whorl in Wolfe's Long Sun books uses a base 9 numbering system, citing Maytera Marble's arithmetic lesson, and then argued that the children's sums are not errors but evidence of base 9. Kevin J. Maroney disputed this, arguing children err in any base and that the base-9 reading needs an unlikely, patterned mistake with no support. Michael Straight wistfully called the idea a fitting Wolfean easter egg, and Borski extended it with a claim drawn from "The Night Chough" about windows and storeys. Jerry Friedman then offered a window-pane counting scheme that makes base 9 workable, and the thread ended with the idea still disputed rather than settled.
  • Robert Borski: the Whorl likely uses base 9, so the children's sums are signs of that system
  • Kevin J. Maroney: children simply make mistakes in any base, and the base-9 reading lacks evidence
  • Michael Straight: base 9 is refuted but would be appealing as a hidden Wolfean pattern
  • Jerry Friedman: lights may be panes of one window, and a base-9 scheme with nR=2n fits the count

Themes: Books, libraries and writing, The dying sun and the cosmos

2002-05-11 Robert Borski #

Asks whether the Whorl uses a base 9 numbering system, citing Maytera Marble's lesson in Nightside the Long Sun where children subtract and add to get nine and twenty-three. Raises whether the numbering drops a digit or omits zero, and asks what a base 9 system would change about ages, the year and distances between Blue and Green.
questions · chronology-and-plot-logic · close-textual-reading · theories: the Whorl uses base 9 numbering; the Whorl numbering omits zero like Romans

In _Nightside the Long Sun_, on p. 29, Wolfe has Maytera Marble presiding over a mathematics lesson, "watching the children take nineteen from twenty-nine and get nine, add seven and seventeen and get twenty-three." This, however, is only possible in a base 9 numbering system, and a strange one at that, since a conventional base 9 system would only include the digits 0 to 8 (there should thus no 9, 19, or 29).

The following questions therefore ensue:

Since the number 9 is present, are we to assume that one of the other numbers has been retired? (If so, it's not evidenced in the Seal of Pas, which contains all ten digits from 0 - 9. But could these sequences be artifacts of the pre-launch numbering system?)

Equally possible, I suppose, is the notion that the Whorl, like the ancient Romans, uses no 0 --meaning that the sequence after the first and second enneads runs ...8, 9, 11, 12... and 18, 19, 21, 22. But can this be supported by text citings: i.e., do the numbers 10, 20, 30, etc., occur anywhere in the narrative?

Also, if we accept the validity of the base 9 system (doubtless enacted by the Whorl gods to reinforce their sanctity, just as Pas's children have co-opted the days of the week), how does this change the facts, figures and chronology of the Long and Short Sun books--from ages of the characters to lengths of the year--or even, say, the hypothetical distance between Blue and Green at apogee and perigee?

Robert Borski

2002-05-12 Robert Borski #

Argues that the Whorl may use base 9 arithmetic, so that the children's sums in Wolfe's text are not childish errors but a sign of that numbering system. Grounds this in the difficulty of adding 7 + 17 in base 9 compared with the easy base 10 subtraction 29 - 19, and in a jokingly religious rule about nines tied to Pas and Echidna.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Whorl uses base 9 arithmetic; Children's sums are base 9 not errors

hartshorn having attempted to throttle my base 9 theory thusly:

"The children are in their childish way making mistakes."

"By the way 7 + 17 = 25 in base 9"

Yes. But if indeed the children are making mistakes, they are making the less likely one. 29 - 19 in base 10 is almost impossible to get wrong--a like number minus another like number has to be zero--it's about the easiest calculation you can make and among the first we learn as children--especially since we have ten fingers and ten toes to help abacus our solution. But a calculation in base 9 of 7 + 17 is much more difficult, even for adults; you need to figure out (or memorize by rote) that the number 17 only contains 16 integers (and yes I know in base 9 the number 17 contains by definition 17 integers, but you know what I mean), add the 7 + 16 and get 23, then readjust to compensate out of base 10 (as I did not) for the missing 10 and 20 in the final tallying. Plus as in base 10 there may be special "rules" of 9 (e.g., any large number whose individual numbers add up to nine or a number that can be reduced to nine is divisible by nine), only in this case the rule would take on additional religious significance, something akin to "In this column here, children, like minus like always equals nine--because as we know the Nine can never truly be reduced. Bless Pas, Echidna, and their children."

I'll also bet that if you ask first graders to subtract 19 from 29, they will almost always get it right;
whereas if you ask most adults to add 7 + 17 in base 9, they will frequently get it wrong.

In other words I stand by my original premise: is it possible base 9 is being used on the Whorl?

Robert Borski

2002-05-12 Kevin J. Maroney #

Disputes Robert's claim that the children's calculations in the Whorl's number puzzle are more likely mistakes in base 9 than in another base. Argues that the children err in any base, and that Robert's view requires an unlikely, precisely patterned error with no supporting evidence.
disputes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: The Whorl uses base 9 numbering

They are, in fact, making mistakes no matter what base the calculations are in. You are asking us to assume that they're getting some of it correct and some of it wrong in one unlikely and terrifically precise way to prove a point for which there is no other support.

2002-05-14 Robert Borski #

Proposes that Wolfe's base-nine numbering is supported by a passage in the story "The Night Chough" from THE CROW anthology, where a window is either the twenty-second or forty-third. Argues that if buried floors hold windows 1-21 and then 22-42, the numbers line up with stacked storeys, using the storm-flooded town described in the story as evidence.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Base nine numbering woven through Wolfe's books; Window numbers in The Night Chough reflect buried storeys

Rostrum writing:

<I think this has been pretty well refuted, but oh, how I wish it were true.

Me too. (Sniff.)

< I really believed it for a minute. It seemed like such a Wolfean idea, him being an engineer and all, and the idea of it being subtly woven into all the books as an easter egg is fabulous, and it seems like the sort of thing Pas would do.

When I first posted my base 9 supposition, I couldn't remember the other passage which I thought supported my theory. Several days later I have. It's in THE CROW anthology, Wolfe's story being "The Night Chough."

In an unnamed town on Blue (though the names are Vironese), and after a violent thunderstorm (apparently spawned by the looming conjunction with Green), the young man known as Starling, accompanied by Oreb, is searching for the killers of his girlfriend Lily. The following passage then occurs (p. 68):

"Bad man. Go where? Bad man!"
"There's one," the young man whispered, pointing. Through the twenty-second window--or perhaps it was the forty-third--could be seen three men and a woman seated around a rough table on which stood three dark bottles and five cheap wineglasses among scattered playing cards.

Now what struck me as odd here was the either/or specificity of these numbers. How could a window be either the 22 or the 43? But there has been a violent storm, with "solid sheets of pounding silver rain that turned the streets to oozing mire" and the town has earlier been described as having "raw, new streets and hastily erected houses," indicating that the area may have been hit hard by previous storms. Is it possible the building in question has been buried in a mud slide and that it had multiple stories? And if my base nine theory is true and the first level was buried (windows 1-21), then directly above window 1 in the second level would be window 22, while if the second level was also buried (Starling not being able to tell how many stories the building actually has), directly above window 22 would be window 43. At least I think that's how the various windows might line up, with L1 = W1-21, L2 = W22-42, L3 = 43-63. Doubtless I've bolixed up the math again and hartshorn will correct me, or someone else will suggest a much more practical reason for why Wolfe chose these specific two numbers. But I thought at least I'd mention it now.

Robert Borski

2002-05-15 Jerry Friedman #

Proposes that Starling's count of lights on the Whorl could be read as a base-9 system, suggesting that adjacent lights may be panes of a single window rather than separate windows. Presents a numbering scheme where each window's left and right panes map onto consecutive values, showing that nR equals 2n, and answers Robert Borski's doubt that the base 9 idea has been refuted.
proposes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Starling counts lights in base 9; Adjacent lights are panes of one window

...

One possibility is that Starling can't tell whether adjacent lights are nearby windows or two panes of a single window. So if 1L represents the left pane of window 1, here are the two possible numbering schemes.

1L 1R 2L 2R 3L 3R 4L 4R ... 22L 22R
1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 ... 43 44

To convince yourself that I didn't get out of alignment in my ellipses, note that "nR" is the same as "2n".

Jerry Friedman
