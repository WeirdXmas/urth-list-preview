The thread: Readers in February 2001 argued over how to read Horn/Silk's history in Wolfe's Long Sun and Short Sun books. BMeyer7 proposed that Silk killed his wife Hyacinth, citing a quoted passage from In Green's Jungles and a discrepancy with Return to the Whorl. Alex David Groce countered that the death was Horn's own deed, recollected by Silk, and that Horn grew morally better; Spectacled Bear rejected a staged-suicide reading, arguing Horn's wounds suggest a frenzy of grief instead. Separately, akt and Kieran Cleary disputed the godling's status and whether Pig's Irish-inflected dialect was researched. The thread ended in disagreement with no consensus reached.
  • BMeyer7: Silk killed his wife Hyacinth and suppressed the memory, given the bloody knife and amnesia
  • Alex David Groce: Horn did the killing, not Silk, and Horn grew morally better before becoming Silk
  • Spectacled Bear: Horn wounded himself in grief, not in suicide, since his wounds rule out a staged sequence
  • akt: the godling is a debased hierodule/Juturna figure and Silk is a holy one, not a Hollywood effect
  • Kieran Cleary: the godling is cover-art spectacle and Pig's dialect was a joking question about Wolfe's sources

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Doubles, twins and clones

2001-02-14 BMeyer7 #

Proposes that Silk killed his wife Hyacinth, arguing that Horn's account of waking beside her body with a bloody knife, amid signs of a struggle, points to murder and that Silk's later amnesia and guilt follow from it. Grounds the theory in a quoted passage from In Green's Jungles and in the discrepancy with Return to the Whorl, where only Horn's arm injuries are mentioned.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Silk killed Hyacinth

<< Woo-hoo! I finally found the passage I was looking for in IGJ.

What seems to have thrown many of us off in RTTW is that only Horn/Silk's arm injuries are mentioned when the farmer's wife attempts to clean him up, whereas this is how Horn describes the situation upon waking up after transfusion into Silk:

"[I] found myself upon my knees besides the open coffin of a middle-aged woman. My hands and arms and face and neck were all bleeding, and an old, worn knife covered with blood was by my hand. There was no one else in the poor little house in which I knelt, and almost nothing in it that was not torn or broken." (p. 127)

Forgive me if this has previously been discussed, but I'm reading all kinds of theories about how Silk and Hyacinth ended up this way--suicide, a bomb, an attack. Has anyone thought about the following? Silk's wife is dead, he's beside the body covered in blood holding a bloody knife, nobody else is there, there are signs of a struggle. He then suppresses all memory of the events, including his own identity and wanders the world(s) trying to do good deeds and talking about what a bad, unworthy person he is (and committing a rape along the way for no apparent reason). Did Silk kill Hyacinth!? I know it's painful to think of our beloved Silk, but it certainly seems to merit discussion as at least a strong possibility.

2001-02-14 Alex David Groce #

Argues that the events leading to Horn's death are Horn's actions, recollected by Silk/Horn, so Silk himself did not commit the rape, and that Horn grew morally better before becoming Silk. Responds to Alex David Groce's theory that Silk killed his wife and suppressed memory, by pointing to Horn's adoption of Krait and the killing of Jahlee as shared guilt.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk does not rape anyone; Horn committed the acts; Horn becomes a better man before becoming Silk

Well one thing that is important to keep in mind is that the events up to the transfer from Green at Horn's death are actions carried out by _Horn_, recollected by Silk/Horn. Silk doesn't rape anyone. This goes the other way around, too--before he becomes Silk, Horn has become a better man himself, and though it is Silk who adopts Jahlee (an easier task, I think, in some ways) it is Horn who adopts Krait-- despite (or because of) the fact that Krait is bitter gall to him, and a constant reminder of the worst event of his life*.

* Worst, that is, until he kills Jahlee, but that is Silk and Horn both, I think.

2001-02-14 Kieran Cleary #

Asks how Wolfe researched the Irish-inflected dialect of the Pig character in The Book of the Long Sun, joking that he may have watched Scottish comedy reruns. Replies to a digest message complaining that the Long Sun fliers' speech was unlike a Scottish or Irish dialect, noting the fliers are Crew and so were spared similar treatment.
questions · genre-and-craft

Yes, I've moved across the water. Thankfully, the fliers being Crew, we were spared any similar reproduction of an Irish dialect. I wonder, though, how Wolfe did his homework for Pig's. (BBC viewers may be forgiven for suspecting he's been watching re-runs of Rab C. Nesbitt).

Kieran

2001-02-14 Peter Westlake #

Argues that Horn's bleeding hands, arms and face, and the knife he still holds, rule out a sequence in which he bought the coffin and had the woman laid in it before cleaning up. Proposes instead that he wounded himself in a frenzy of grief, not in an attempt at suicide.
disputes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Horn wounded himself in grief frenzy, not suicide

...

So he's been to the undertaker in the nearest town, bought the coffin and he or they have put her into it, and his hands and arms and face are still bleeding? I don't think so. He's still holding the knife!

My personal theory is that he wasn't trying to kill himself, either, but did all the damage in a frenzy of grief. Rending his garments and all that sort of thing.

Spectacled Bear.
