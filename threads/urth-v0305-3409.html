The thread: Nigel Price read Wolfe's story "The Tree is My Hat" as a Christian allegory, weighing whether Baden's mystery illness is sexually transmitted, contrasting Baden's materialism with Rob's spiritual mission, and reading the tree as a cross and Hanga's grave marker. The thread then drifted into a factual sidetrack about arsenic in medicine, where Josh Geller and mournings glory corrected each other's dates for the syphilis treatment (c. 1900 versus Salvarsan in 1909) and mournings glory added a 1940 film about Ehrlich. No one connected the historical detail back to the story, so the thread ended without resolving the allegorical reading.
  • Nigel Price: Tree Hat is a Christian allegory, with the tree as cross and Eden echo and Hanga's grave marked by the tree
  • Josh Geller: arsenic was a standard syphilis treatment credited to Paul Ehrlich until penicillin
  • mournings glory: Salvarsan dates to 1909, not c. 1900, and a 1940 Edward G. Robinson film covers Ehrlich

Themes: Christ figures and messiahs, Catholic faith and sacraments, Suffering and sacrifice

2003-05-12 Josh Geller #

Informs the list that arsenic was used to treat syphilis, crediting Paul Ehrlich with the discovery around 1900 and noting the treatment stayed standard until penicillin. Answers Nigel Price's question about arsenic's medicinal uses with a historical fact and no reference to Wolfe's fiction.
answers

The use of arsenic to cure syphillis was discovered by Dr. Paul Ehrlich I think around 1900 or maybe a little before. The treatment remained the standard treatment until the invention of penicillin.

J.

2003-05-12 Nigel Price #

Responds to earlier posts on Wolfe's story "The Tree is My Hat" by weighing whether Baden's mystery ailment is sexually transmitted and contrasting Baden's materialist development with Rob's spiritual missionary work. Links the tree to the cross and the Garden of Eden, arguing Hanga's buried spirit is marked by the tree as his grave.
disputes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Baden's paradise isle is a fallen Eden; The tree marks Hanga's grave; The tree signifies the cross

Thanks to Mantis, Patera Nutria and Robert Borski for their helpful contributions to the analysis of "The Tree is My Hat".

I was going to comment on their comments in detail, but a lot of it boiled down to "Yes, I agree!" and "Oh, that's interesting!", which was amicable but not particularly scholarly or insightful on my part. Instead, then, I'll broadly agree with them, thank them here and comment on just one or two details.

(And still go, "Yes, you're right!" occasionally.)

Good stuff from Nutria on the names Mark and Adam, but I'm still puzzled as to the significane of Mary having twins.

Good spot - yes!

Maybe it's because Rob gets off his metaphorical hill of western affluence and authority and comes down to work with the people by the sea. He has turned his back on ruling and become a servant. Note that Rob welcomes Baden's can of coke from the fridge as a great western luxury. He himself seems to have access to neither coka cola nor refrigerators.

OK, I'll buy that. Maybe it's a reminder too that magic and spiritual evil aren't restricted to the South Seas. They exist back in America as well.

On the subject of the UFO which Baden sees, Nutria comments...

This has to be one possibility. If it's right, we can go back to seeing the Polynesians as being the descendants of an ancient Andean ruling class. Over against this, though, is all the talk about the stars being like islands. Your comment on the SF dimension feeling wrong is the very thing I was getting at when I suggested that Wolfe might be "overegging the pudding", as the expression goes. I don't think that we can completely rule out the science fictional element, though. It's still there, at least as one of the story's explanations of what is going on. Whether its significance is ultimately literal or metaphorical is hard to define.

Agree entirely that "Baden" = "Bad'un". So far I have managed to resist the temptation to make any reference to the founder of the scouting movement, running round in shorts on Brownsea Island in Poole Harbour...No, I don't even want to think about it...

I agree with Robert Borski that it would be thematically convenient if Baden's mystery ailment were some sort of sexually transmitted disease.

The trouble is, while is all eminently possible, there just doesn't seem to be enough evidence in the text to support this interpretation in any conclusive fashion. My instinct is that if Wolfe wanted us to think that Baden's disease were sexually transmitted, there would be a reference somewhere in the story to the risk of his passing it on either to his wife Mary or to his new partner Langi. But there's nothing remotely about that - in the audio version, anyway.

(In Robert's original message, it was the sentence "Which cures it." which was in italics.)

Does anyone know about the medicinal uses of arsenic?

Reading the quotation in cold print makes me realise how patronising and dismissive Baden is about the very people he is supposed to have come to help. Nutria has already mentioned the contrast between Baden's apparent inactivity and Rob's industry. They also represent two different streams of "development" for the island: Baden's is materialist and secular and Rob's is spiritual and, apparently, medicinal. Of the two, the sensitive missionary comes out better in this story than the federal development man.

I seem to remember that in one of his interviews, possibly the one in _Across the Wounded Galaxy_, Wolfe spoke approvingly of a friend of his who was going off (to Africa, I think) to be a Christian missionary.

Yes! Baden does indeed go bathing in the shark-infested bay.

Robert refers to the story's titular tree being a possible reference to the cross, while Mantis mentions a possible link with the Tree of Knowledge in the Garden of Eden. Both seem likely. I'm sure that Baden's paradise isle with a devilish shark is a deliberate, fallen, counterpart to Eden with its cunning snake. I'm sure that the comment "The tree is my hat." is also an indication of Hanga's underground and therefore, traditionally, hellish nature. The Lord may refer to the Earth as his footstool, but Hanga's spirit is dead and under the earth, with the tree as the marker of his grave.

Nigel

Corrects the earlier poster's date for arsenic-based syphilis treatment, saying Salvarsan was discovered in 1909, and adds a 1940 film about Ehrlich starring Edward G. Robinson. Offers this as factual information with no link to Wolfe's books, responding to Nigel Price's question about arsenic's medicinal uses.
informs

Salvarsan (or 606 as it was sometimes called) was discovered in 1909.

A relatively good movie about it (considering the times and the topic) was made in 1940, "Dr. Ehrlich's Magic Bullet," and starred Edward G. Robinson.
