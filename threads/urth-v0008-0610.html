Themes: Christ figures and messiahs

1998-03-11 C R Culver #

Argues that the Christ parallels in the Book of the New Sun are hard to read religiously, since Severian's flooding of the jacal is unchristlike, and proposes that Severian's revival of Miles echoes Christ casting out the Legion demons. Grounds this in the Latin sense of miles as soldier or thousand and in the Pancreator and Increate terms, while calling the reading tentative.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · source-and-allusion · theories: Severian's revival of Miles echoes Christ expelling the Legion demons

Said Peter S:

This section is hard for me to interpret religiously, because Severian does release the floodgates, killing the prisoners in the jacal. That is, IMHO, not in any way Christlike.

Continues Peter:

I notice 'Pancreator' is used historically, in God's role as Creator, and Increate is used in the present, explaining a God who watches, remotely, though.

One interesting thing I did notice is Severian's reviving of Miles. The word Miles is Latin for soldier (actually, it is nominative or accusative plural), but it also can mean "thousand." How many people are in a Roman legion? A thousand, or some variation of it? Thus, Severian was able to revive Jonas, but casting out his demons (that is, his personal demons, or his pain over not having Jolenta), just as Christ, in Luke, drove the demons out of the man who identified himself as Legion. I'm pretty much thinking out loud right now, so don't take this too seriously.

1998-03-12 Peter Stephenson #

Argues that Severian repeatedly appears as a fleeting Christ figure only to fall away from the role, citing the Pilate-like scene where he tortures a soldier and the climax where he kills Typhon/Piaton. Responds to C R Culver's claim that the Thrax episode recalls Christ, using the jacal massacre as counter-evidence and framing such references as mosaic pieces.
disputes · theology-and-religion · allegory-and-typology · theories: Severian's Christ parallels collapse at key moments

Even if this isn't what happens in this particular episode, it's certainly striking how quickly Severian `falls off' in any episode where he is somehow fleetingly put up against Christ... In the first example I hazarded, which looked a bit like Christ before Pilate, the chapter ends with Severian demonstrating his torturing skills on one of the soldiers (at the lochage's suggestion, it should be said). In the last, he ends up by killing Typhon/Piaton (though if he's somehow the Devil in this episode then my weak theology has run out of exegetic steam). These sort of references seem like little pieces in a mosaic, which is a suitably Byzantine image.

(Which reminds me I've been meaning to ask about some possible relationships between Wolfe and Yeats...)

Peter
