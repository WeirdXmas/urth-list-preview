_Dhalgren_: Scathing Towards Women? and a few ?'s about Sev

Urth list · 6 messages · 2006-10-22 to 2006-10-23

2006urthThe Book of the New SunAllan AndersonBob MillerJames CrossleyDan'l Danehy-OakesJack Redelfs
The thread: Jack Redelfs opened by asking whether Dhalgren is scathingly hateful toward women and, separately, what made Severian's Claw healings work and whether drowning in the Gyoll made him an aquastor. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that healing depends on the person healed and that The Urth of the New Sun defines the power's source. James Crossley preferred the four-volume tetralogy without Urth, citing Wolfe's account of writing it and genre audiences' demand for explanations, and Allan Anderson agreed. Dan'l and Bob Miller then defended Urth as worth reading for explaining hidden matters while raising new paradoxes. The thread ended without resolution, drifting from the Dhalgren question into a split over whether Urth should be counted.
  • Jack Redelfs: asks about Dhalgren's views of women and suggests the aquastor theory explains the Claw's power
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes: healing depends on the person healed; Urth defines the power's origin and is worth reading
  • James Crossley: the tetralogy is more satisfying without Urth's explanations
  • Allan Anderson: agrees with Crossley and plans to reread Urth
  • Bob Miller: Urth is worth loving for different reasons and raises new paradoxes

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Books, libraries and writing, Time loops and causality

2006-10-22 Jack Redelfs #

Asks whether Dhalgren is scathingly hateful toward women and, in reply to Dan'l, asks what determined Severian's success with the Claw and whether his drowning in the Gyoll made him an aquastor. Cites the Triskele healing at the end of Citadel and Silk's moralizing in Long Sun, and says the aquastor theory may explain the Claw's power.
questions · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian drowned in the Gyoll and became an aquastor; Claw healing power came from Severian being an aquastor

Dan'l, I read _Dhalgren_ just a couple of years ago, and I seem to recall that all or most of the female characters were objectified, scorned and degraded in one way or another.
I don't remember any outright rape, but that's not requisite for a hateful attitude towards women.

_Dhalgren_ is supposedly famous for being easy to start, tough to finish, and I know I almost quit a couple of times. I don't know if I've ever read a long novel that was so inconsistent. He wrote the first chapter in an impressive stream-of-consciousness style, only to abandon it entirely (except for a brief encore at the end), unless you count the bizarre monologues. I lost track of how many times he'd introduce a few characters and a new plot, only to abandon everything and write 50 pages of pornography, or 50 pages of rambling semi-coherent monologue. It's a weird book, but not weird-good. I think It may well have been condemned to obscurity if not for the naughty bits. It's probably one of the worst novels I ever completed. A 250 page condensed version would likely be solid, though no _Babel-17_. Maybe "Reader's Digest" could be interested in the undertaking? *grin*

Dan'l:

You're right, of course. His characters do moralize,
as people are wont to do, but I think Wolfe does a
good job of leaving a bit of doubt about who's
acting as the authorial mouthpiece. Sure,
Silk may show a lot of wisdom, but within
definite limits. The mere thought of Hyacinth turns
him into a quivering schoolboy, and his moral center
is defined by devotion to a absurd religion that
worships feuding videogame deities - plus it only
recently quit sacrificing humans, and for the wrong
reason at that.

On an unrelated note, let me throw out a question that
has always intrigued me. Sometimes Severian was able
to heal with the Claw, sometimes not. Did we ever figure out what criteria determined his success or failure?
Toward the close of _Citadel_, in one of
Wolfe's fuzziest Hallmark moments,
Sev realizes that the claw is just a common thorn,
and that "the power was inside me all along!
I healed Triskele!"

Was the power specific to Sev as the Conciliator,
or just general divine (or Hierogrammatic, if you
want to be SFnal) power accessible to any old
body?

Either way, you'd think that afterwards he would heal
people by simply laying his hands on them or
something. Why didn't he ever do this?

I seem to remember an idea that Sev
dying for the first time in _Shadow Of The Torturer_,
drowning in the Gyoll. Is this theory strongly supported by the text or is it just wild conjecture? If it's a good theory, it means Sev was an aquastor throughout. Did "the Claw's" healing power come from the fact that Sev was an aquastor? Is that the SFnal justification?

2006-10-23 James Crossley replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Asks the list whether others find The Book of the New Sun more satisfying without The Urth of the New Sun, preferring the suggestiveness of unexplained endings. Grounds the view in Wolfe's accounts of writing the fifth volume and in the claim that genre audiences, not literary publishers, demanded the explanations.
questions · author-and-biography

Dan'l's question reminded me of something I've been wanting to ask the list at large--who, if anyone, besides me finds BotNS more satisfying without the fifth volume? I prefer the suggestiveness of not knowing all the explanations provided in _Urth_, and judging by Wolfe's accounts of how he came to write that last piece, it seems he would have been content to let the tetralogy stand alone. I think it's only the perceived demands of the genre audience that brought _Urth_ into existence; the publishers of literary fiction are more generally tolerant of open-ended conclusions. Thoughts?

Delurking,
James

2006-10-23 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes replying to James Crossley #

Argues that Urth is worth reading despite first seeming unnecessary, since it explains much the tetralogy leaves suggested but raises paradoxes both within itself and with tBotNS. Responds to James Crossley's dismissal of Urth by describing his own rereading, and floats then rejects the idea that Urth's narrator is not the same Severian who wrote the Book.
disputes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Urth not written by the same Severian

James,

I disliked "Urth" at first, for much the same reasons you suggest. I thought it, well, _unnecessary_.

And perhaps it is unnecessary: but thanks to others on this list (especially Alga, whose absence in recent times I greatly regret), I reread it and was far more impressed. It does explain much that the tetralogy proper leaves only suggested; but it also adds new questions, and indeed seems to introduce some paradoxes, points that are quite hard to resolve both with itself and with _tBotNS_.

One possibility, of course, is that _Urth_ isn't written by "the same" Severian (I will do no more than put it in quotes, so as not to spoil things for those who have not yet read it) who wrote the _Book_, but I find that unsatisfactory on a number of levels...

2006-10-23 Bob Miller replying to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes #

Argues that The Urth of the New Sun is worth loving for different reasons than The Book of the New Sun, since it explains some hidden matters but raises new questions and paradoxes with both itself and the tetralogy. Responds to Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's earlier view of Urth as unnecessary, crediting other list readers for prompting a reread and speculating that Wolfe wrote Urth to get it off his mind.
disputes · close-textual-reading

James,

I disliked "Urth" at first, for much the same reasons you suggest. I thought it, well, _unnecessary_.

And perhaps it is unnecessary: but thanks to others on this list (especially Alga, whose absence in recent times I greatly regret), I reread it and was far more impressed. It does explain much that the tetralogy proper leaves only suggested; but it also adds new questions, and indeed seems to introduce some paradoxes, points that are quite hard to resolve both with itself and with _tBotNS_.

I loved Urth, but for different reasons than I loved BotNS. BotNS was almost incredibly rich without being boring. This is more than very difficult. BotNS was actionpacked and still gave the sense of being a morality fable like Silverlock and <hmmm> Ralph Bakshi's Coonskin? Urth was meeting an old friend I'd thought I'd lost and learning interesting things about him/it that I didn't know before.
Some authors--read all these long series making yawns of the past 2 decades would still be milking this milieu. After all, there's an awful lot there to millk.

I think, whatever Himself says, that Urth forced itself out of his mind in a manner perhaps reminiscent of the birth of Venus, and perhaps for the same reason. To get her off his mind.

Enjoy
