2010urthThe Book of the New SunRyan DunnJames WynnNick Lee
The thread: Readers debated whether critic Peter Wright's atheism or motives should bear on the validity of his critique of religious readings of Wolfe's Sun books, and whether he was really dismissed for being an atheist. Nick Lee argued that a critic's atheism should not taint his reading and that the dismissal claim was overstated, noting Wright's own essay criticizes religious readers on the basis of their religion. James Wynn countered that Wright's critique could be turned back on him as circular, and that weighing a writer's motives is legitimate for understanding a text. Ryan Dunn held that motives should not decide validity and that essays should be judged one at a time on their arguments. The thread ended in disagreement, with no visible consensus.
  • Nick Lee: a critic's atheism should not discredit his reading; the dismissal claim is overstated
  • James Wynn: Wright's critique is circular since his motives may show the bias he attacks; weighing motives is legitimate
  • Ryan Dunn: motives should not decide validity; judge each essay on its arguments

Themes: Gnosticism, heresy and other theologies, Catholic faith and sacraments, Books, libraries and writing

2010-12-02 James Wynn replying to Nick Lee #

Argues that Wright's critique of religious readings of Wolfe can be refuted as circular, since the critic's own motives may exemplify the bias he attacks. Responds to Nick Lee's quotation by calling it a tit-for-tat move and suggesting that a reader may legitimately weigh a writer's motives to understand the text.
disputes · author-and-biography · theology-and-religion · theories: Wright's critique of religious readings is circular

I think the Logician term for that is "tit for tat". And here is an applicable opportunity to address the motives of a writer to understand his text. It would be a valid refutation of Wright's arguments to say that it is circular and that there is a reasonable probability that it exemplifies what he attempts to debunk. That would have been a good argument from the vantage of hindsight.

2010-12-02 Ryan Dunn replying to Nick Lee #

Argues that critics' motives should not decide whether their arguments are valid, responding to the debate over whether Attending Daedalus rejects religious readings of the Sun books because of its author's physicalism. Grounds this in the poster's reading of Wright's essays, saying critics may be assessed on their arguments and readers may agree or disagree with each essay separately.
disputes

I've read most of the essays in Attending Daedalus, and yes, he can criticize whatever he wants, based on whatever he wants, and we as readers can agree with him, disagree with him, or agree with parts of what he criticizes and not others.

I was not getting into the glass houses side of the argument, just the asterisks part of it. And yes, from what I've read through this chain, several people seem to be of the mind that the motivations of a critic or a peer in an argument should be considered as criteria for the validity of their argument. Not even for the argument itself, but whether that person is even qualified to talk about it.

It is possible I have misread. Some of these posts do tend to read like legalese while others are written for laymen to digest.

:)

...ryan
