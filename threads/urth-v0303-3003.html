The thread: A seminar leader on the Urth list reported students reading Wolfe's stories superficially and asked why Wolfe spelled "Werwolf", whether the drug at the end of "Three Fingers" is real, and how to read the ending of "Doctor Island". James Jordan offered Biblical and messianic allusions and suggested Wolfe may twist them. Gareth Jelley posted several times, speculating that the spelling follows German or Germanic roots and asking whether the title story's open questions are meant to be solved or left to linger. Richard Horton answered with an Old English etymology, manwolf, and asked about the extra 'e' in English. The thread stayed on word origins and ended with no firm answer on the spelling or the other questions.
  • James Jordan: admits not knowing the answers, sees possible Biblical allusions and suggests Wolfe may invert them
  • Gareth Jelley: spelling likely follows the German form or Germanic roots, with no firm evidence
  • Richard Horton: the word derives from Old English wer plus wulf, meaning manwolf
  • Fernando Q. Gouvea: reports students reading shallowly and poses the open questions

2003-03-03 Fernando Q. Gouvea #

Reports on the first session of the poster's seminar on Wolfe's stories, finding students read them superficially though a few caught hints that Daw may not be what he seems. Lists three upcoming stories and asks why Wolfe spelled Werwolf, whether the drug at the end of Three Fingers is real, and how to read the ending of Doctor Island.
informs · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Daw may not be what he seems; Daw intentionally caused the empathist's death

Well, I thought I'd report a little bit about the book seminar I'm running. In the first session, we discussed "The Island of Doctor Death and Other Stories", "Alien Stones", and "La Befana". I found it was harder to generate discussion than I expected! Most of the students seemed to have settled for reading the stories at a rather superficial level, and were surprised that I wanted to poke at them further. Still, a few seemed to have captured some of the hints (for example, that Daw may not be what he seems in "Alien Stones", or that he may have intentionally caused the death of the empathist). But overall it seems to have gone reasonably well, though I'm hoping that I'll do better at generating discussion with the next batch of stories.

The next three are "The Hero as Werwolf", "Three Fingers", and "The Death of Doctor Island". There are obvious non-literary issues to pursue: genetic "improvements" for "Hero", the Disney corporation and their brethren for "Fingers", and mental health and utilitarian calculations about who is valuable to society for "Doctor Island". Of course, in each case there are interesting literary questions too. Suggestions for avenues to pursue are welcome!

Some questions of my own:

- Does anyone know why Wolfe chose the spelling "Werwolf"?

- Is the drug mentioned at the end of "Three Fingers" a real drug? If so, what does it do?

- How should we read the ending of "Doctor Island"? Has the boy been helped, or has his personality been destroyed?

Fernando

2003-03-03 Gareth Jelley #

Asks whether an archive exists for a book seminar discussing the title story of The Island of Doctor Death and Other Stories and Alien Stones. Wonders whether Wolfe's unanswered questions in the title story are meant to be deciphered or simply leave readers wondering and remembering.
questions

From: "Fernando Q. Gouvea" <[email]>

Is there an archive on this? I'm currently reading the collection 'The Island of Doctor Death and Other Stories and Other Stories', and have read the title story, and most of 'Alien Stones'. Both have really intrigued me, but the first one particularly, because Wolfe (as one would expect) leaves so many questions unanswered, and leaves the reader asking so many questions...

I always find it difficult /not/ to read into Wolfe, because he is one of these writers who, in his style of story-telling, invites the reader to rummage around between the lines. However, I don't know how much of 'The Island of Doctor Death and Other Stories' I have deciphered, or, in fact, if there is anything to 'decipher'; it may simply be that we are supposed to be left wondering and remembering.

2003-03-04 Richard Horton #

Explains the spelling "Werwolf" by tracing its etymology to Old English "wer" (man) plus "wulf" (wolf), meaning manwolf, and asks why the extra "e" was added. Responds to a earlier poster's question about Wolfe's choice of spelling by offering a word-origin account without citing any text from the books.
answers · source-and-allusion

The etymology, as far as I can tell, is "wer" (man) + "wulf" (wolf), hence manwolf. As such, why add the "e", one might ask?
