The thread: Participants debated whether the strangled woman in the opening coffin is the Catherine Severian meets, and whether Severian's pre-nosh knowledge and youthful naivete can surface given his memoir frame. Jeff Wilson and Jordon Flato argued that Thecla's memories and the old Autarch's voice intrude on the narration, while David Stockhoff and Lee Berman defended a deliberate, memory-based naive narration. Marc Aramini argued that the later works are more solvable than the New Sun. The thread ended in a dispute over Yahoo mail formatting and attribution, with no shared resolution on the Wolfe questions.
  • Lee Berman: strangled woman is the same Catherine, cloned maids extend the count
  • Jeff Wilson: Thecla's memories intrude into Severian's narration
  • Jordon Flato: the raised-in-the-tower Severian cannot fully resurface in the memoir
  • David Stockhoff: the naive narration is deliberate, shaped by Severian's strong memory
  • Marc Aramini: ambiguous passages need explication, later works are more solvable
  • Lee Berman: Thecla is possibly a sibling of Severian

Themes: Unreliable narration, Memory and forgetting, Doubles, twins and clones

2011-01-10 David Stockhoff replying to Jeff Wilson #

Argues that Severian the autarch would judge Severian the youth too naive to rule, and that the narrator's keeping youthful knowledge out of the tale is a deliberate choice in a bildungsroman. Responds to Jordan Flato's point about superlative memory by linking Wolfe's strong memory to the need for a coherent narrative of Severian's youth, with Thecla and the Antechamber cited as examples.
supports · author-and-biography · genre-and-craft · theories: Severian's strong memory enables coherent narration of youth

True. However, Severian the autarch probably considers Severian the youth to be too naive to be allowed to take over, if that were even possible. He may have the memory but he lacks the most basic understanding. And yet the narrator gives us a good deal of background, because Severian the youth does fit well into the bildungsroman (someone called it a conversion narrative and I think that almost fits as well) he is constructing. At very least, there must be a contrast between the early fool and the late magician; in Severian's case it is a complex contrast.

Jordan, you made an excellent point about the narrator trying to keep his narrative straight from Severian's point of view. It's one thing to be king and tell the tale of when you were an apprentice or a scullery maid; it's another to have intimate exterior knowledge of the circumstances of your own youth and keep that knowledge out of the tale. This is commonly done in such narratives---the difference is a matter of degree as well as a qualitative one. (Usually handled with "Well, of course I didn't know that then, silly boy that I was, but the man to whom my father lent his cows was the Duke of Zzzz...") It helps that Severian the boy lived an isolated, almost monastic life.

This needed clarity of narrative may be another reason why Wolfe makes Severian's memory so strong---not only would he be overwhelmed by the voices as autarch if his own memory was weak, but Wolfe would not be able to justify a coherent narrative of Severian's youth at all. Severian has enough trouble himself at points, especially with Thecla (who exists partly to suggest what it's like to be autarch). Through his entire journey, pre- and post-nosh, he encounters situations, like the Antechamber, that he _will already know_. However, enough mystery must remain about some of them (Baldanders, the magicians, Typhon) that he can still recall and almost convincingly relate his original naive experience.

2011-01-10 Lee Berman replying to Lee Berman #

Supports David Stockhoff's point that Severian's strong memory keeps the narration tied to his naive youthful viewpoint, and argues that everyone knows the doubled perception of remembering how they once thought. Notes that Mantis once linked Wolfe's own life to the Book of the New Sun, and says a good author uses readers' experience of stories so the narrator need not interrupt events with hindsight.
supports · author-and-biography · genre-and-craft

I think this is a very good analysis. And it is not only relevant to SF and brain-eating characters. We all have childhood and youthful memories and we all know what it is like to have a doubled perception; how we thought and felt then vs. how we think and feel now.

I'll note that Mantis once posted a nice summary of how BotNS paralleled Gene Wolfe's life. As he wrote this book, he himself was facing the story-telling problems Severian was facing. We have all told stories of our youth so we all know (if we are good story-tellers) that it would ruin the story to constantly, repeatedly interupt the story events with "well, of course now I know that...".

We are not all good story tellers but we are all experienced story readers/listeners. A good author acknowledges and uses that fact.

2011-01-10 Marc Aramini replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that Wolfe's ambiguous passages, such as Kypris's "this woman is a spy" and Silk's "I caught the ball" in On Blue's Waters, need explication, and that the later works are more solvable than the New Sun. Grounds this in comparison with Latro, Peace, the Long Sun and Short Sun, and Severian's treatment of Agia and others, while reflecting on the poster's own changing reading.
proposes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Severian may be a bastard who uses people unacknowledged

I do agree with one point, and that is that Wolfe often does do plain straightforward stuff like the quote above but somehow makes it exceedingly ambiguous, such that it is mentioned in a dream, or trapped in metaphor that seems merely figurative, or in this kind of sensory overload situation where it is not clear who is talking. ie Kypris saying "this woman is a spy" talking about the body she possesses.

At the end of Obw, the narrator says, I caught the ball, I won the game. We know that is Silk, but there is so much "white noise" around it that even straightforward statements like that become infinitely complicated until a mechanism is found to explain them logically. So yes, these kind of ambiguities need explication. They are begging for it.

I don't know if Wolfe became more concrete in his solutions as he got older or my reading style changed, but the child who first read New Sun is still puzzled by the same stuff that puzzled me as a kid, whereas the texts I have come to as an older man, including Latro, seems to me much more solvable to some degree with the slippery symbolic word association Wolfe employs (tree/death in Peace, for example). I think New Sun has some very important elided parts that simply can't be filled in as neatly as the elided portions of Latro (we KNOW why Latro is depressed in Arete - the Spartan's incredible cruelty and the death of his amazon woman, and there seem to be other explanations as to why he can succeed in pushing Sysiphus' boulder to the top when a demigod can't do it, too. We know that one guy is a goat like satyr or faun, the puzzles seem to have solutions.)

Long Sun and Short Sun seemed the same way to me, imminently solvable, with the Plan of Pas dominating much of the conflict in the Whorl and the mystical trees controlling much in the Short Sun, but I have not been able to find the satisfying kinds of statements like "kypris who was mother was was Hyacinth" out in the open in New Sun. Instead I have this vague fear Severian is a bastard not from his own descriptions but in the way he uses people and does not seem to acknowledge it. Sometimes I think he only spares Agia because of some presentiment that she will save him later.

I would be more likely to say Wolfe thrives on that purposeful inexplicable Heisenberg uncertainty in his earlier fiction like 5hoc and New Sun but his later stuff seems to me more like Peace, very very very clever obfuscations. There is a reason Weer our narrator turns away from certain facts, but we can read between the line and reach his own refusal to explore the evil he has done in a life that is for all intents and purposes long over.

Proposes that a memory passage in which Severian and Thecla merge, with Thecla playing by the fallen curtain wall, hints that Thecla is a family member of Severian, possibly a sibling. "I played again with pebbles in the courtyard beside the fallen curtain wall, as Thecla dodged the hooves of my father's mounted guard."

Jeff Wilson: .."extraordinary" identity problem that results in an acute dislocation that this passage captures very well.

It is a likely interpretation. But I see another possibility with that particular passage. This occurs at the terminus of a sequence of memories which switch back and forth between Severian and Thecla. The final combined memory could be showing us a merging of their identities (though the merge does not seem to take; the identities for the rest of the story remain back and forth switching).

I think there is a possibility that this is a disguised hint regarding identification of a family member of Severian (which I roundly think we are supposed to be doing as we re-read). Perhaps Severian's first memory has been jogged to include the presence of a young Thecla there with him at the Citadel. Severian is loathe to admit any incidences of incest, and he is suspiciously reluctant to admit the truth of his sexual congress with Thecla (it takes him more than 3 books, despite his open revelations of other sexual liaisons).

Roy has argued that a family connection is impossible because Thecla reports her family has a villa in the north. I have argued with Roy that a villa is defined as a secondary residence. So where was her primary residence? So it goes. I still find it possible. (and who is "the Castellan" of the Citadel? Why is he lame? Does he have a mounted guard?)

I have further wondered about the ancestry of Thecla based on Severian's dream after his wounding at Orithya. As Thecla he dreams of her father as a weird, mythological bull figure. I have some guesses about this but either way I am pretty sure there is more to Thecla and her ancestry than is generally acknowledged. Since there is a constellational aspect to this dream, I wonder if James might have any input here.
