The thread: Joseph Herbert identified the spiny orange of Able's bow in The Fifth Head of Cerberus as Osage orange, pasting a Wikipedia entry on the tree and noting Wolfe owns a walking stick of that wood. The thread then drifted into a botany exchange between Jerry Friedman and brunians about dioecious plants and whether ancient cultivars were mostly dioecious. brunians conceded Friedman's counterexamples and offered cannabis and osage orange as ancient dioecious cultivars, while Friedman listed crops with bisexual versus separate-sex flowers. The exchange ended amicably with no return to the novel's text.
  • Joseph Herbert: the spiny orange in Fifth Head of Cerberus is Osage orange
  • Jerry Friedman: willows and many crops show dioecious plants are not mostly ancient cultivars
  • brunians: concedes the point but holds ancient cultivars include dioecious ones like cannabis and osage orange

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Deep time and ruins, Evolution and the future of humanity

2009-11-03 Herbert, Joseph #

Identifies the spiny orange of Able's bow in The Fifth Head of Cerberus as Osage orange, and notes Wolfe owns a walking stick of that wood carved by Joe Mayhew. Supports this by pasting a Wikipedia entry on Maclura pomifera describing the fruit, its latex, its use for bows, and its colloquial names.
informs · source-and-allusion

The spiny orange of Able's bow. Wolfe has a hand carved walking stick of osage orange - carved by Joe Mayhew.
Maclura pomifera
Osage-orange, Horse-apple or Bois D'Arc even know as the Drewedic Bullochus (Maclura pomifera) is dioeceous </wiki/Plant_sexuality> plant species, with male and female flowers </wiki/Flower> on different plants. It is a small deciduous </wiki/Deciduous> tree </wiki/Tree> or large shrub </wiki/Shrub> , typically growing to 8-15 metres (26-49 ft) tall. The fruit </wiki/Fruit> , a multiple fruit </wiki/Multiple_fruit> , is roughly spherical, but bumpy, and 7-15 cm in diameter, and it is filled with a sticky white latex </wiki/Latex> sap </wiki/Sap> . In fall, its color turns a bright yellow-green and it has a faint odor similar to that of oranges </wiki/Orange_(fruit)> .[1] <> The Osage-orange is commonly used as a tree row windbreak </wiki/Windbreak> in prairie states, which gives it one of its colloquial names, "hedge apple".
The trees acquired the name bois d'arc, or "bow-wood", from early French </wiki/France> settlers who observed the wood being used for war clubs and bow-making by Native Americans
</wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States> .[3] <> Meriwether Lewis was told that the people of the Osage Nation </wiki/Osage_Nation> "esteem the wood of this tree for the making of their bows, that they travel many hundred miles in quest of it." Many modern bowyers assert the wood of the Osage Orange is superior even to English Yew for this purpose, though this opinion is by no means unanimous. The trees are also known as "bordarch" trees, most likely originating from a corruption of "bois d'arc."

Concedes that Jerry Friedman's point about dioecious plants is news to him and modifies his earlier statement accordingly. Offers cannabis and osage orange as examples of ancient cultivated plants with separate male and female stems, and says his deep-history views match Gene Wolfe's, though he doesn't know whether Wolfe agrees.
supports

I didn't know that. I am actually a very ignorant person.

I would have to modify my statement in light of that knowledge. I have no doubt that you are correct when you say this,

Some plants that have male and female flowers on seperate stems seem to be ancient cultivers. These plants tend to be useful for food, drugs, as material for making tools etc. Good examples include cannabis and osage orange.

I have to say that while this makes sense to me, I don't expect it to make sense to everybody. I have some ideas about history and deep history that are seriously at variance with the versions generally accepted these days, though (coincidentally or not) they are basically the same as Gene Wolfe's views on the same subjects. I do not know whether he agrees with my opinion expressed above: I hope to ask him some time. I suspect that he does or would.

Concedes a point about ancient cultivated plants and lists crops with bisexual flowers (legumes, cereals, rose-family fruits, cotton, flax) and those with separate male and female flowers (squashes, calabash, corn). Responds to brunians' quoted claim about dioecious cultivars and adds a light remark hoping Wolfe's views on plant domestication might be shared.
supports · source-and-allusion

I can't argue with your new formulation, especially after you gave the examples I requested. On the other hand,
it's easy to think of ancient cultivated plants that have flowers with both male and female parts--legumes, most
cereals, fruits of the rose family, cotton, and flax--and others that have separate male and female flowers on the same plant--squashes, the calabash, and for a Wolfean
connection, corn.

Of course, if you ever learn Gene Wolfe's thoughts on
these subjects, I hope you'll share them on this list.

Jerry Friedman
