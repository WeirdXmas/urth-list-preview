The thread: Fred Kiesche dismissed the forthcoming biography of Alice Sheldon (James Tiptree, Jr.) as pop psychology and pseudo-feminism, warning against psychoanalysing writers and recommending Charles Platt's Dreammakers interview instead. Dave Lebling disagreed, calling the biography a sympathetic, insightful portrait of Sheldon's character and generational split in feminism, and recommended it to Tiptree readers. David Duffy shared a Australian review that called the posthumous collection Meet Me At Infinity a curiosity rather than a classic, and said he was surprised by that verdict. The thread stayed on the biography and Tiptree, never reaching Gene Wolfe's fiction, and ended without agreement.

2006-10-19 Dave Lebling replying to Fred Kiesche #

Argues that the new biography of Alice Sheldon (James Tiptree, Jr.) is not pop psychology or pseudo-feminism but a sympathetic, insightful portrait of her character and of the generational split between 1940s-early 1960s feminism and the later generation. Responds to Fred Kiesche's dismissal of the book by citing the author's own familiarity with women of Sheldon's generation and recommending the book to Tiptree readers; the post does not discuss Wolfe's fiction.
disputes

I found the Tiptree/Sheldon biography to be simultaneously reverential and revelatory. I don't think it was full of pop psychology or pseudo-feminism. On the contrary, it very accurately (to my mind at least) portrayed the generational split between the feminism of the 40s to early 60s and the next generation that came of age in the late 60s and 70s. Its insights into Sheldon's character and motivations seemed both sympathetic and insightful.

I never met Alice Sheldon but I knew enough women of her generation to see the portrait as true-to-life. I would highly recommend the book to any fan of her writing.

-- Dave Lebling (aka vizcacha)
