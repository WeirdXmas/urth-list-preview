2008-11-25 Marc Aramini #

Argues that Severian is simultaneously a true conciliator and a false, diabolically mimicking Christ-figure whose title, roles and the new sun/new son pun make him torturer, conciliator, lictor, autarch and new sun. Grounds this in the claw as both relic and thorn, the saints' names, miracles, water and healing imagery, and the false sun of Vodalus's coin, drawing an analogy to the Church's doctrine of Christ being fully divine and fully mortal.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Severian is torturer, conciliator, lictor, autarch and new sun; Severian is a false Christ mimicking the divine; Terms for God translated as New Sun

The complexity of Wolfe&#39;s symbols are beyond simple categorization. The claw is both a divine relic and an ordinary thorn. Severian is both the conciliator and a dupe of aliens. Time turns our lies into truths.

The church has similar mysteries - three in one, the substance of christ being fully divine and fully mortal, and other paradoxes that can&#39;t be explained but can explain other things.

The most basic perusal of the text assures us that terms for God are translated as New Sun - the sun dial for example. The conflation of new son and new sun, as well as the symmetry of all the titles leads us to the obvious conclusion that the narrator is torturer, conciliator, lictor, autarch, and new sun.

This was obvious to me in the seventh grade when I read this as a tale of the parousia, but Severian is more than a revelatory Christ - the text tries very hard to establish credence as divine by the use of saints names, the miracles, contiguous use of water and healing, the triumph over death - but there are diabolical forces at work that mimic the divine. The coin of vodalus is a symbol of the sun, but it is false. Severian is both a fraud and a conciliator, and this is something that I can find only in Wolfe. A false Christ can still get the job done by pretending to be what he wants the people to believe he is.

Expresses enjoyment of the ambiguity in Wolfe's symbols, especially the diabolical forces mimicking the divine and Severian as a possible dupe, and reads the false coin as about false oaths and improper allegiance. Responds to Marc Aramini's post on divine and mortal paradox by agreeing, admitting his earlier Christological reading may be self-imposed, and noting how the clues on rereading seemed consistent.

you're surely right.
the diabolical forces that mimic the divine part is especially apt. and I suppose surely one could argue that the cosmology is bogus and he is 'just' a dupe. I like the ambiguity.
this is the sense I meant with the satanic catholic church comment. I'd say the same about many yogis and such as well.
I also found the false coin very profound. I took it somehow as about the false oaths, oath breaking, and , how would you put it, improper allegiance.

having just read it my second time, I suppose I'm just geeked on all this and surprised how thorough and consistent it seems to be within the seemingly convoluted structure. I didn't get it at all the first time. Early in Urth, I sort of got it, but I agree with Jordon that it is sort of a spoiler to the puzzle. The first read through was a serious WTF?!?!? for me. the second was more exciting than any fiction I've read. Upon rereading, I was determined to pay close attention to the clues, figuring that the 4 books should have a tight symbology if it was really suggesting all that Urth was giving away. I was not imposing this Christological viewpoint. this stuff all just started springing out at me. surely somewhere in me my inner cathaholic was suggesting it all.

really great to hash this stuff out with you all.

2008-11-26 Jordon Flato replying to Son of Witz #

Proposes that the Cock and the Angel story in The Book of the New Sun serves as a metaphor for the divine hierarchy, mapping the angel to the Hierogrammates, the eagle to the Hierodules, and the cock to humanity. Responds to Son of Witz's question about whether the transcendental themes resolve in God, offering the inset tale as a clue to that larger order.
proposes · allegory-and-typology · theology-and-religion · theories: Cock and the Angel allegorizes the divine hierarchy

I think a clue here is in the Cock and the Angel story. That has always seemed a blatant metaphor for the Hierogrammates (angel), Hierodules (eagle) and the Cock (Humanity).

"The eagle looked at the angel when her heard what the cock said, and the angel looked at the eagle. 'The Pancreator is infinitely far from us,' the angel said. 'And thus infinitely far from me, though I fly so much higher than you. I guess at his desires-no one can do otherwise."
