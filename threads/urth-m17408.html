Atlantis and Gonawanaland

Urth list · 4 messages · 2011-01-03 to 2011-01-05

2011urthGerry QuinnDavid StockhoffLee BermanDavid Duffy
The thread: Readers debated whether the Atlantis and Gondwanaland names in The Fifth Head of Cerberus point to real ancient truths about the gods and the Shadow Children. David Stockhoff argued that evil comes from humanity rather than Creation and that Wolfe holds no eternal Devil, disputing Lee Berman's view that something essentially evil lies in Creation's essence. Lee Berman argued Wolfe treats the gods as real and that fiction best expresses such reality, citing the Shadow Children's antiquity. Gerry Quinn replied that the names are a whimsical riff on the narrator's remarks, with the Shadow Children's memory showing a human origin, and David Duffy then argued a quoted line is a typo for 'I, for Five'. The thread ends without resolution, drifting toward a textual correction.
  • David Stockhoff: evil is rooted in humanity, not Creation; Wolfe holds no eternal Devil
  • Lee Berman: Wolfe treats the ancient gods as real; fiction best expresses this
  • Gerry Quinn: Atlantis and Gondwanaland are whimsical riffs; Shadow Children descend from humans
  • David Duffy: the quoted line is a typo for 'I, for Five'

Themes: Myth and folklore sources, Gods, angels and the Increate, Beasts and the animal in us

2011-01-04 Gerry Quinn replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the Atlantis and Gondwanaland names in The Fifth Head of Cerberus are a whimsical riff on the narrator's remarks about aborigines and earlier human waves, and that the Shadow Children's memory of those names shows they descend from a human culture. Grounds this in the narrator's debate with Mr. Million and in the Old Wise One's list of remembered names, replying to Lee Berman.
proposes · source-and-allusion · close-textual-reading · theories: Shadow Children descend from human star travellers; Shadow Children's remembered names come from Earth culture

The simplest explanation of Atlantis and Gondwanaland is that it's a riff on the observations of the narrator in the first novel, when forced by Mr. Million to debate the origins of the abos:

"...it is disrtinctly possible that the aborigines were descendants of some earlier wave of human expansion..."
Mr. Million says mildly, "I would confine myself to arguments of higher probability if I were you."
I nevertheless gloss upon the Etruscans, Atlantis, and the tenacity and expansionist tendencies of a hypothethical technological culture occupying Gondwanaland.

The Shadow Children have forgotten the name of their original home. Speaking for them, the Old Wise One mentions certain names as possibilities: "I, for five, remember all these names."

Of course these possibilities are for the most part absurd (except for Africa, I guess) - it is pretty clearly established that the Shadow Children are the descendants of human star travellers who arrived a century before the French colonists. They have forgotten the name of their original home (though they remember where it is and point out Sol in the sky). Wolfe whimsically has them remember some of the same names mentioned by the narrator of the first novel. The names also serve as an additional demonstration that they originate from our own culture or a successor, for only such a culture would know the names Atlantis and Gondwanaland.

- Gerry Quinn
