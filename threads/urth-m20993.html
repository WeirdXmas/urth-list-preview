The thread: Participants debated whether Silk's parentage is Typhon-linked, a clone, adopted, or something else, with Tussah's 'son not of my body' phrase central. Marc Aramini argued for Typhon or Kypris-based parentage and later adoption, James Wynn pressed for a clone of Typhon, and David Stockhoff and Gerry Quinn tested whether shared genes imply sonship. A parallel dispute over whether the Neighbors and inhumi are literal trees and vines, and over Gerry Quinn's and Lee Berman's interpretive methods, grew sharply personal. The thread ended without agreement, with posters defending entrenched readings.
  • Marc Aramini: Silk's parentage is Tussah-Kypris-Typhon, with adoption rather than a hidden genetic link; Neighbors and inhumi are literal trees and vines
  • James Wynn: Silk is a clone of Typhon or of Typhon's son; Tussah is a clone of Typhon
  • Gerry Quinn: shared genes do not imply sonship; Neighbors and inhumi are metaphors, not literal plants
  • David Stockhoff: Silk may be genetically linked to Typhon via engineering, though legal or social sonship is also possible
  • Andrew Mason: Silk's father is likely unknown or selectively bred, with Kypris's line suggesting an exchange with Pas
  • Lee Berman: interpretive methods differ; myth unlocks only one level of the work

Themes: Fathers, mothers and children, Doubles, twins and clones, Robots, androids and artificial life

2011-10-10 Andrew Mason #

Argues that Silk's two sets of parents count against the clone theory, since a clone lacks genetic parents, and proposes that Silk's mother may be Mamelta while his father remains unknown. Grounds this in a Fifth Head of Cerberus scene with Number Five and Wolfe's answer that Silk is 'the son of the Calde and his mistress', which he reads as a deflection. Responds to Gerry Quinn's point about implanted embryos.
disputes · puzzle-solving · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk's mother is Mamelta; Typhon is not Silk's father

I think the two sets of parents actually count against the clone theory; a clone does not have genetic parents in the ordinary sense. One might call the parents of his source his parents in a way; (this seems to be the line taken in _The Fifth Head of Cerberus_, where Number Five is shown a picture of his 'mother', i.e. the mother of the source of his line, back on Earth). But if Typhon was 'not born' it's not clear he has parents, and if he does it's hard to see how they are relevant to the story.

Moreover, we are told in a couple of places that the embryos are the result of selective breeding, rather than clones.

Regarding who his parents are: Wolfe was once asked about Silk's ancestry, and replied 'He is the son of the Calde and his mistress'. This answer, which seems to relate to his birth-mother and adoptive father, looks like a deliberate deflection of the question, not telling us what the questioner wanted to know. This in turn suggests that:
a. It is a significant question, which has an answer - his parents aren't just Some Guy and Some Girl.
b. It's not meant to be obvious from the story, since when something is meant to be obvious and readers don't get it, Wolfe is generally happy to explain (e.g. who is Blood's father, or why did Auk kidnap Hyacinth).

I think his mother may be Mamelta. I don't know who his father is, though I take it the answer is lurking somewhere. I can think of a plausible sequnce of events that would allow it to be Typhon, but I can also see problems with that - notably, that if spirits on the Aureate Path appear as they did when they died, Silk should have noticed that his father had two heads.

2011-10-11 David Stockhoff replying to James Wynn #

Argues that Silk may be a clone of Typhon whose appearance need not match Typhon's, since clones from different surrogate mothers might not look alike. Grounds this in Horn's subjective opinion that Silk and Chenille do not resemble each other, the uhlans' faces in the text, and a suggested classic SF model of cloning, such as Asimov's Mule.
proposes · genre-and-craft · close-textual-reading · theories: Silk is a clone of Typhon; Clones from different wombs may differ in appearance

Siblings often don't look alike. However, this must be one of those situations where the facts we are told can't matter as much as the narrator's motive for telling us. Horn's opinion is subjective.

But going back to my point: are there any existing data on human perception of facial similarity between clones born from different surrogate mothers, i.e., developing in different wombs? No. Is there any reason therefore to stick to the assumption that clones are always identical? (The only real evidence we have of cloning on Urth is that the uhlans' " faces were more akin than the faces of brothers." We know nothing else about how they were "made." Silk-clone-of-Typhon ought to follow that rule simply because it has been put forth, but Wolfe can break it when he wants.)

Which leads me to another angle. Is there a classic SF model from which we could derive rules of cloning "behavior"? For example, Asimov's Mule is a genetic mutation giving rise to super-telepathic powers; therefore he is a precursor to Typhon that we can "observe" in action. Has some author already worked out this cloning business?

Argues that Silk could be genetically linked to Tussah without being a straight clone, offering three possibilities: a shared source embryo, a non-genetic contribution from Tussah's mind, or Typhon's stored sperm. Grounds the case in a hypothetical about claiming cloned embryos as offspring and in the mismatch between Silk's and Tussah's genetics and looks.
proposes · puzzle-solving · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Silk is a clone of Typhon's son; Silk derives from an embryo predating Tussah; Tussah contributed non-genetically to Silk; Silk is son of Typhon and Tussah

As far as cloning, recall that your son gets only half your DNA. Cloning (100%) is not needed. I've argued this for three days, of course. To me, "cloning" need not be taken too literally, as there is a wide range of genetic manipulation. Any genetic contribution that is meaningful would count.

As an example of meaningful genetic contribution: if I went to my clonebank and ordered one copy, then found that the bank had made 20 copies (because my superior genes and oh, say, rulership of Earth made my DNA a hot property), then I could sue and claim those 20 embryos as my property/offspring. Even if they took only half of my DNA, I could at least claim paternity. (Look at what Monsanto does to farmers whose crops have been contaminated by their GM corn.) I could then call them all my sons and predict that one would be a future ruler of Earth. The bigger mystery is how I knew that would happen!

Three thoughts:

(1) It's almost as though Silk received a genetic contribution from a source that is not Tussah but identical to Tussah. That is, from a source for both Tussah and Silk, e.g., if Silk is clone #2 to Tussah's clone #1 off some original. If Silk's embryo preceded Tussah himself by 200 years, that's the only way he could be Tussah's genetic son anyway.

(2) Might Tussah have contributed something of himself to Silk that was not from his body, but from his mind? I have no idea how that might work.

(3) What if Typhon's sperm were kept for insemination rather than having his DNA cloned? You'd have any number of variations on Typhon as a result, some looking like him, some not, because Typhon would contribute half the genes (and any male result would be his son). Is there a solution for the other half that would result in Silk and also make him son of Tussah?

I see no particular reason why Typhon's and Tussah's genes couldn't be combined without any female contribution whatsoever. Whatever technological hurdles could be overcome or ignored; the bigger problems would be the narrative ones.
