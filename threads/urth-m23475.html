The thread: Readers debated the secret of the Inhumi in The Book of the Short Sun, with Nate Jarvis proposing a consent-based secret and linking it to the torturers' guild secret in The Book of the New Sun. Jerry Friedman argued the secret would be a practical threat to the Inhumi, since honest, non-parasitic humans would starve them, and later proposed it works gradually through parenting. David Stockhoff and Jarvis pushed back, calling honesty too costly and the threat merely theoretical, while Daniel Petersen backed Friedman by urging readers to take Wolfe's moral stances seriously. The thread drifted into Rajan spirit speculation and a side argument about the Sword of the Holy, ending without consensus.
  • nate jarvis: the Inhumi secret is that consent to drink once requires consent again, offered as a threat
  • Lee Berman: torturers' guild secret may tie to Wolfe's own life and parallels the Inhumi secret
  • Jeff Wilson: the guild is trusted because it places itself at the bottom; the abolition was likely figurative
  • Jerry Friedman: honest humans would starve the Inhumi, so the secret works gradually through child-rearing
  • David Stockhoff: honesty among humans is too costly, so the threat is only theoretical
  • Daniel Petersen: Wolfe's books argue moral and political positions, and Short Sun shows this poignantly

Themes: Beasts and the animal in us, Fathers, mothers and children, Suffering and sacrifice

2012-11-26 Lee Berman #

Proposes that the torturers' guild secret in The Book of the New Sun may tie to a personal event in Gene Wolfe's life, such as an order he received as a soldier, and that it parallels the secret of the Inhumi. Grounds this in Severian's justification to Dorcas for the torturer's role and his abolishing the guild once he holds Autarchial power, and in a multi-level reading of the book that uses an earlier biographical framework.
proposes · author-and-biography · puzzle-solving · theories: Torturers' secret relates to Wolfe's military service; Torturers' secret parallels the Inhumi secret

I agree with badger that this is too broad and simplistic. I agree with Jeff that this is, in some fashion, "the secret" that Master Gurloes imparted but there is more to it that we are supposed to derive.

When talking to Dorcas, Severian provides an elaborate, well-thought out justification for why his job as torturer is necessary. Yet when given Autarchial powers, one of Severian's first acts is to abolish the guild.

M. Andre-Druissi has provided an nice framework for showing how BotNS tracks Gene Wolfe's life up to that point, including his military service in Korea. I wonder if the "secret" of the torturers might relate to something personal that happened to Gene Wolfe; perhaps something he was ordered to do as a soldier.

(this is in keeping with my interpretive notion that BotNS is written at numerous levels- fictional/ narrative, mytho-religious, socio-political and autobiographical. So when a gap appears at the narrative level, the answer may be found by extrapolation at one or more of the other levels)

2012-11-26 Jeff Wilson replying to Lee Berman #

Argues that the torturers' guild is trusted because its members put themselves at the bottom of society, and that Severian's religious awakening makes the official justification for torture abhorrent. Responds to Lee Berman's quote about Severian abolishing the guild by doubting it was literal, suggesting instead the guild lost its licence to torture and became jailers and executioners.
disputes · close-textual-reading · theology-and-religion · theories: Severian's religious awakening makes torture abhorrent; Severian reduces the guild to jailers and executioners

I think it's more about the contrariness of being the lowest of the low, that they purposefully put themselves at the bottom of the heap because they have no scruples about what they will do for the Autarch, which also makes them the most trusted and valued. This may be what is qualitatively different from the other guilds' mystery.

Sev has also had a religious awakening about this time, and realizes that the party line that he presents to Dorcas makes perfect sense as a justification for a secular autocrat to ordain a guild of torturers, but such a thing is abhorrent to the Increate and anyone who truly follows Him.

I don't know that he literally abolishes the guild (he still speaks of the masters by title), or perhaps removes the license to perform torture from their charter, reducing them to jailers and executioners.

2012-11-28 Jerry Friedman replying to nate jarvis #

Proposes that if people treated one another better, the inhumi would suffer, so Horn's secret would work gradually through how parents raise their children. Responds to nate jarvis's skepticism by citing Andrew Mason's 'too heavy for our hands' line and a quoted passage on Tolkien's vision of society, arguing Wolfe wants readers to imagine that change.
proposes · close-textual-reading · puzzle-solving · theories: Better treatment of people would harm the inhumi; Horn's secret works through parents raising children better

Thanks to Andrew Mason for pointing out the "too heavy for our hands" line, which I'd forgotten.

Also, even if one person or a few people start treating others better, it would be bad for the inhumi.? And it would help others treat each other better, which in turn would help others.? In particular, if people raised their children better, the children would grow into better people, and so forth--and I don't need to say that parents' effects on their children are an important theme of BotSS.? I think Wolfe wants us to picture this and connect people's effect on the inhumi to parents' effect on their children.

(What would happen if an inhumu got the Rajan's personality or spirit or ka or whatever they get?)

On the subject of Wolfe recommending?an impossible spreading transformation of society, let me quote some non-fiction, "The Best Introduction to the Mountains":

"He [Tolkien] uncovered a forgotten wisdom among the barbarian tribes who had proved (against all expectation) strong enough to overpower the glorious civilizations of Greece and Rome; and he had not only uncovered but understood it. He understood that their strength -- the irresistible strength that had smashed the legions -- had been the product of that wisdom, which has now been ebbing away bit by bit for a thousand years. ?
"Having learned that, he created in Middle-earth a means of displaying it in the clearest and most favourable possible light. Its reintroduction would be small -- just three books among the overwhelming flood of books published every year -- but as large as he could make it; and he was very conscious (no man has been more conscious of it than he) that an entire forest might spring from a handful of seed. What he did, then, was to plant in my consciousness and yours the truth that society need not be as we see it around us. ?
[Government chains us.]
?
"It need not be so. We might have a society in which the laws were few and just, simple, permanent, and familiar to everyone -- a society in which everyone stood shoulder-to-shoulder because everyone lived by the same changeless rules, and everyone knew what those rules were. When we had it, we would also have a society in which the lack of wealth was not reason for resentment but a spur to ambition, and in which wealth was not a cause for self-indulgence but a call to service. We had it once, and some time in this third millennium we shall have it again..." ?

2012-11-28 Daniel Petersen replying to Jerry Friedman #

Supports Jerry Friedman's point and urges readers to apply Wolfe's essay on Tolkien to his own works, arguing that the books do argue for certain moral and political positions. Cites Short Sun as an obvious but dense example and praises its parent-child dynamic as poignant rather than sermonic, whatever the reader's politics.
supports · genre-and-craft · theories: Short Sun argues for certain moral and political beliefs

Very good. We need to see more application of his essay on Tolkien to his own works. It's easy to get so lost in his intricacies and tensions that we lose sight that the works do, in some fashion, 'argue' for certain moral and political beliefs and practices. I actually think Short Sun is one of the more 'obvious' examples, perhaps often missed because it is also one of the densest and most elliptical. (I would add that his parent-child dynamic is rich and poignant - not 'sermonic', in the negative sense - so whatever your politics you can *feel *some of the good familial pathos and be freshly 'sensitized' if nothing else.)

-DOJP
