The thread: Readers of Pirate Freedom debated whether Chris's confession and salvation through faith in Christ are credible, and whether his choices were genuinely free. Paul Witcover and bill reilly raised questions about his monastery placement and his guilt, while thalassocrat argued the confession was hypocritical given real choices like the Veracruz ministry. Adam Thornton and gwern0 framed the salvation as Protestant or Catholic theology, and Matthew Groves and Matthew King split over whether recurring weapon and purse imagery was a Freudian symbol or simply naval detail. Later posts shifted to Michael Straight's and Paul Witcover's agreement on the confessor reading and David Duffy's objection about the time-slips, with no resolution.
  • paul witcover: Chris's confession and Christ's love are serious moral questions, not light fun
  • thalassocrat: Chris had real choices and his confession is hypocritical or worthless
  • Adam Thornton: salvation by faith alone reflects a Protestant view with ironic overtones
  • gwern0: as a Catholic, Wolfe would accept salvation by faith, so Chris can be saved
  • Matthew Groves: weapons and purses are recurring Freudian symbols of Chris's cruelty
  • Matthew King: gun-size emphasis is literal naval concern, not a hidden joke

Themes: Catholic faith and sacraments, Suffering and sacrifice, Free will and choice

2007-11-30 paul witcover replying to thalassocrat #

Responds to bill reilly's questions about Pirate Freedom's Chris: whether his monastery placement really protected him, whether his confession excuses choices he actually had, and whether Christ's message that loving him suffices is good theology. Calls the questions excellent and says he is puzzled that critics dismiss the novel as light-hearted fun, missing its weightier issues.
supports · theology-and-religion

On Sat, 01 Dec 2007 00:40:53 +1100 bill reilly

That's an excellent review.

One point: was it really to protect Chris that his father put him in a monastery? I thought about that, but how much protection would it actually offer, compared to other options? And Chris in the early period spends a fair chunk of time back home - Christmas, 8 weeks over summer ...

His father seems to have some personal experience of monasteries: he thinks abbotts "bring you down".

On Chris' hypocrisy: framing & limiting his whole confession is his assertion that he had no choice but to act badly - those times are not these times, and so on. But he did he have choices. A critical point for me is right at the beginning of his career, in Veracruz. He helps a priest minister to slaves; he could keep on doing that, but instead chooses to return to the Weald.

Actually, I suppose the real beginning is just before that, when he excuses himself for stealing bread: God isn't going to send him to hell for any amount of venial sinning. Then he quickly progresses to assault, violently stealing a chicken. His older self helps him do it - a little scene encapsulating the whole of Chris' story.

Chris is indeed a bad man. But he has direct encounters with Christ (or at least believes he does). In one of them, Christ tells him that all that is needed is for Chris to love him. What to think about that? You can be a mobster and a pirate, but if you love Christ it doesn't matter? Is that good theology? For me it might serve as a reductio ad absurdum against that theology; but what's Wolfe's view?

###

All excellent questions, many of which I continue to puzzle over! I've been a little disappointed by the critical reception of this novel so far -- the majority view seems to be that it's a light-hearted romp, just pure fun, without any of the weightier issues normally present in his work. It's baffling to me that the book could be so significantly misapprehended.

2007-11-30 James Crossley replying to paul witcover #

Argues that Wolfe in Pirate Freedom disguises his meanings so far that casual readers may take it as a simple boy's adventure, missing its science fiction frame and buried moral questions. Grounds this in the reverse time travel that begins in the future and in a remembered review image of the Caribbean sun hiding shadows, responding to Paul Witcover's review thread.
supports · genre-and-craft

Not that I think he wants to be misunderstood, but Wolfe tends to purposely disguise his meanings, and in PF he seems to have pushed that tendency about as far as it can go. The book almost begs for readers to see it as simply a boy?s adventure. I?d bet that a significant portion of casual readers (if Wolfe still has any of those) wouldn?t be aware of the SF nature of the story, granting that time travel is a mite fantastical, but not even noticing that Chris?s reverse time travel begins in our future. The moral questions are buried even deeper.

Wasn?t there something in the review about the brightness of the Caribbean sun helping us forget about the inevitable shadows? That says it right.

James

2007-11-30 Adam Thornton replying to thalassocrat #

Argues that the Pirate Freedom plot, where a mobster-pirate can be saved by faith in Christ, reflects a Protestant view that faith alone suffices, and notes the irony given the religious background of the author and the mafia. Grounds this in the Epistle of James on faith without works being dead, contrasted with Ephesians on grace, and John 3:16 used in sermons.

2007-11-30 Matthew Groves replying to bill reilly #

Argues that Chris's cruelty in Pirate Freedom is reflected in recurring images of swords, daggers, guns and coin purses being taken, traded or lent, read as Freudian symbols. Grounds this in specific scenes: the saved slaves outside Portobello, the captain's cabin with its long nine, and the Hispaniola parley where Chris's coin purse is taken.
proposes · close-textual-reading · allegory-and-typology

To me, the most chilling insight into Chris's "half-monstrous" nature comes when he is recounting having saved the slaves in the Spanish stockade outside Portobello. Chris says this incident was the exception, but that "That just makes it sweeter."

Was anyone else struck by the constant references to swords and daggers of various shapes, often very carefully described, always being taken away or found or lost or traded or given? And all the guns and the importance of their size. Chris and Capt. Burt "share the captain's cabin with a long nine." Later he puts a long nine stern chaser in his cabin on the Castillo Blanco. Also doubloons and coin purses of various sizes being taken by force or carried or lent. It all struck me as Freudian. In particular, the incident on Hispaniola when Chris and the Buccaneers parlay with the Spanish and they take Chris's coin purse seems to be the source of a great deal of Chris's rage.

2007-12-01 Matthew King replying to Matthew Groves #

Argues that the gun-size emphasis in Pirate Freedom should be read literally as a naval commander's concern with firepower, not as a hidden joke, and compares it to Patrick O'Brian's Aubrey-Maturin novels. Responds to Matthew Groves's quoted remark about a captain's cabin sharing a long nine gun, offering a pun-based distinction between a stern-chaser and a stern chaser as the basis.
disputes · genre-and-craft

You find the same emphasis on number and size of guns in Patrick O'Brian's Aubrey-Maturin novels. I think it is better to take a naval commander's obsession with gunpower at face value. A sizeable stern-chaser is not the same thing as a sizeable-stern chaser.

2007-12-01 thalassocrat replying to bill reilly #

Argues that Chris in Pandora by Holly Hollander is a monster who knowingly sins, killing Michet and then refusing to undo it, and that his confession is worthless. Responds to Bill Reilly's and Paul Witcover's remarks on the novel's reception by citing Chris's choice to shape his younger self's trajectory so his older self benefits.
proposes · close-textual-reading · chronology-and-plot-logic · theories: Chris is the most unambiguously evil Wolfe character; Chris deliberately engineers his younger self's sins

On Sat, 01 Dec 2007 09:22:18 +1100 paul witcover <[email]>

In fact, isn't it quite clear that Chris is a monster? He sins, wilfully and in complete knowledge of what he is doing. Even when he is given a chance to undo his sinful acts, he rejects it, and instead uses the opportunity to try to ensure he will sin in the same way, again. His confession is worse than worthless.

Look: the whole story is supposed to be a kind of confession. No matter how you look at it, there is at least one thing for which he definitely needs the premium-level forgiveness package: the killing of Michet. Assume that everything else can be marked down to circumstances, battle, bad luck, youth, whatever. He killed Michet when Michet was offering him reconciliation, while he was effectively unarmed, and solely to preserve his respect as captain. The Michet killing triggers the whole "confession".

He comes back from the past. He can, perhaps, undo Michet. Whether or not he can actually alter the future/past, he has an obvious moral obligation to try to do so. But he wilfully chooses not to try. He does not go to visit himself in Jersey. He obviously plans to act exactly as Ignacio did during his "own" youth, and do everything to ensure that "Chris1" follows exactly the same trajectory as "he" did, so he can take over Novia when "Chris1" goes back to the future.

He is going to ensure that his youthful self sins in exactly the same way, so his older self can benefit. He preys not only on others, but also on his own youth.

I think Chris is perhaps the most unambiguously evil character Wolfe has presented in a novel. I find it breathtaking.

"Sometimes I have had to slip." Maybe, sometimes, but when he came back to the future he had a miraculous chance to try to undo the slips - and he rejected it.

2007-12-05 Michael Straight replying to thalassocrat #

Praises a review of Pirate Freedom and quotes a commenter's idea that Wolfe casts the reader as a priest in a confessional, forcing the reader to confront the difficulty of loving and forgiving one's neighbour. Says this perspective illuminates every Wolfe book, and that the commenter suggests Wolfe shares the reader's repugnance yet extends compassion to his character.
supports · theology-and-religion · theories: Wolfe makes the reader a priest hearing confession

Paul, that's a great review. Thanks for sharing.

And yet one of the commenters at your website offers one of best perspectives for reading Wolfe that I have yet seen:

"I suppose Wolfe makes of the reader a priest, and puts him on the receiving end in the confessional, and shows him at its most squirmy how tough it is to love (and forgive!) thy neighbor, harder far than loving God and yet its essential corollary. And thus we can safely assume that Wolfe shares our repugnance while simultaneously putting it aside in favor of compassion for his character, so essential to a believable ? let alone a soulful ? portrayal of him?"

Wow. I don't think there's a book Wolfe has written that is not illuminated by that idea.

2007-12-06 David Duffy replying to Michael Straight #

Argues that the time-slips in Pirate Freedom lack any hint of whether they are supernatural or technological, and that the reader needs a rationale for such loops. Connects the confessor theme of the quoted comment to Peace and to The Power and the Glory, noting an unrepentant character who cannot give absolution and a bad priest who remains efficacious.
disputes · chronology-and-plot-logic · theology-and-religion

Two things I thought about after reading this comment were: C. is unrepentant, and comments that he cannot offer absolution to others who are in that state (_Peace_ anyone?); the other is a la _the Power and the Glory_ etc, that even a bad priest is still efficacious.

I'm not completely happy with the central event in the story, the time-slips. We have no hints about its nature, whether supernatural or technological. I have no problem with supernatural/fantastical displacements providing they don't have disturbing effects on overall causality ;) ie unhappy young man yearns to slip back in time to the romantic age of pirates, mysteriously it happens, and today comes back to tell his story and mourn his lost love. And I don't mind a _By his Bootstraps_ or even a _New Sun_. But in the latter, I want some kind of a rationale for my closed timelike curves eg meddling superintelligences ;)

David Duffy.
