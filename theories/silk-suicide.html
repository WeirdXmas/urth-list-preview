Silk killed himself

At the end of Return to the Whorl, Silk (in Horn's body) killed himself beside Hyacinth's coffin, or died of grief; the counter-reading is that he did not.

45 threads · 55 posts · for 20 / against 29 · first raised 2000-08-08 by Michael Andre-Driussi
Main voices: Adam Stephanides (6), Marc Aramini (5), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (4), Michael Andre-Driussi (3), Alex David Groce (3), Robert Borski (3)

The debate

The idea that Silk killed himself beside Hyacinth's coffin was first raised on 2000-08-08 by Michael Andre-Driussi, who simply stated that Silk, not anyone else, used a knife there, without argument [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000]. The same day Tim Boolos partly backed that reading but argued Silk only came close, that the blood came from a sacrifice like Orpine's, and that Hari Mau took the body after a transference between two despondent spirits [Tim Boolos, 2000]. Fernando Q. Gouvea asked how the suicide reading fit the book's searching-for-Silk passages [Fernando Q. Gouvea, 2000]. Early rivals included Dennis G. Berdanis, who suggested Silk died of grief after Hyacinth's death [Dennis G. Berdanis, 2000], and James Jordan, who read him as dying of wounds from a fight [James Jordan, 2000].

In 2001, around Return to the Whorl, the debate sharpened. Dave Lebling called the book a suicide note [Dave Lebling, 2001], and Robert Borski agreed it was a long suicide note while proposing a bomb or assassination as an alternative cause of the wounds [Robert Borski, 2001][Robert Borski, 2001]. Adam Stephanides argued that Silk cut and wrecked his house in grief and that the attempt was half-hearted [Adam Stephanides, 2001][Adam Stephanides, 2001]. Alex David Groce and Timothy Reilly rejected a suicide reading, Groce on the grounds that someone must have attacked Silk [Alex David Groce, 2001] and Reilly because a dead body would not suit Silk's planned new body [Timothy Reilly, 2000]. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes proposed self-gashing in grief, drawing on Orpine's sacrifice [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2001]. Dan Parmenter cited the house-and-wife passage as textual support, and Kevin Maroney answered that it reads as profound grief rather than proof of suicide [Dan Parmenter, 2001][Kevin J. Maroney, 2001].

In 2002 Stephen Case made what Danehy-Oakes called the strongest case yet, arguing that Silk's soul was dying at the end of the Long Sun series, as the Neighbors' remark suggests [Stephen Case, 2002][Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2002]. Jason Ingram and Seth Lombardi offered rival readings of the arm wounds as mourning frenzy or a genuine failed attempt [Jason Ingram, 2002][Seth Lombardi, 2002], while Michael Straight argued for a failed attempt given Silk's toughness [Michael Straight, 2002]. Robert Borski rejected the suicide reading as too heinous for Wolfe's Catholicism [Robert Borski, 2002]. Case later argued the Remora wedding scene loses its meaning unless Silk is suicidal [Stephen Case, 2002]. From 2009 to 2013 the theory was revived by James Wynn, who read the Oreb passage as alluding to suicide [James Wynn, 2010], Lee Berman [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2013], and Marc Aramini, who argued that accumulated betrayals, not the clone revelation, drove Silk to it [Marc Aramini, 2011][Marc Aramini, 2011]. António Pedro Marques objected on timing grounds [António Pedro Marques, 2011].

For

  • Silk's suicide beside the coffin was first proposed with no textual argument, and posters later tried to test it against the text [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000][Fernando Q. Gouvea, 2000].
  • Dave Lebling and Robert Borski read Return to the Whorl as a suicide note by Silk [Dave Lebling, 2001][Robert Borski, 2001].
  • Adam Stephanides cited the oozing cuts, the house wrecked, and the Neighbor's statement that Silk's spirit is dying as evidence [Adam Stephanides, 2001].
  • Dan Parmenter and Stephen Case took the house-and-wife passage and the Neighbors' remark as textual support for a suicide attempt [Dan Parmenter, 2001][Stephen Case, 2002].
  • James Wynn read the Oreb passage as an allusion to Silk's suicide after Hyacinth's death [James Wynn, 2010].

Against

  • Dennis Berdanis and James Jordan read the wounds as grief or dying from a fight, not suicide [Dennis G. Berdanis, 2000][James Jordan, 2000].
  • Robert Borski proposed a bomb or assassination as an alternative cause and called the suicide reading strained [Robert Borski, 2001].
  • Alex Groce and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued someone must have attacked Silk, or that the wounds were grief gashing [Alex David Groce, 2001][Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2001].
  • Timothy Reilly argued a dead body would not suit Silk's planned new body [Timothy Reilly, 2000].
  • Kevin Maroney read the passage as profound grief and doubted Silk would cut his own throat [Kevin J. Maroney, 2001].

Where it stands: Still disputed when the list moved on: the suicide reading had repeated advocates over 2000–2013, notably Case, Aramini and Wynn, but no consensus, and many posters rejected it or offered grief, attack or failed-attempt alternatives.
