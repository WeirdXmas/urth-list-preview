Silent Silk and Silver Silk are Silenus and Silvanus

The names Silent Silk and Silver Silk allude to Silenus and Silvanus.

7 threads · 14 posts · for 5 / against 10 · first raised 1997-06-19 by Alice Turner
Main voices: Lee Berman (5), James Wynn (4), Gerry Quinn (3), Alice Turner (2), Nick Lee (1)

The debate

The theory was first raised by Lee Berman in November 2010 ([Lee Berman, 2010]), who proposed that Silk's nicknames allude to Silenus and Silvanus as part of a larger Dionysian reading of the Long Sun and Short Sun books. James Wynn immediately endorsed it ([James Wynn, 2010]) and repeated it in later posts ([James Wynn, 2010]), though he added no independent evidence of his own. Alice Turner had earlier, in 1997, discussed Silenus's blackness but did not link him to the Silk names ([Alice Turner, 1997]).

The discussion sharpened in November 2011, when Berman, Wynn and Nick Lee defended the link against Gerry Quinn and others. Berman argued that the text explains the Silver Silk epithet but readers should look past it ([Lee Berman, 2011]), and Nick Lee extended the idea to a pun on Latin argentum and the god Silvanus ([Nick Lee, 2011]). Gerry Quinn rejected these readings, arguing that the names have clear in-text explanations and that syllabic resemblances are chance ([Gerry Quinn, 2011] [Gerry Quinn, 2011] [Gerry Quinn, 2011]).

Later posts kept the theory alive mainly through Berman and Wynn, who tied the Silenus and Silvanus identification to a faun equation of Silk and Horn ([Lee Berman, 2011]) and to the wider Dionysus reading ([James Wynn, 2012]). Berman also argued that Wolfe leaves mythic references deliberately fuzzy and should be read through Greek myth ([Lee Berman, 2011]). The thread summaries show that the exchanges often turned personal and produced little new textual evidence.

For

  • Lee Berman first proposed that the Silent Silk and Silver Silk nicknames tie Silk to Silenus and Silvanus within a Dionysian reading ([Lee Berman, 2010]).
  • James Wynn endorsed the identification and linked Silenus and Silvanus to the Dionysus cult, citing Silenus's appearance in Soldier of the Mist ([James Wynn, 2010] [James Wynn, 2010]).
  • Nick Lee argued that Silver may echo Silvanus through a pun on Latin argentum and ager, and extended this to Phanes ([Nick Lee, 2011]).
  • Berman defended mythological sources for the Silver Silk epithet against Quinn's objection that the text explains it ([Lee Berman, 2011] [Lee Berman, 2011]).
  • Berman read Silk as a faun equated with Silvanus and Silenus, linking him to Horn ([Lee Berman, 2011]).

Against

  • Gerry Quinn argued that the Silent Silk and Silver Silk names already have clear explanations in the text, so no hidden allusion is needed ([Gerry Quinn, 2011] [Gerry Quinn, 2011]).
  • Quinn held that character names are chosen for ambiance and that similar syllables appear by chance in random noise ([Gerry Quinn, 2011]).
  • Quinn and other critics questioned the methodology of reading deeper layers, arguing that deeper layers are not automatically more true ([Gerry Quinn, 2011] [Lee Berman, 2011]).
  • James Wynn later disputed Berman's Dionysus reading, citing the Silvanus and Silenus parallels only as a basis for a different interpretation of Silver Silk ([James Wynn, 2012]).
  • Quinn and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes disputed the wider name-based readings, and Quinn repeatedly rejected hidden-allusion readings in the Silver Silk thread ([Lee Berman, 2011] [Gerry Quinn, 2011]).

Where it stands: Still disputed: the theory was raised by Berman and kept by Wynn and Nick Lee, but Quinn and others rejected it, and the threads ended in impasse without agreement.
