Wolfe is the translator of Severian's manuscript

The books present themselves as Wolfe's translation from a future language, and the appendices and word choices should be read in that light.

33 threads · 35 posts · for 20 / against 12 · first raised 1997-05-08 by James Jordan
Main voices: David Stockhoff (6), Jeff Wilson (4), Gerry Quinn (4), Paul C Duggan (2), Jerry Friedman (2), Lane Haygood (2)

The debate

The idea that the book's framing is a translation goes back to James Jordan in 1997, who proposed that Wolfe may have modelled the translator-author idea on Fitzgerald's Rubaiyat and pointed to the Isaac Watts epigraph in The Shadow of the Torturer [James Jordan, 1997]. The card's wording, that Wolfe is the translator of Severian's manuscript, first appears with Paul C Duggan in 2000, who argued that Wolfe appears inside The Book of the New Sun as translator and cited the archaic diction of In Green's Jungles [Paul C Duggan, 2000]. Duggan developed this in 2002 by proposing that Severian wrote in a distant past universe and that Wolfe is only the translator [Paul C Duggan, 2002]. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Jerry Friedman had earlier, in 2001, pointed to the fictional translator as a way of setting aside narrator-reliability questions [Jerry Friedman, 2001].

Through the 2000s and 2010s the discussion shifted from whether a translator exists to what the translator changes. Jeff Wilson argued in 2008 that the manuscript was originally a future of our Earth [Jeff Wilson, 2008], and in 2010 that the translator's notes signed G.W. are a fictional, less-informed Wolfe who plants errors [Jeff Wilson, 2010]. David Stockhoff argued that the translator added chapter titles and epigraphs and introduced the chapter breaks, citing Latro's scroll and Kipling epigraphs Severian could not have known [David Stockhoff, 2010], and in 2013 that archaic political words are the translator's usage [David Stockhoff, 2013]. Nicholas Gevers argued that a translator-Wolfe might have embellished the diction, citing the Burton dedication of Soldier of Sidon [Nicholas Gevers, 2010].

Opponents mainly questioned how much the translator frame changes the story. Matthew Groves argued in 2009 that the persona plays little role in The Book of the New Sun [Matthew Groves, 2009]. Ryan Dunn disputed that translation embellishes Severian's story, citing Severian's own musings on the Book of Gold [Ryan Dunn, 2010]. Stockhoff also argued that the hedge about horses is satirical pedantry rather than a sign of the translator's presence [David Stockhoff, 2010]. Jeff Wilson's 2011 argument that the translator places the manuscript in the absolute future was, by his own account, only circumstantial [Jeff Wilson, 2011]. Later posts such as Greg Kurzawa's 2014 note on a Polish translator who may have consulted Wolfe about renderings were offered as tangential evidence [Greg Kurzawa, 2014].

For

  • Duggan argued in 2000 that Wolfe appears inside The Book of the New Sun as translator, citing the archaic diction of In Green's Jungles [Paul C Duggan, 2000].
  • Duggan proposed in 2002 that the manuscript was written in a past universe and Wolfe is only its translator [Paul C Duggan, 2002].
  • Fusch treated The Book of the New Sun as a translated work from another culture when discussing narrator expectations [Daniel Fusch, 1999].
  • Stockhoff argued the translator added the chapter titles, epigraphs and chapter breaks, citing the convention of translated editions and Latro's scroll [David Stockhoff, 2010] [David Stockhoff, 2010].
  • Jeff Wilson argued the translator's notes signed G.W. are a fictional Wolfe, which lets errors be read as deliberate [Jeff Wilson, 2010].

Against

  • Matthew Groves argued the translator persona plays little role in the New Sun, since Wolfe drops the pretense and a further layer would distract [Matthew Groves, 2009].
  • Ryan Dunn doubted that the translation frame changes Severian's story beyond Wolfe's own examples, citing Severian's musings on the Book of Gold [Ryan Dunn, 2010].
  • Jeff Wilson argued the manuscript was originally a future of our Earth rather than a translated text, citing shared artifacts and buildings [Jeff Wilson, 2008].
  • Jeff Wilson conceded that the evidence for the translator's note placing the manuscript in the absolute future is only circumstantial [Jeff Wilson, 2011].
  • Stockhoff argued that the appendix hedge about horses is satirical pedantry suited to the translator rather than evidence of a distinct translator voice [David Stockhoff, 2010].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the translator reading is repeatedly raised and developed by a few posters, but the list never reached agreement on whether the translator persona matters to the reading of the text.
