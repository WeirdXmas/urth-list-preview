Free will is consent to what will happen

In Wolfe's books free will means cooperating with what will happen anyway.

28 threads · 33 posts · for 16 / against 12 · first raised 1998-01-29 in the thread "Free will in BOtNS"
Main voices: Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (6), Daniel Petersen (5), Alice Turner (2), Alex David Groce (2), Maru Dubshinki (2), Son of Witz (2)

The debate

The earliest post on the list that carries this wording is a 2003 post by Nigel Price [Nigel Price, 2003], which discusses Wolfe's recurring theme of assuming the virtue and cites Silk's remark on free will, with the poster then contrasting the in-Time view with God's Eternity. The idea lay dormant for years. In 2011 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes recalled a Long Sun passage in which Silk realizes free will exists so that people can consent to what will happen anyway, framing it as a response to Lane Haygood on evil and adding a point about grace [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011]. Daniel Petersen then asked whether Silk was presenting a free-will theodicy that still depends on strong divine sovereignty [Daniel Petersen, 2011].

In November 2012 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes restated the theory as a formula, arguing that God and humans are free only to act according to their nature, so nature constrains choice, and offering an Einstein quotation on whether God had any choice [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2012]. The same day Daniel Petersen argued that Severian's choices look more like libertarian free will than determinism [Daniel Petersen, 2012], and Bill Burgess argued that free will and determinism are complementary because commitments continually reshape us [Bill Burgess, 2012]. Petersen later said Wolfe leaves the question open in the Solar Cycle [Daniel Petersen, 2012], and the thread on symbols drifted into Molinism and Omega Point ideas without returning to consent.

In 2014 the free-will discussion moved to theism versus materialism. Marc Aramini argued that Wolfe believes in free will and that a transcendent God can grant it more readily [Marc Aramini, 2014][Marc Aramini, 2014], Jeff Wilson argued that free will exists under theism but not under materialism [Jeff Wilson, 2014], and Gerry Quinn argued that a deterministic robot could have free will [Gerry Quinn, 2014]. None of these posts returned to the consent formulation.

For

  • Nigel Price's 2003 post cites Silk's remark on free will in support of cooperating with what will happen anyway [Nigel Price, 2003].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes says a Long Sun passage has Silk realize that free will's purpose is to let people consent to what will happen anyway [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argues that nature constrains choice for both God and humans, so free will is consenting to what will happen [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2012].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes links the idea to grace, arguing that consent is part of how people relate to what is already fixed [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011].

Against

  • Daniel Petersen argues that Severian's choices shape his story through libertarian free will rather than acceptance of fate [Daniel Petersen, 2012].
  • Daniel Petersen argues that Wolfe's characters, like Silk, may freely turn toward or away from goodness [Daniel Petersen, 2012].
  • Bill Burgess holds that free will and determinism are complementary, grounded in commitments rather than consent to fate [Bill Burgess, 2012].
  • Marc Aramini argues that Wolfe's world view supports free will against deterministic readings [Marc Aramini, 2014].
  • Gerry Quinn argues that a deterministic robot could have free will just as humans do, so freedom need not mean consent [Gerry Quinn, 2014].

Where it stands: Raised in 2003 and revived by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes in 2011 and 2012, but no other poster on the list takes up the consent formulation, and most free-will posts argue for libertarian choice or compatibilism, so it remains a minority reading and was largely dropped.
