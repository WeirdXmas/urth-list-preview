Wolfe's fiction is orthodox Catholic

Wolfe writes as a fully orthodox Roman Catholic, and his fiction works out his faith (the counter-reading is that it is not Christian allegory).

132 threads · 135 posts · for 56 / against 76 · first raised 1997-04-04 by James Jordan
Main voices: Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (19), Lee Berman (13), James Jordan (11), James Wynn (11), Marc Aramini (7), David Stockhoff (6)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the list by James Jordan on 1997-04-04, who insisted Wolfe is orthodox Roman Catholic and read the Outsider's incarnation language as an inadequate expression of orthodoxy rather than heresy [James Jordan, 1997]. Early posts split on whether Christian symbols in the books were genuinely Christian: Peter Cash argued the Castle is a church with pagan traits and that paganism predominates [Peter Cash, 1997], while Jordan replied that mixed imagery attacks idolatry [James Jordan, 1997]. Through 2000, Jordan kept supporting orthodoxy against Manichaean or gnostic readings, citing Emery in The Detective of Dreams and Wolfe's reported reading of Aquinas on conversion [James Jordan, 2000], while Peter Stephenson and David Duffy floated a tortured Catholic sensibility and a medieval Christian worldview [Peter Stephenson, 2000] [David Duffy, 1999].

From 2002 the debate sharpened around gnosticism. Alice Turner and Josh Geller argued Wolfe is orthodox rather than a real Gnostic, with Geller saying the gnostic label was coined by Orthodox Christians [Alice Turner, 2002] [Josh Geller, 2002], while James Wynn answered with the Catholic Encyclopedia's entry on Gnosticism [James Wynn, 2002]. In 2005 Wynn argued Wolfe layers Gnostic and Christian ideas in the Long Sun books rather than writing allegory, and that the Sun books riff on Christian Gnostic heresies [James Wynn, 2005] [James Wynn, 2005]. Dan'l Danehy-Oakes repeatedly argued that Wolfe embeds non-Christian cosmologies inside an orthodox Christian frame, a claim disputed by Andy Robertson, who called the New Sun Christian only loosely [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2005] [Andy Robertson, 2007].

The thread was revisited many times. Adam Stephanides argued Wolfe's practice matches his distinction between writing as a Catholic and writing Catholic propaganda [Adam Stephanides, 2005], and John Watkins argued Wolfe is broad-minded rather than in the Mel Gibson camp [John Watkins, 2008]. Later posters such as Lee Berman argued that belief in real pagan gods conflicts with orthodox Catholicism [Lee Berman, 2010], and Ross Arlen Tieken argued Wolfe is not a traditional Catholic [Ross Arlen Tieken, 2013], while Marc Aramini argued Wolfe holds the orthodox creed on Christ's natures [Marc Aramini, 2014]. The question was never settled in the list.

For

  • Wolfe's theology is orthodox rather than Manichaean, supported by an Emery statement in The Detective of Dreams and Wolfe's reported reading of Aquinas on conversion [James Jordan, 2000].
  • Wolfe takes Catholic dogma seriously, since orthodoxy leaves room for oddities like belief in ghosts, as Alex David Groce argued [Alex David Groce, 2003] [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2003].
  • Wolfe's fiction is decidedly Christian, citing the Judeo-Christian value on the physical world and the stories No Planets Strike and Queen [Roy C. Lackey, 2005].
  • Wolfe holds the orthodox creed that Christ is fully human and fully divine, so Christ's power is inherent [Marc Aramini, 2014].
  • Wolfe's religious content is more than subtle, and readers miss much without knowing he is a Catholic with an incarnationalist outlook [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2007].

Against

  • Wolfe is orthodox rather than a real Gnostic, with the Demiurge reading in the Long Sun books not making him one [Alice Turner, 2002] [Josh Geller, 2002].
  • Wolfe layers many Gnostic and Christian ideas in the Long Sun books and draws deliberately on Gnostic Christian themes, as James Wynn argued [James Wynn, 2005] [James Wynn, 2005].
  • Wolfe believes in the reality of pagan gods, which Lee Berman and Ross Arlen Tieken argued is irreconcilable with orthodox Catholicism [Lee Berman, 2010] [Ross Arlen Tieken, 2013].
  • Wolfe's Christian elements are indirect and paganism predominates, as Peter Cash argued from the Castle [Peter Cash, 1997].
  • Wolfe's theology is Neoplatonic rather than orthodox Christian, since God is categorically different from created beings, as Stanisław Bocian argued [Stanisław Bocian, 2009].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the orthodox reading gained support from Jordan, Groce and Danehy-Oakes and was largely accepted in the 2003 thread, but gnostic, pagan and heterodox readings were argued repeatedly through 2014, and the list never reached consensus.
