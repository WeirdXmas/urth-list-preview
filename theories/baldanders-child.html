The giant child is Baldanders's or Severian's

The giant child in Baldanders's chamber (or Baldanders himself) is linked to Severian's family.

11 threads · 13 posts · for 7 / against 6 · first raised 1999-04-08 by Robert Borski
Main voices: Gerry Quinn (3), mournings glory (2), Lee Berman (2), Robert Borski (1), Ori Kowarsky (1), Roy C. Lackey (1)

The debate

The theory first appears in 1999, when Robert Borski proposed that Baldanders fathered a catamite child and linked Severian's family to Becan's father and Dorcas [Robert Borski, 1999]. The same year Ori Kowarsky argued that Erebus and Baldanders are Severian's siblings, resting this on biology, the aquastor machine and a shared dream [Ori Kowarsky, 1999]. The theory lay quiet until 2006, when Roy C. Lackey pointed out that Solar Labyrinth discusses the giant child and its gold hair, which the book treats as a mark of Severian's blood relatives [Roy C. Lackey, 2006]. mournings glory then argued that Idas was turned by her father Baldanders [mournings glory, 2006], and b sharp argued that the child was cloned from Dorcas cells, a view tied to David DiGiacomo [b sharp, 2006]. mournings glory also argued that Ouen could not be ruled out as a Baldanders or Severian offspring, noting his blue eyes like Dorcas's [mournings glory, 2006].

In 2010 the debate turned on the child in Baldanders' bed. John Watkins argued that hair colour can be made to fit any relation in the family tree, so colour cannot prove a genealogy [John Watkins, 2010]. Lee Berman argued the child is Baldanders' real child, because the setting and chains make the sexual reading unavoidable, and Josh Geller argued that Severian's denials stem from fondness for Baldanders [Lee Berman, 2010][Josh Geller, 2010]. Gerry Quinn offered son, replacement or a child like Baldanders from the start, citing the boy's apparent retardation and Baldanders' moodiness, while noting that Talos wandering off weighs against paternity [Gerry Quinn, 2010]. Berman also linked the pale pandours to Idas and Erebus's warriors [Lee Berman, 2010]. The Juturna and child-in-bed threads both ended without agreement, with posters such as Watkins and Danehy-Oakes disputing whether colour proves anything.

In 2011 Nick Lee reread the Tale of the Boy Called Frog so that Frog stands for Severian and Fish for Baldanders, making Baldanders his sibling, and he rejected James Wynn's denial of Typhon's role [Nick Lee, 2011]. Adam Thornton reported a dream in which he argued that Severian is Baldanders' father, but he said the evidence vanished on waking, so the post is only a jokey aside [Adam Thornton, 2011].

For

  • Lackey cited the book's claim that gold hair marks only Severian's blood relatives, so the giant child may be his kin [Roy C. Lackey, 2006].
  • Berman argued the chained child is Baldanders' real child, since the fancy setting and chains make the sexual reading unavoidable [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Quinn proposed the child is Baldanders' son, citing the boy's apparent retardation and Baldanders' self-modification moodiness [Gerry Quinn, 2010].
  • Kowarsky argued that Severian's biology, the aquastor machine and the shared dream with Baldanders make Erebus and Baldanders his siblings [Ori Kowarsky, 1999].
  • Borski argued Baldanders fathered the catamite child, citing the Contessa's resemblance to Mom and the basket at Oldgate [Robert Borski, 1999].

Against

  • Watkins argued that hair colour can be made to fit any relation in the family tree, so it cannot prove a genealogy [John Watkins, 2010].
  • Geller argued that Severian denies the catamite reading out of affection for Baldanders, not from evidence of a real child [Josh Geller, 2010].
  • Quinn noted that Talos wandering off weighs against paternity [Gerry Quinn, 2010].
  • b sharp argued that the child was cloned from Dorcas cells, not fathered by Baldanders, and that modern beauty norms make the reading of the figure suspect [b sharp, 2006].
  • mournings glory noted that Ouen has blue eyes like his blonde mother Dorcas, so the child does not clearly break the family's inherited traits [mournings glory, 2006].

Where it stands: Still disputed and never settled: the theory was raised repeatedly from 1999 to 2011, the child-in-bed discussions of 2010 ended in impasse, and posters kept citing the same passages to opposite conclusions.
