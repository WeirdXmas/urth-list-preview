Wolfe is a modernist

Wolfe's central literary affiliation is modernist (or postmodernist).

40 threads · 22 posts · for 14 / against 8 · first raised 1997-02-13 by CoxRathvon
Main voices: Alex David Groce (5), Daniel Fusch (3), Marc Aramini (3), Jonathan Laidlow (2), David Duffy (2), Ross Arlen Tieken (2)

The debate

The theory was first raised on 1997-02-13 by CoxRathvon, who placed Wolfe beside Nabokov, Borges, Calvino and Barthelme as a postmodern metafiction stylist rather than an SF writer [CoxRathvon, 1997]. The main debate came in October 1999, when Daniel Fusch argued that Severian's narration is modernist and that The Book of the New Sun is more modernist than postmodernist, comparing it to Moby-Dick, Faulkner and Joyce [Daniel Fusch, 1999][Daniel Fusch, 1999]. Alex David Groce countered that Wolfe's influences are mainstream and genre sources such as Proust, Borges and Chesterton, and that his prose lacks modernist experiment [Alex David Groce, 1999][Alex David Groce, 1999]. Damien Broderick then offered modernism as his opinion, citing McHale and Jameson [Damien Broderick, 1999], while Groce himself later argued that Wolfe believes real natures exist and so is modernist rather than postmodernist [Alex David Groce, 1999].

From 1999 the argument broadened into specific works and readers' tastes. Nigel Price tied Wolfe's modernism to pre-modernist Dickensian autobiography and Severian's memory-driven narration [Nigel Price, 1999], and Jonathan Laidlow backed a modernism that reconnects with reality [Jonathan Laidlow, 1999]. David Duffy later called Wolfe a conventional modernist in the manner of Joyce [David Duffy, 2008] and argued that Peace uses modernist dream logic [David Duffy, 2000]. In 2012 Marc Aramini claimed objective markers place Wolfe as a modernist before 1973 [Marc Aramini, 2012] and read Morning-Glory as expressing a modernist loss of direction [Marc Aramini, 2012]. Ross Arlen Tieken linked Wolfe's Catholic traditionalism to modernists such as Eliot and Pound and to New Criticism [Ross Arlen Tieken, 2013][Ross Arlen Tieken, 2013].

The 2014 and 2018 posts moved toward a mixed or anti-postmodern position. Frederick Norwood said Wolfe is not a postmodernist [Norwood, Frederick Hudson, 2014], while Daniel Petersen argued that Wolfe's postmodern play undercuts postmodern fragmentation, a view he gave after Aramini's video on the subject [Daniel Petersen, 2014]. Aramini argued that Wolfe's puzzles are objectively solvable and so not postmodern [Marc Aramini, 2018]. Later threads on Calvino and on the New Criticism saw Alice Turner and Groce both call Wolfe a modernist rather than a postmodernist, though Turner and Groce disagreed about direct influence and the thread ended without resolution.

For

  • Fusch argued that Severian is an unreliable modernist narrator, citing the contest tales, Ascian storytelling and a comparison with Faulkner [Daniel Fusch, 1999].
  • Fusch argued that the book's unjudged inset tales and cyclical structure make it more modernist than postmodernist, comparing it to Joyce and Moby-Dick [Daniel Fusch, 1999].
  • Groce argued that Wolfe believes real natures exist and can only be approximated, which marks him as a modernist unlike postmodernists who doubt unitary natures [Alex David Groce, 1999].
  • Duffy argued that The Book of the New Sun works like Joyce's Ulysses, dense enough to occupy readers and sprinkled with hints [David Duffy, 2008].
  • Aramini argued that objective markers in Wolfe's stories place him as a modernist before 1973 [Marc Aramini, 2012].

Against

  • Groce argued that Wolfe's influences are mainstream and genre sources rather than modernist stylists like Joyce, Faulkner or Woolf [Alex David Groce, 1999].
  • Groce argued that Wolfe's narration uses few of the formal tools of modernism, unlike Proust or Faulkner [Alex David Groce, 1999].
  • Price argued that Wolfe's roots are pre-modernist, drawing on the Dickensian model and humane narration [Nigel Price, 1999].
  • Fusch conceded that the story itself is not modernist, even though Severian's narration is [Daniel Fusch, 1999].
  • Aramini argued that Wolfe's puzzles are objectively solvable, which he contrasted with postmodern indeterminacy [Marc Aramini, 2018].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the theory was raised repeatedly from 1997 to 2018, drew sustained counter-arguments, and the list never reached consensus, though Groce and Turner later also called Wolfe a modernist.
