Silk is a Christ (or Moses) figure

Silk's story is modelled on the Gospels (or on Moses leading the Exodus, or another figure), and the Book of Silk works as a gospel.

104 threads · 133 posts · for 79 / against 54 · first raised 1996-12-04 by James Jordan
Main voices: James Jordan (27), James Wynn (14), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (13), Lee Berman (10), Alex David Groce (8), Kieran Mullen (5)

The debate

The theory that Silk is a Christ (or Moses) figure was first raised on the list by James Jordan in December 1996, who argued that Father Silk's life follows the events of Jesus' life [James Jordan, 1996]. Jordan returned to it in 1996 with the Good Shepherd parable and the defence of Silk's burial and soul-journey [James Jordan, 1996]. Ranjit Bhatnagar matched Silk's actions to Christ's temptations in March 1997 [Ranjit Bhatnagar, 1997], and Michael Andre-Driussi and Derek Bell extended the idea into a Moses pattern covering Silk, Auk and Quetzal in April 1997 [Michael Andre-Driussi, 1997][Derek Bell, 1997]. Over 1997 to 1999 the list widened to include Silk's staff-sword, his disappearance as an ascension in Acts, and Silk's Gospel-based writing by Horn [mary whalen, 1998][Michael Straight, 1998][James Jordan, 1999].

The 2000s brought the Christ, Moses, Odin and trinity readings into competition. Jordan argued for a Christ and Paul pattern with Silk and Horn [James Jordan, 2001], while Sheila Herndon proposed a Silk, Horn and Oreb trinity [Sheila Herndon, 2001]. Thomas Bitterman built a Moses-and-conquest reading [Thomas Bitterman, 2002], and Don Doggett argued that Robert Graves's King Jesus anticipates Silk's Jesus-like engineering [Don Doggett, 2002]. In 2009 Son of Witz and David Stockhoff argued over whether Silk is a Christ figure in any doctrinal sense [Son of Witz, 2009][David Stockhoff, 2009], and Adam Thornton and David Stockhoff raised an Odin reading [Adam Thornton, 2009][David Stockhoff, 2009].

From 2010 onward the debate split between Christian and non-Christian readings. James Wynn proposed a Gnostic Simon Magus and Helena retelling [James Wynn, 2010][James Wynn, 2010], while Lee Berman argued for Dionysian or pre-Christian readings and Gerry Quinn and Daniel Petersen defended Christian primacy [Lee Berman, 2012][Gerry Quinn, 2012][Daniel Petersen, 2012]. Marc Aramini and Lee Berman also argued that Silk is a saint rather than a Jesus figure [Marc Aramini, 2011][Lee Berman, 2014]. The question of whether Severian or Silk fits the Christ role best was taken up repeatedly, with Lee Berman and Darrell Burgan giving opposing answers [Lee Berman, 2011][Darrell Burgan, 2014].

For

  • Silk's life tracks the Gospel events, including death, resurrection and ascension, according to Jordan's numbered parallels [James Jordan, 1996].
  • Silk's temptations match Christ's in the text, such as eating at Auk's expense, the leap from a high place, and accepting Viron's crown [Ranjit Bhatnagar, 1997].
  • Silk's disappearance at the end of Exodus parallels Christ's ascension in Acts 1 [Michael Straight, 1998].
  • Silk's pursuit of Hyacinth reads as the Good Shepherd seeking the lost sheep and as Christlike redeemed love [James Jordan, 1996][John Watkins, 2009].
  • Silk's story follows Moses and the Exodus, with Silk leading people out of bondage toward a promised land [Alex David Groce, 1999][Matthew Keeley, 2007].

Against

  • Severian fits the Christ-figure role better than Silk, since he heals, raises the dead and sustains the sun [Lee Berman, 2011][Darrell Burgan, 2014].
  • Silk is a saint and a would-be holy man who lacks Severian's healing power, so he is not Jesus [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • Silk's world is pre-Christian and his Jesus-like visions do not show Christ, since Urth still awaits salvation [Lee Berman, 2011][Lee Berman, 2014].
  • Silk resembles Odin, with a raven and one eye, rather than Christ, and Adam Thornton and David Stockhoff raised that reading [Adam Thornton, 2009][David Stockhoff, 2009].
  • Silk is a good man in a bad religion, and Wolfe's Christian hints are more typological than Christ-identifying [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2012].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Christ and Moses readings have been argued repeatedly since 1996 with no consensus, and the 2009 to 2014 posts split between Christian, Gnostic, Dionysian and Odin readings.
