Olivia and her suitors

Datable facts about Aunt Olivia (her death) and the identities of the women around her.

44 threads · 56 posts · for 64 / against 8 · first raised 1998-05-17 by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes
Main voices: Michael Andre-Driussi (26), Stone Ox (13), Roy C. Lackey (7), Marc Aramini (7), Adam Stephanides (5), Dave Tallman (4)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the list by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes in 1998, who proposed that Olivia in Peace marries the suitor who tells the best story [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 1998]. Its datable and identity claims then spread through several strands: Alex David Groce questioned whether Eleanor Bold's name alludes to Trollope and later argued that she is named after Barchester Towers characters [Alex David Groce, 1998] [Alex David Groce, 1999]. C R Culver proposed that Weer's parents died and Olivia covered it up [C R Culver, 1998], while Groce disputed the murder-by-Weer idea and argued the Professor killed Olivia with his car [Alex David Groce, 1998].

The dating debate ran from 2000 to 2002. Michael Andre-Driussi argued that Olivia died in 1928, using Den's ages and the warlord-period dating [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000] [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000]. Andre-Driussi's 2002 timeline placed the picnic and prank in order [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2002], and Stone Ox then revised the death to spring 1929 using the Depression and the Frazier-Lemke Act [Stone Ox, 2002]. Causes of death were argued in parallel: Stone Ox proposed that Peacock killed Aunt Olivia, citing his car and gnome imagery [Stone Ox, 2002], Roy C. Lackey argued Peacock killed her at Julius's behest [Roy C. Lackey, 2002], and Andre-Driussi floated a hit-and-run [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2002]. Adam Stephanides disputed Lackey's blackmail reading of Weer and Smart [Adam Stephanides, 2002].

The identity claims became the most persistent strand. Dave Tallman proposed in 2008 that Eleanor Bold murdered Alden Weer as an agent of divine justice [Dave Tallman, 2008], then revised this to a prophecy [Dave Tallman, 2008]. Marc Aramini argued from 2011 to 2013 that Eleanor Bold is Mrs. Porter, citing matching details and claimed authorial patterns, and other posters called the link too thin [Marc Aramini, 2011] [Marc Aramini, 2011] [Marc Aramini, 2013]. The egg-bidding argument was contested directly: Stone Ox argued Olivia bids up the egg as a scheme against Macafee [Stone Ox, 2002], Stephanides argued she bids to keep her independence [Adam Stephanides, 2002], and Stone Ox conceded the checkbook point while keeping a scheme reading [Stone Ox, 2002].

For

  • Danehy-Oakes proposed that Olivia marries the suitor with the best story, grounded in the party scene [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 1998].
  • Andre-Driussi dated Olivia's death to 1928 using Den's ages and the warlord-period dating [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000] [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000].
  • Stone Ox argued Peacock killed Aunt Olivia, citing his car, his absence from the funeral and gnome imagery [Stone Ox, 2002].
  • Aramini argued Eleanor Bold is Mrs. Porter, citing matching hair colour, beauty and trees and claimed authorial patterns [Marc Aramini, 2011] [Marc Aramini, 2013].
  • Stone Ox argued Olivia deliberately bids up the egg so Macafee must buy it, with Em Lorn's cash rule as the mechanism [Stone Ox, 2002] [Stone Ox, 2008].

Against

  • Groce argued Weer did not kill anyone, and that the Professor killed Olivia with his car, as a rival reading [Alex David Groce, 1998].
  • Stephanides argued Olivia bids for the egg to stay independent and questioned whether a woman alone in 1929 would have had a checkbook [Adam Stephanides, 2002].
  • Stone Ox conceded the checkbook point, which weakened the purely scheming reading of the egg bidding [Stone Ox, 2002].
  • Stephanides disputed the blackmail reading, arguing Weer would have secured wealth sooner and Smart had time to remove him [Adam Stephanides, 2002].
  • Tallman's birthday-date argument was disputed by Lackey, who cited the August birthday party [Dave Tallman, 2008].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the dating, the cause of death and the Eleanor Bold identity each drew different answers from different posters, and no consensus was reached.
