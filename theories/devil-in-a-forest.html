The Devil in a Forest's politics

The Devil in a Forest makes a political point about redistribution.

16 threads · 23 posts · for 12 / against 10 · first raised 1998-10-02 in the thread "the Devil in a Forest"
Main voices: James Wynn (7), David Stockhoff (4), Josh Geller (3), James Jordan (2), Mo Holkar / UKG (2), Marc Aramini (2)

The debate

The redistribution reading was first raised on 16 May 2009, when James Wynn argued that The Devil in a Forest critiques socialism and big government, pointing to Wat robbing the rich, the villagers' reliance on law, and the king's indifference [James Wynn, 2009]. The same day Wynn disputed Robin Dunn's claim that Wolfe favors authoritarian good kings, citing the tradesmen and Wolfe's rulers as not officials [James Wynn, 2009]. David Stockhoff answered the next day, reading the novel as an indirect fable against economic and social interference with an ideal king rather than a good one [David Stockhoff, 2009], and then argued that it demolishes the Robin Hood ideal, showing benevolent redistribution as neither simple nor likely [David Stockhoff, 2009].

Wynn refined the case over the following days. He called Wat a demagogue whose scheme of paying the poor from robberies allegorizes how welfare is sold, and the staged robbery breaks the villagers' coalition [James Wynn, 2009]. He then argued that the novella makes a libertarian argument against redistribution, comparing taking property by majority vote to personal theft [James Wynn, 2009]. Stockhoff agreed the libertarian-leaning reading was apt, while suggesting a single knight riding in would have made the story openly propagandistic [David Stockhoff, 2009]. A separate post under Stockhoff's name read Wat as a demagogue illustrating how redistributive systems corrupt through impersonal transactions [David Stockhoff, 2009].

A propaganda strand ran alongside the political argument. Josh Geller called the novel subtle propaganda that critics deny because good propaganda makes people deny it [Josh Geller, 2009], and later repeated that readers hostile to its agenda would miss it [Josh Geller, 2009]. James B. Jordan read it as propaganda for a Christian worldview through its ending miracle [James Jordan, 2009]. In June 2009 Wynn said he had first found the politics unsubtle but changed his mind after discussion, citing the Robin Hood homage [James Wynn, 2009]. In 2012 Mo Holkar rejected the common right-wing parable reading in favour of mistaken perception and the loss of childhood [Mo Holkar / UKG, 2012].

For

  • Wynn argued the villagers' reliance on law becomes a worse predator than the highwayman, citing Wat robbing the rich and the king's indifference [James Wynn, 2009].
  • Wynn read Wat as a demagogue whose scheme of paying the poor from robbery allegorizes how welfare is sold to voters [James Wynn, 2009].
  • Wynn argued that taking property by majority vote is no more ethical than personal theft, using a historical example of lawful nationalization [James Wynn, 2009].
  • Geller and Wynn described the novel as subtle propaganda whose political content readers fail to notice or deny [Josh Geller, 2009] [Josh Geller, 2009] [James Wynn, 2009].
  • Stockhoff accepted the libertarian-leaning reading as apt, adding that group consensus does not make an action right [David Stockhoff, 2009].

Against

  • Stockhoff read the novel as a fable against government beneficence, with the king as an ideal rather than a good ruler [David Stockhoff, 2009].
  • Stockhoff argued the novel demolishes the Robin Hood ideal, showing benevolent redistribution as neither simple nor likely [David Stockhoff, 2009].
  • Stockhoff countered Wynn's libertarian point by arguing that governments redistribute wealth routinely [James Wynn, 2009].
  • Holkar rejected the right-wing parable reading and placed the book's theme in mistaken perception and leaving childhood [Mo Holkar / UKG, 2012].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the reading was argued repeatedly between Wynn, Stockhoff, Geller and Jordan from 2009 onward, with no consensus reached and later posters such as Holkar rejecting it.
