Father Inire is Dionysus

Father Inire is modelled on, or is a version of, Dionysus (or another Greek god).

12 threads · 23 posts · for 12 / against 11 · first raised 2006-07-24 by b sharp
Main voices: Lee Berman (16), David Stockhoff (2), António Pedro Marques (2), b sharp (1), Andrew Mason (1), James Wynn (1)

The debate

The theory that Father Inire is a version of Dionysus, or of another Greek god, was first raised on the list by b sharp in July 2006, who compared Inire to pagan gods and to Apollo and Jupiter while disputing the idea that he serves the autarchs [b sharp, 2006]. The idea was then taken up at length by Lee Berman from December 2010 onward, who proposed Inire as a shapeshifting alien modelled on Greek gods and linked him to Dionysus through Pan, Inuus, Typhon and the thyrsus [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2011][Lee Berman, 2011]. Andrew Mason and James Wynn disputed the Dionysus link early on, Mason framing it as part of a Dionysus/Christ analogy and Wynn calling it an extrapolation beyond the Pan and Faunus link [Andrew Mason, 2010][James Wynn, 2011].

The discussion grew from October 2011, when Berman posted a run of proposals tying Inire to Talos, Malrubius, Cyriaca's uncle, Catherine and the Dionysian Father Inire-Hethor identity [Lee Berman, 2011][Lee Berman, 2011][Lee Berman, 2011]. David Stockhoff cautioned that Wolfe selects threads rather than webs and suggested Appian as a better candidate for Cyriaca's uncle [David Stockhoff, 2011]. Antonio Pedro Marques argued that Inire's alien status is explicit in the text, citing his abnormal longevity compared with his kind, while Sergei Soloviev argued that figures presented as aliens such as Inire show no human emotions [António Pedro Marques, 2011][Sergei SOLOVIEV, 2011]. Later Berman went further, proposing Inire, Typhon and Tzadkiel as aspects of Wolfe himself as demiurge, which drew objections [Lee Berman, 2011].

The main long threads, 'Father Inire as Dionysus' and 'Father Inire Theory', ended in impasse, with posters including Gerry Quinn, Jeff Wilson and Sergei Soloviev pressing that the resemblances were too thin and that the theory was not testable against the text [David Stockhoff, 2011][António Pedro Marques, 2011]. Berman's last post on the list proposed a pre-Christian trinity linked to Dionysus, with no agreement reached [Lee Berman, 2012].

For

  • Lee Berman argued that Inire is a shapeshifting alien modelled on Greek gods and that Boatman is a form of Inire, grounded in Borski's Moses reading and Foila's shapeshifter clues [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Berman linked Inire to Dionysus through Pan, Inuus and the etymology of Inuus, endorsing Nick Lee's view that Dennis means Dionysus [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Berman argued that shapeshifters in the Sun books keep a constellation of marks while changing form, so human-scale objections do not hold, citing Semele, Foila and Hildegren's card [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Berman argued that Talos's play reveals the actress playing Holy Katharine is Catherine, Severian's mother, grounded in the oval-faced woman on the Path of Air [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • b sharp compared Inire to pagan gods and cited the three autarchs who travelled to Yesod, Appian's mirrors and Ymar's parable [b sharp, 2006].

Against

  • Andrew Mason argued that the Dionysus references form part of a Dionysus/Christ analogy, pointing to a Long Sun and Short Sun god whose blood is wine [Andrew Mason, 2010].
  • James Wynn argued that linking Inire to Dionysus goes beyond the Pan and Faunus link, and that Wolfe's sign system may be used differently in later books [James Wynn, 2011].
  • David Stockhoff argued that the Father Inire as Dionysus idea is too deep and mythology-driven, and that Wolfe selects threads rather than webs [David Stockhoff, 2011].
  • Antonio Pedro Marques argued that a human-seeming biography would be wasted effort if Inire were an alien, and that Inire's alien status is explicit in the text [António Pedro Marques, 2011][António Pedro Marques, 2011].
  • Sergei Soloviev argued that figures presented as aliens such as Inire show no discernible human emotions, unlike Hethor and the boatman [Sergei SOLOVIEV, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the theory was pressed by Lee Berman for years, but posters including Stockhoff, Marques, Soloviev and Wynn disputed it, and the long threads ended in impasse without agreement.
