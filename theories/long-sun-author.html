Horn wrote the Long Sun

Horn (with Nettle) wrote the Book of Silk, so the Long Sun has a narrator who is a character, revealed by the sudden "I" in Exodus.

18 threads · 16 posts · for 8 / against 6 · first raised 1996-12-06 by Peter Cash
Main voices: Doug Eigsti (3), James Jordan (2), Peter Cash (1), David Adrian (1), CoxRathvon (1), Dan Rabin (1)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the list by Peter Cash on 1996-12-06, who complained that the end of Exodus from the Long Sun, revealing Horn as narrator, felt like a surprise trick and asked whether earlier hints had been missed [Peter Cash, 1996]. Within weeks David Adrian argued that the reveal was signposted earlier, in the Silk and Potto surrender scene [David Adrian, 1996], and Doug Eigsti defended Wolfe's single-clue method while proposing Horn as the hidden narrator of Nightside the Long Sun [Doug Eigsti, 1996]. CoxRathvon grounded the reading in the sudden first-person I in chapter 13 of Exodus [CoxRathvon, 1997], and Fernando Gouvea cited Horn's skill at imitating Silk's voice [Fernando Q. Gouvea, 1997].

The argument then broadened from narrator to author. Dan Rabin pointed to the Exodus name list calling Horn the colonist who wrote the Book of Silk [Dan Rabin, 1997]. Eigsti later argued from nautical vocabulary on Lake Limna, where the narrator uses Silk's terms before using Auk's, and from flattering Horn cameos in scenes he is absent from [Doug Eigsti, 1997] [Doug Eigsti, 1997]. In 1999 James Jordan and Alice Turner doubted that Horn and Nettle could have written the Long Sun without supernatural help, and Turner compared them to the Gospel writers [James Jordan, 1999] [Alice Turner, 1999]. In 2001 Jordan called the revelation annoying [James Jordan, 2001], and Michael Straight argued that the ball-game passage in On Blue's Waters belonged to Horn's persona and that Nettle wrote the flattering Horn descriptions [Michael Straight, 2001]. Michael Andre-Driussi cited the authorship chain to compare the Short Sun with fan fiction [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2001].

Later posts mostly restated the attribution. Don Doggett argued in 2005 that nothing in the Long Sun and Short Sun books can reliably be attributed to Silk, since Horn and Nettle wrote them [Don Doggett, 2005]. Jordon Flato in 2009 proposed that the Long Sun and Short Sun swap narrative voices [Jordon Flato, 2009]. António Pedro Marques in 2011 challenged the Horn and Nettle authorship of the Pike's ghost story and asked what evidence supports the theory [António Pedro Marques, 2011]. Terence Nickolette's undated repeated question was answered by Michael Straight, who pointed to Silk having talked about his enlightenment afterward, so Horn knows it only from Silk's retelling. Several threads, including the merging and Horn Help discussions, ended without resolution.

For

  • Horn's sudden first-person I at the end of chapter 13 of Exodus marks him as narrator, per CoxRathvon [CoxRathvon, 1997].
  • The name list in Exodus calls Horn the colonist who wrote the Book of Silk, per Dan Rabin [Dan Rabin, 1997].
  • Narration in the Lake Limna scenes uses Silk's nautical vocabulary before Auk's, which Eigsti read as a deliberate clue from Horn [Doug Eigsti, 1997] [Doug Eigsti, 1997].
  • Horn appears flatteringly in scenes he is absent from, which Eigsti read as authorial cameos [Doug Eigsti, 1997].
  • Silk talked about his enlightenment afterward, so Horn knows it only from Silk's retelling, per Michael Straight.

Against

  • Peter Cash found the Exodus reveal a surprise trick and asked whether earlier clues existed [Peter Cash, 1996].
  • James Jordan and Alice Turner doubted Horn and Nettle could have known and written the Long Sun without supernatural help [James Jordan, 1999] [Alice Turner, 1999].
  • Michael Straight argued the ball-game memory is Horn's persona and that Nettle wrote the flattering Horn passages, and that Silk would not recall a childhood game [Michael Straight, 2001].
  • James Jordan found the authorship revelation annoying and not credible for the children's sections [James Jordan, 2001].
  • António Pedro Marques asked what evidence supports the Horn and Nettle authorship and proposed Marrow's slave for the Pike's ghost story [António Pedro Marques, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Horn-as-narrator and Horn-as-author readings were argued by a few posters with textual evidence, but the list never settled them, and several threads ended without agreement.
