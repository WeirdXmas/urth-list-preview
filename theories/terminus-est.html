Terminus Est and its meaning

The sword's name ("This is the line of division") also means "it is finished", Christ's last words, and its history (a gift to Palaemon) matters.

38 threads · 50 posts · for 31 / against 17 · first raised 1996-11-27 by Ranjit Bhatnagar
Main voices: Michael Andre-Driussi (4), Matthew Malthouse (4), Gwern Branwen (4), Peter Cash (3), Kevin J. Maroney (3), Lee Berman (3)

The debate

The theory that the sword's name Terminus Est means "It is finished" and echoes Christ's last words was first raised on the list by Ranjit Bhatnagar, who proposed the "Azoth" punning idea in the 1996 whorl posts [Ranjit Bhatnagar, 1996], and then taken up by Peter Cash, who playfully adopted the translation [Peter Cash, 1996]. The Christ-parallel was pressed most explicitly by Nigel Price in November 1998, who argued that the sword name echoes the words from the cross and made Severian messianic [Nigel Price, 1998]. Kevin J. Maroney then corrected him that John 19:30 uses "consummatum est" in the Vulgate, not a phrase matching the sword's name [Kevin J. Maroney, 1998], and Nigel Price conceded the Vulgate point while defending the translation [Nigel Price, 1998].

The discussion then divided between Peter Cash and Michael Straight, who argued the English phrase "It is finished" carries the sense regardless of the Vulgate [Peter Cash, 1998][Michael Straight, 1998], and Maroney, who argued that the Latin phrase probably lacks that connotation and that the reading is an overinterpretation [Kevin J. Maroney, 1998]. Jonathan Laidlow then argued the title might echo Jesus' words in another language [Jonathan Laidlow, 1998], and Mary Whalen offered an astronomical reading of the title as a planet's terminator without textual evidence [mary whalen, 1998]. Marc Aramini later read the sword as a cross in the crucifixion-imagery argument [Marc Aramini, 2002], and Bennewitz later called it a crucifix symbol [Bennewitz, 2008].

The sword's history, the other half of the statement, was debated separately. Michael Andre-Driussi guessed in 1998 that it was a gift to Palaemon from Thrax exultants [Michael Andre-Driussi, 1998], while Matthew Malthouse proposed a noble's gift [Matthew Malthouse, 1998] and Peter Cash joked it was military surplus [Peter Cash, 1998]. Michael Andre-Driussi later proposed that Pas obtained it during exile [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2000], and thalassocrat asked how Palaemon came to own it [thalassocrat, 2008]. The thread on Palaemon ended with competing proposals and no recorded reply [thalassocrat, 2008].

For

  • Ranjit Bhatnagar listed Azoth and related names as possible choices in the Long Sun books, with the alchemical sense of azoth linked to the sword's name [Ranjit Bhatnagar, 1996].
  • Peter Cash argued that Wolfe could have meant "terminus est" as "it is finished" even though the Vulgate reads "consummatum est" [Peter Cash, 1998].
  • Michael Straight argued the English "It is finished" can mean "It is over", "It is the end" and "It is completed", so the link does not depend on the Vulgate [Michael Straight, 1998].
  • Nigel Price cited the Latin dictionary sense of terminus as "end, conclusion" and linked the Typhon temptation to Christ's wilderness temptation [Nigel Price, 1998][Nigel Price, 1998].
  • Jonathan Laidlow argued the title may deliberately echo Jesus' dying words in another language given the book's eclectic religious symbolism [Jonathan Laidlow, 1998].

Against

  • Kevin J. Maroney reported that the Vulgate at John 19:30 reads "consummatum est", not a phrase matching the sword's name [Kevin J. Maroney, 1998].
  • Kevin J. Maroney argued the Latin phrase probably does not carry the sense of "finished" that the English translation suggests, comparing Kafka's "arrested" [Kevin J. Maroney, 1998].
  • Nigel Price admitted the Vulgate reading weakened his earlier theory that the name means "It is finished" [Nigel Price, 1998].
  • Kevin J. Maroney questioned whether "it is finished" is a sound translation of terminus est, and noted that foreign words carry different connotations [Kevin J. Maroney, 1998][Kevin J. Maroney, 1998].
  • Michael Straight's argument that the Church's tradition makes scholarly guessing about the Vulgate unpersuasive was countered by the point that Jesus, the Greek New Testament and the Vulgate are all translations [Michael Straight, 1998][Peter Cash, 1998].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Christ-parallel was reframed rather than settled, and the list ended with the Vulgate correction standing, while the sword's history remained a set of unresolved guesses.
