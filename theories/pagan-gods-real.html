Pagan gods exist in Wolfe's Christian cosmos

Wolfe's pagan gods are real beings (angels, demons, lesser powers) within a Christian cosmos; he baptizes non-Christian worldviews rather than denying them.

73 threads · 102 posts · for 69 / against 33 · first raised 1997-06-18 by Michael Straight
Main voices: Lee Berman (43), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (7), Marc Aramini (7), b sharp (6), Daniel Petersen (5), Andrew Mason (4)

The debate

The earliest post on the list that bears on this theory is Michael Straight's 1997 argument that Latro's and Silk's pagan gods are false not because they lack existence or power but because they are not good [Michael Straight, 1997]. Peter Cash's 1997 reading that Wolfe's Christianity is indirect and paganism predominates [Peter Cash, 1997] and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's 2001 treatment of "god" as a general category of powerful beings [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2001] came early too. The idea of pagan gods as angels or demons was argued in 2003, when Danehy-Oakes proposed that the gods of the Soldier books and Sun books are bounded creatures [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2003] and later argued that Latro's gods are self-serving demons [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2003].

A 2005 thread turned on what Wolfe himself had said. James Wynn corrected David Lebling's claim that Wolfe called the pagan gods demons, noting that Paul made that suggestion and that Wolfe said the gods existed [James Wynn, 2005]. Lebling had argued the Overcyns were godlike [Dave Lebling, 2005], and Andy Robertson described the gods as a lower order beneath one perfect god [Andy Robertson, 2005]. From 2010 Lee Berman made the theory central, quoting Wolfe's 1996 Jordan interview that pagan gods were real [Lee Berman, 2010] and proposing that pagan gods and fallen angels are one kind of being [Lee Berman, 2010]. Many of his readings of individual characters, such as Father Inire and Severian, drew sharp objections from Marc Aramini [Marc Aramini, 2014] and from Daniel Petersen, who argued Wolfe fulfils pagan gods in Christ [Daniel Petersen, 2011].

The debate was long and repetitive. Berman, Andrew Mason, Mark Millman and Jeff Wilson argued in various ways that the gods are real or that Wolfe takes them seriously [Jeff Wilson, 2013] [Mark Millman, 2014] [Lee Berman, 2014], while Wynn and Antonin Scriabin's allies argued that real gods can be non-heretical [James Wynn, 2012]. Several threads ended in impasse and drift, including "Wolfe as Heretic" and "christ, already", and the list never reached agreement on what Wolfe's remarks meant.

For

  • Lee Berman cited Wolfe's 1996 Jordan interview that pagan gods were real as the basis for treating them as fallen or misunderstood angels [Lee Berman, 2010]
  • Jeff Wilson argued that the pagan gods in the Latro books are meant to be literally real, citing Wolfe's stated belief in lesser gods [Jeff Wilson, 2013]
  • Lee Berman argued that Wolfe believes the pagan gods were real and part of God's plan [Lee Berman, 2011]
  • James Wynn argued that the pagan gods Wolfe believes in can be real without heresy, citing C.S. Lewis and Paul [James Wynn, 2012]
  • Andy Robertson described Wolfe's pagan gods as a lower order of divine beings beneath one perfect god, linked to Tolkien's Valar [Andy Robertson, 2005]

Against

  • Michael Straight argued the pagan gods are false because they are not good rather than because they do not exist [Michael Straight, 1997]
  • Danehy-Oakes argued that Latro's pagan gods are self-serving demons, contrasting Wolfe with Lewis's angelic view [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2003]
  • James Wynn corrected the claim that Wolfe called the pagan gods demons, noting Paul made that suggestion [James Wynn, 2005]
  • Marc Aramini argued that Severian's miracles make him an explicitly Judeo-Christian savior rather than a pagan god [Marc Aramini, 2014]
  • Andrew Mason argued that pagan gods alluded to in the Urth books are not literally present [Andrew Mason, 2011]

Where it stands: Still disputed: the list never settled whether Wolfe's pagan gods are real beings in a Christian cosmos, and discussion ended in impasse and drift.
