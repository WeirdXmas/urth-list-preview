Lake Diuturna is Lake Titicaca

Lake Diuturna, with its floating islands, is a far-future Lake Titicaca.

9 threads · 11 posts · for 6 / against 4 · first raised 1997-04-10 by Alice Turner
Main voices: Lee Berman (4), Mitchell A. Bailey (2), Alice Turner (1), Charles Dye (1), Greene, Carlton (1), John Smith (1)

The debate

The idea that Lake Diuturna is a far-future Lake Titicaca surfaces on the list in 1997, when Alice Turner's map of Severian's journey placed Diuturna over Rome, so the Titicaca reading is not her point. The first explicit Titicaca argument is Carlton Greene's 1999 post [Greene, Carlton, 1999], which cited the lake's resemblance to Titicaca, its reed islands and boats, and a shore-versus-lake rivalry. Mitchell A. Bailey followed the same month [Mitchell A. Bailey, 1999], framing the Lake Diuturna culture as a far-future survival of South American peoples and proposing two reinterpretations of where the lake lies. John Smith's 2008 post [John Smith, 2008] tied the floating islands to Titicaca's islands via Darwin's account.

The discussion grew most sharply in 2010 and 2011, when the question shifted from the lake's setting to the eel Abaia. Lee Berman argued Abaia is a freshwater eel living in Lake Diuturna [Lee Berman, 2010], and Jeff Wilson disputed this, saying Juturna and Baldanders end up as sea-dwellers [Jeff Wilson, 2010]. The thread summaries record that the debate also produced a splitting-survivor revision by Berman and speculation from Wilson about stranded megatherians.

In November 2011 Berman argued that the slavery and human sacrifice in the books reflect pre-Christian Andean cultures [Lee Berman, 2011], while Jerry Friedman countered that the Parana and Lake Diuturna do not match well and that grasslands and cold islands are common worldwide [Jerry Friedman, 2011]. The South America thread records that Friedman admitted he had overstated the link, and that Berman later held that the Nessus-Buenos Aires name mainly points to the Hercules myth, but the two still disagreed on how close the correspondence is. Berman's final reply [Lee Berman, 2011] insisted on Titicaca as a source and dismissed Friedman's doubts without resolving them.

For

  • Carlton Greene argued the lake's resemblance to Titicaca, its floating reed islands and reed boats, and a shore-lake rivalry support the reading [Greene, Carlton, 1999].
  • Mitchell A. Bailey supported a far-future survival of South American peoples with Uros islands, Inca and Quechua origin legends, and Tiahuanaco ruins [Mitchell A. Bailey, 1999].
  • John Smith suggested the floating islands may derive from Titicaca's real islands, as Darwin describes them [John Smith, 2008].
  • Lee Berman cited the lake's name, the town name Murene and Baldanders living under the lake to link Abaia to Diuturna [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Lee Berman cited Inca brutality and Apu Punchau's tribe in support of Andean sources for the sacrifices [Lee Berman, 2011].

Against

  • Jeff Wilson argued that Juturna's claim that her kind swim between the stars and the varied salt and pH conditions make the lake an unlikely home for Abaia [Jeff Wilson, 2010].
  • Jerry Friedman argued the Parana and Lake Diuturna do not match well and that grasslands and cold islands are common worldwide [Jerry Friedman, 2011].
  • Jerry Friedman said South American geography is a poor key for reading the books and admitted he had overstated the link [Jerry Friedman, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Titicaca reading was argued by a few posters, most strongly by Lee Berman, and Jerry Friedman and Jeff Wilson pressed objections without a shared conclusion.
