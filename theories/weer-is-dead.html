Weer is dead in Peace

Alden Dennis Weer, the narrator of Peace, is dead (a ghost) as he tells the story.

53 threads · 66 posts · for 48 / against 16 · first raised 1997-04-16 by James Jordan
Main voices: Michael Andre-Driussi (15), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (8), Roy C. Lackey (7), William H. Ansley (5), C R Culver (5), Stone Ox (5)

The debate

The theory that Weer is dead in Peace was first raised on the list by James Jordan in April 1997 [James Jordan, 1997], with Reusch asking the same question shortly after [Reusch, 1997]. In 1998 Damien Broderick argued that the novel is Weer's memory palace inside his skull [Damien Broderick, 1998], and William H. Ansley argued that Weer is dead and largely unaware of it [William H. Ansley, 1998]. Ansley also pressed the point in a dispute with C R Culver, who doubted that the internal evidence settled the matter [C R Culver, 1998][William H. Ansley, 1998].

The death reading returned in 2000, when Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that the frame is Weer's retelling after the elm on his grave blows over [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2000], and Robert Borski quoted a Wolfe interview saying Weer is dead and reliving his life as a ghost [Robert Borski, 2000]. Adam Stephanides disputed the reading, arguing that the text proves only that someone died [Adam Stephanides, 2000], while Michael Straight insisted that the elm passages make the death too striking to be accidental [Michael Straight, 2000]. Roy C. Lackey and Michael Andre-Driussi carried the reading into 2001 and 2002, with Lackey arguing that Weer must have known from the first page [Roy C. Lackey, 2001] and Andre-Driussi offering ghost-based readings of the coldhouse [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2002].

Later discussion moved to the Bierce source story, with Christopher Keiser proposing that Weer's ghost lives in the far future [Christopher Keiser, 2009], and to Marc Aramini, who treated Weer's death as a settled hidden fact in the Long Sun and Short Sun debates [Marc Aramini, 2010][Marc Aramini, 2011]. Roy C. Lackey later argued against exotic readings and for a straightforward dream frame [Roy C. Lackey, 2005].

For

  • James Jordan first argued that Weer is definitely dead, drawing on a claimed personal conversation with Wolfe [James Jordan, 1997].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes grounded the death reading in elm-tree lifespans, Weer's evasions about Bobby Black and Sherry Gold, and the intercom ending [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2000].
  • Robert Borski quoted Wolfe's 1983 THRUST interview saying Weer is dead and reliving his life as a ghost, freed by the felled tree [Robert Borski, 2000].
  • Roy C. Lackey argued that the fallen elm planted by Eleanor Bold implies that everyone Weer knew has died [Roy C. Lackey, 2001].
  • Michael Straight argued that the two elm-tree passages in Peace point to Weer being dead [Michael Straight, 2000].

Against

  • C R Culver argued that the internal evidence does not settle the death question and that rereading may change the view [C R Culver, 1998].
  • Adam Stephanides argued that the text proves only that someone died, not that it was Weer [Adam Stephanides, 2000].
  • Michael Andre-Driussi rejected the claim that Sherry Gold's death sets off a posthumous stroke, arguing the post-stroke scenes are dream-like [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2002].
  • Adam Stephanides questioned whether Wolfe's interview statements should count as evidence, since they may conflict with his other remarks [Adam Stephanides, 2000][Adam Stephanides, 2000].
  • Michael Andre-Driussi argued that Weer's physical ailments and axe-chopping are inconsistent with being dead, and offered readings of purgatorial discomfort instead [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2001].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the death reading has many supporters across the list's history, but posters including Culver, Stephanides and Andre-Driussi kept challenging the textual proof, so no consensus was reached.
