Silk belongs to the Order of Seekers for Truth and Penitence

Silk (and the torturers of the New Sun) belong to the Order of the Seekers for Truth and Penitence, linking the two series.

9 threads · 11 posts · for 6 / against 4 · first raised 1997-06-14 by Michael Andre-Driussi
Main voices: mary whalen (2), Michael Andre-Driussi (1), Wesley Parsons (7065) (1), Joel Priddy (1), James Jordan (1), Andrew Bollen (1)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the list in June 1997, when Michael Andre-Driussi answered a batch of questions and named Silk as a member of the Order of Seekers [Michael Andre-Driussi, 1997], and Wesley Parsons then argued that the Order is a specific organisation, so Silk or Severian must be or become a torturer [Wesley Parsons (7065), 1997]. James Jordan, in the same week, proposed the Order is the Catholic Church with Silk a true member and the torturers an antichrist copy [James Jordan, 1997]. The idea was revived in July 1998 by mary whalen, who relayed Wolfe's reported answer and argued the torturers are a far-future form of Catholic priests [mary whalen, 1998][mary whalen, 1998].

The main debate came in July 2003, when Andrew Bollen read Wolfe's Q&A wording as tying Silk's synthetic church to the Seekers while the torturers' guild stayed a distinct secular body [Andrew Bollen, 2003]. Roy C. Lackey countered that Silk belongs to the Seekers in a religious sense, citing Sev and Silk breaking the creed 'Torturers obey' through Thecla's death and Silk's affair with Hyacinth [Roy C. Lackey, 2003]. Later, Dan'l Danehy-Oakes extended the Seeker link to a masked assassin [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2006], and in March 2012 Gerry Quinn disputed Craig Brewer's religious reading, calling the name an ironic guild label [Gerry Quinn, 2012]. In 2019 Gem Caccetta applied the Seeker reading to Silk in The Book of the Long Sun, comparing augurs' black clothing with the torturers' cloak [Gem Caccetta, 2019].

Across the threads, posters including Matthew Malthouse argued the guilds resemble police or intelligence bodies or are lay inheritors of religious organisations, and Jason Voegele backed the priesthood reading with the broken-pews passage [mary whalen, 1998]. Michael Straight argued Wolfe's 'Yes, Silk' answer plays on ambiguity rather than proving a priesthood. The discussion of Silk's parents also touched the Seeker link, with James Jordan reading the guild name as a parallel to sacrifice and the Eucharist.

For

  • Wolfe's reported answer names Silk as a Seeker for Truth and Penitence, which posters took as literal evidence [Wesley Parsons (7065), 1997][mary whalen, 1998].
  • The Order is a specific organisation rather than a pun, so Silk or Severian must belong to the torturers' guild [Wesley Parsons (7065), 1997].
  • The torturers are a far-future Catholic priesthood, shown by black garb, religious titles and the loss and return of women members [mary whalen, 1998][mary whalen, 1998].
  • The broken-pews passage in Shadow XI.10 supports a church reading of the torturers [mary whalen, 1998].
  • Silk and Sev breach the creed 'Torturers obey', which Lackey reads as Silk belonging to the Seekers in a religious sense [Roy C. Lackey, 2003].

Against

  • The name is an ironic label for a secular guild, with the torturers' history secular apart from a patron saint many real guilds have [Gerry Quinn, 2012].
  • Wolfe's 'Yes, Silk' answer plays on ambiguity rather than proving a priesthood [mary whalen, 1998].
  • Andrew Bollen read the Q&A wording as tying Silk's church to the Seekers while leaving the torturers' guild distinct and secular [Andrew Bollen, 2003].
  • The women members, separate chapel and lack of theological function make the guild look like police or intelligence, per Matthew Malthouse's thread argument.
  • Unexplained time gaps cannot carry the priesthood theory, so the reading relies on gaps in the text [mary whalen, 1998].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Seeker link was argued from 1997 to 2019 with no consensus, and the religious versus secular reading of the guilds stayed contested.
