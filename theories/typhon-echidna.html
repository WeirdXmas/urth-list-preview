Typhon and Echidna are siblings

Typhon and Echidna, husband and wife in the Long Sun, are also brother and sister.

18 threads · 34 posts · for 15 / against 18 · first raised 2000-04-11 by Timothy Reilly
Main voices: Lee Berman (11), Gerry Quinn (8), David Stockhoff (5), Marc Aramini (4), António Pedro Marques (3), James Wynn (2)

The debate

The theory was first worded on the list by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes in 2006, who called Typhon Echidna's husband and sibling while defending names as symbolic [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2006]. Earlier posts had touched the family without the sibling claim: Timothy Reilly argued in 2000 that Echidna refers to the Greek monster [Timothy Reilly, 2000], and James Wynn in 2003 placed Silk as a son of Typhon and Echidna [James Wynn, 2003]. Gerry Quinn disputed a Typhon-sister reading in 2010 and 2011 [Gerry Quinn, 2010] [Gerry Quinn, 2011].

The debate sharpened in October 2011. Lee Berman proposed that Echidna is Typhon's sister, citing the mythological pairing and dynastic sibling marriage [Lee Berman, 2011]. Quinn answered that the two were not incestuously related and that Typhon would have avoided inbreeding [Gerry Quinn, 2011]. David Stockhoff backed Berman with a gothic-incest reading [David Stockhoff, 2011], while Marc Aramini argued that Wolfe does not map Greek myth onto family ties and that the text must state such a mapping [Marc Aramini, 2011] [Marc Aramini, 2011]. Berman and Stockhoff pressed the implied-backstory case further [David Stockhoff, 2011] [Lee Berman, 2011], and Quinn called the names raw material rather than hidden plot [Gerry Quinn, 2011].

The theory returned in later threads. Berman linked Echidna to the Cumaean in 2013 and 2014 [Lee Berman, 2013] [Lee Berman, 2014], and Quinn rejected that identification as resting on weak clues [Gerry Quinn, 2014]. Posts on the list's thread summaries repeatedly show the same split without new textual evidence, and Antonio Pedro Marques reported Quinn's view that the sibling reading has no direct textual support while noting it would explain why the two stay married [António Pedro Marques, 2011] [António Pedro Marques, 2011].

For

  • Reilly's Greek-myth reading of the name Echidna gives the opening identification of the monster figure [Timothy Reilly, 2000].
  • Berman argued that the mythological pairing and dynastic sibling marriage support Echidna as Typhon's sister [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Berman argued that the clues from the snake-woman, Typhon, Scylla and Sphinx fit together as a puzzle, and that incest was normal for pagan gods [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Stockhoff argued that incest works as a covert gothic device, and that Echidna's pagan monster family implies incest [David Stockhoff, 2011] [David Stockhoff, 2011].
  • Marques reported that a sibling relation would explain why the couple stayed married rather than through ritual incest [António Pedro Marques, 2011].

Against

  • Quinn argued that Typhon would have avoided inbreeding and that Rigoglio's marriage is not remarked upon [Gerry Quinn, 2011].
  • Quinn argued that Typhon's offspring and Echidna's family are not a mythic pantheon and that Echidna is not his sister [Gerry Quinn, 2011].
  • Aramini argued that Wolfe does not map Greek myth onto the family ties, so the siblings reading lacks textual basis [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • Aramini argued that authorial intent cannot support a myth-based sibling reading unless the text states the mapping explicitly [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • Quinn argued that the names and classical sources are raw material rather than hidden clues to the relationship [Gerry Quinn, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed and unresolved: the sibling reading was argued repeatedly across 2011 and later with no consensus, and the record shows most of the thread participants defending entrenched positions.
