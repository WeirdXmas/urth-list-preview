Dr. Talos's play is prophetic

Dr. Talos's play (Eschatology and Genesis) foretells the end of the books, unknown to its author.

36 threads · 39 posts · for 22 / against 15 · first raised 1997-03-04 by Alice Turner
Main voices: Lee Berman (8), Tony Ellis (6), Alice Turner (5), Roy C. Lackey (4), b sharp (2), Dave Tallman (2)

The debate

The idea that Dr. Talos's play is a prophecy surfaces early on the list. Alice Turner first argued in 1997 that the play and the whole novel work as a creation myth and prophecy [Alice Turner, 1997]. Jim Henley in 1999 read the play as meant to convey the eschatological nature of the New Sun, while doubting the flood logic [Jim Henley, 1999]. Roy C. Lackey countered in 1999 that Severian's account of Urth's destruction lies in his future, so it cannot be the play's source [Roy C. Lackey, 1999].

The explicit 'unknowingly prophetic' card appears in May 2011, when Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that the play should be taken seriously as unknowing prophecy, comparing Talos to the High Priest who prophesies without knowing it [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011]. James Wynn backed this by comparing the play to the two inset tales, 'The Story of Frog' and 'The Story of the Student and His Son' [James Wynn, 2011]. Andrew Mason later suggested in October 2011 that Father Inire's accurate account of Urth's last day may derive from Severian's book rather than Canog's, and weighed prescience against other explanations [Andrew Mason, 2011]. Lee Berman linked the play's Flood and Nephilim material to a cleansing reading, citing the play as narrated by Gabriel [Lee Berman, 2012] and an earlier Flood-purpose reading [Lee Berman, 2010].

Opposition came mainly from Roy C. Lackey, who argued in 2006 that the play cannot have known about the woman on the Path of Air and proposed a closed causal loop in which Canog's book and the play cite each other [Roy C. Lackey, 2006]. In 2008 he also argued that the play is unreliable as a guide to events because it mixes Canog's book, Genesis and Talos's imagination [Roy C. Lackey, 2008]. Dave Tallman, responding to the same objections, defended using the play's details as raw material [Dave Tallman, 2008].

For

  • Alice Turner argued the novel works as a prophecy as well as a creation myth [Alice Turner, 1997].
  • Jim Henley argued the play was meant to convey the eschatological nature of the New Sun [Jim Henley, 1999].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued the play should be read as unknowingly prophetic, like the High Priest in the Gospel [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011].
  • James Wynn supported the reading by comparing the play to the two inset tales and complaining they are dismissed as larks [James Wynn, 2011].
  • Andrew Mason suggested Talos's accurate foretelling of Urth's last day reflects prescience or a source in Severian's book [Andrew Mason, 2011].

Against

  • Roy C. Lackey argued Talos's play cannot have known about the woman on the Path of Air, so it cannot be the source [Roy C. Lackey, 2006].
  • Roy C. Lackey argued Severian's account of Urth's destruction is in his future when he writes, so it cannot be the source of the play [Roy C. Lackey, 1999].
  • Roy C. Lackey argued the play is unreliable for particulars because it blends Canog's book, Genesis and Talos's imagination [Roy C. Lackey, 2008].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes's reading was itself offered as a dispute against Lee Berman's claim that the Flood conclusion is incontrovertible [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011].
  • Dave Tallman argued the play's back-story is Talos's invention built from Severian's raw material, which weakens a strict prophecy reading [Dave Tallman, 2008].

Where it stands: Raised repeatedly since 1997 but never widely accepted; the unknowingly prophetic version was pushed by only a few posters in 2011 and the list left it disputed and unresolved.
