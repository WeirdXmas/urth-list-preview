Typhon grafted his head onto Piaton's body

Typhon's second head belongs to Piaton, the original owner of the body, onto which Typhon's head was grafted to preserve his life.

47 threads · 92 posts · for 48 / against 44 · first raised 1997-01-22 by Ranjit Bhatnagar
Main voices: Larry Miller (11), Roy C. Lackey (10), Lee Berman (10), James Wynn (8), Gerry Quinn (8), David Stockhoff (7)

The debate

The theory that Typhon grafted his head onto Piaton's body is raised early on the list by Ranjit Bhatnagar, who in January 1997 argued Pas is Typhon, citing Typhon's account of his head grafted onto the slave Piaton's body in The Sword of the Lictor [Ranjit Bhatnagar, 1997]. Joel Priddy followed in May 1997 by asking when the grafting happened, weighing three possibilities and grounding them in Severian's return from Yesod [Joel Priddy, 1997]. David Wells in 2000 defended the grafting speculation while doubting Typhon's stated reason for it [David Wells, 2000]. Thread summaries and posts from 2002 onward focus on the Whorl's timeline, with Michael Andre-Driussi and Roy C. Lackey debating whether Typhon was scanned before or after the graft [Michael Andre-Driussi, 2002] [Roy C. Lackey, 2002] [Roy C. Lackey, 2002].

The discussion grew sharply in 2005 and 2010 to 2011, when posters turned the grafting into parentage theories. Don Doggett proposed Piaton as Severian's father [Don Doggett, 2005], and James Wynn argued Severian is a clone of Piaton, with Piaton as Typhon's son Hieros [James Wynn, 2005]. Roy C. Lackey rejected the son theory by arguing Typhon's children were too young to rebel [Roy C. Lackey, 2005], and Wynn conceded the point [James Wynn, 2005]. Later Ashley Crill, Marc Aramini, and Lee Berman argued over whether Silk is the child of Piaton, Typhon, or Kypris, with Aramini disputing Crill's First Exodus reading on physical grounds [Ashley Crill, 2011] [Marc Aramini, 2011] [Lee Berman, 2011].

In the Pas-icon debates of late 2011, Gerry Quinn argued Silk's head would replace Piaton's in the two-headed Pas, while David Stockhoff countered that Silk replaced Typhon's head and that Piaton was only a host [Gerry Quinn, 2011] [David Stockhoff, 2011]. Larry Miller proposed Typhon was a non-human monster who grafted onto Piaton's body as a last-ditch extension of life [Larry Miller, 2011] [Larry Miller, 2011], while Sergei Soloviev argued Typhon is human and grafted to keep his face and power, citing Typhon's own account [Sergei SOLOVIEV, 2011].

For

  • Typhon's own account in The Sword of the Lictor describes his head grafted onto the slave Piaton's body, which Bhatnagar cited as direct support [Ranjit Bhatnagar, 1997].
  • Typhon's grafting was a desperate measure to keep his face and rule, which Larry Miller and Sergei Soloviev read from Typhon's account [Larry Miller, 2011] [Sergei SOLOVIEV, 2011].
  • Typhon's need for healing and his nerve grafts were cited as reasons for the arrest of Severian, as Lee Berman argued [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Pedro Pereira argued Typhon grafted his face onto a slave's body and got outside help, citing the Book of the New Sun account [Pedro Pereira, 2007].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued the two-headed state was meant to be temporary while the nervous system adapted to Piaton's body [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2001].

Against

  • Jeff Veyera argued Pas's two heads are a simple visual signal to readers about Typhon as builder, not evidence of a complicated graft plan [Jeff Veyera, 2002].
  • Roy C. Lackey argued Piaton was unlikely to be scanned and was superfluous, given Typhon's congenital blindness limiting the scan [Roy C. Lackey, 2002].
  • Larry Miller questioned the timing of the graft, asking where the text gives a one-year interval before Typhon's death [Larry Miller, 2011] [Larry Miller, 2011].
  • Gerry Quinn and Marc Aramini argued Silk is not Piaton, citing Silk's slender build against Piaton's strength [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • James Jordan judged the information insufficient to settle whether the second head is Piaton's [James Jordan, 2002].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the grafting itself is widely cited from The Sword of the Lictor, but posters never agreed on its timing, its purpose, or whose head is which, and the parentage and Pas-icon offshoots remained unresolved.
