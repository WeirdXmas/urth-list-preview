Where Inire's name comes from

Inire's name is drawn from Inuus (an epithet of Faunus), Janus, or is an anagram (Fenrir).

29 threads · 47 posts · for 37 / against 11 · first raised 1998-10-15 by mary whalen
Main voices: Lee Berman (21), James Wynn (7), John Watkins (4), mary whalen (2), Jeff Wilson (2), António Pedro Marques (2)

The debate

The idea that Inire's name is a Roman or classical borrowing was first raised by mary whalen in October 1998, who proposed that Inire derives from Latin in and ire and links to Ianus [mary whalen, 1998][mary whalen, 1998]. Nicholas Gevers the same year read the name as 'Father In Ire' [Nicholas Gevers, 1999], and Robert Borski suggested Abraxas [Robert Borski, 1999]. The Inuus derivation surfaced in 2008 when Dave Tallman offered Inuus as a better source than Janus [Dave Tallman, 2008], and it was pressed hardest from July 2010 onward, when Lee Berman proposed that Inire's name comes from Inuus, an epithet of Faunus [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2010].

The Fenrir anagram was the most contested alternative. James Wynn proposed that Fr. Inire is an anagram of Fenrir [James Wynn, 2010], and John Watkins combined Father, initiate and Fenrir [John Watkins, 2010]. Ryan Dunn disputed it on letter count, leaving one I over [Ryan Dunn, 2010], while David Stockhoff supported a Fenrir echo [David Stockhoff, 2010]. John Watkins disputed the Inuus reading as a coincidental pun [John Watkins, 2010], and António Pedro Marques defended the Inuus-inire link with a Wikipedia citation [António Pedro Marques, 2010].

Over the following years Lee Berman repeatedly restated the Faunus, Inuus and Dionysus line, extending it to Pan, monkeys, Palaemon and Ossipago [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2011][Lee Berman, 2013], while James Wynn kept pressing Fenrir and a Ragnarok mapping [James Wynn, 2011]. Jerry Friedman and others objected that the reading went beyond what the text showed [Lee Berman, 2011]. Thread summaries record that the debate never reached agreement and drifted into other topics [mary whalen, 1998].

For

  • mary whalen proposed the Ianus derivation in 1998 by tracing Latin in and ire [mary whalen, 1998][mary whalen, 1998].
  • Dave Tallman offered Inuus as a better source for the name than Janus [Dave Tallman, 2008].
  • Lee Berman argued that Inire's name derives from Inuus, an epithet of Faunus, citing the Inuus entry and sexual 'entering' [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2010].
  • James Wynn argued that Fr. Inire is an anagram of Fenrir and that Inire is chained like Fenrir [James Wynn, 2010][James Wynn, 2010].
  • aaron agreed that the name's source meaning of entrance fits Inire's role with passages [aaron, 2013].

Against

  • Ryan Dunn showed the Fenrir anagram fails on letter count, since one I is left over [Ryan Dunn, 2010].
  • John Watkins argued the Inuus link is a coincidental pun and that Typhon already fills the Pan role [John Watkins, 2010].
  • thalassocrat rejected a Fenrir connection and saw no Latin inire behind the name [thalassocrat, 2010].
  • Jeff Wilson questioned whether the Fenrir and Inuus readings were recognizable and offered Entire as a hidden name [Jeff Wilson, 2008].
  • Jerry Friedman objected that the Inuus reading went beyond Typhon and was unsupported by the text [Lee Berman, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Inuus and Fenrir readings were argued repeatedly by a few posters, notably Lee Berman and James Wynn, and the list never reached agreement.
