Dionysus prefigures Christ

Wolfe uses Dionysus as a precursor of Christ.

7 threads · 9 posts · for 4 / against 5 · first raised 2010-12-02 by Andrew Mason
Main voices: Lee Berman (4), Andrew Mason (3), Daniel Petersen (1), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (1)

The debate

The idea was first raised on 2010-12-02 by Andrew Mason [Andrew Mason, 2010], who argued that Dionysus can be read as prefiguring Christ, noting that C.S. Lewis and Wole Soyinka read him that way, and that this undercuts the claim that the Outsider must be a lower deity than the Increate. Lee Berman responded in December 2010 that Wolfe's layered meanings support one another and that the origins of Severian, Typhon and Father Inire can be read through legendary models [Lee Berman, 2010]. Berman then proposed that the Outsider is a form of Dionysus, son of Thyone, and asked how this relates to the Christ parallels [Lee Berman, 2010]. Mason also pressed the point against Berman's reading of Father Inire as a negative Dionysus figure, arguing that the Dionysus references form part of a Dionysus/Christ analogy grounded in a god whose blood is wine and who imparts his spirit to drinkers [Andrew Mason, 2010].

The debate was sporadic. Berman floated a Dionysus or Jesus allusion in the staff and death of Zama, but admitted uncertainty and suggested staffs might simply be important [Lee Berman, 2011]. In 2012 Daniel Petersen agreed that Wolfe absorbs a pagan myth into the Christ story, treating Silk as a figure of Aristaeus and Dionysus [Daniel Petersen, 2012]. Mason replied that the Hierogrammates' era fixes the ambiguity in Urth and that Dionysus matters as a Whorl god and son of Pas, not as a Christ precursor [Andrew Mason, 2012].

In September 2014 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that the Outsider gives Silk a clear vision of Christ's passion, and that Dionysus, a dying-and-reanimated god, prefigures Christ [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2014]. The next day Berman disputed Marc Aramini's claim that Silk is a Christ figure, arguing that Silk and Dionysus rather than Christ figure in the Short Sun and that Urth is pre-Christian [Lee Berman, 2014]. Several longer threads on the Outsider, the Increate, Father Inire and the Wizard Knight cosmology repeated these splits without reaching agreement.

For

  • Mason argued that Lewis and Soyinka read Dionysus as prefiguring Christ, which undercuts the lower-deity reading of the Outsider [Andrew Mason, 2010].
  • Mason grounded a Dionysus/Christ analogy in the inhumi, a god in the Long Sun and Short Sun books whose blood is wine and who gives his spirit to drinkers [Andrew Mason, 2010].
  • Berman proposed the Outsider as a form of Dionysus, son of Thyone, and cited a Long Sun legend about a figure who drove moneychangers from a temple as a Christ parallel [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Petersen argued that Wolfe absorbs the pagan Aristaeus and Dionysus material into the Christian myth, which he treated as primary for a Catholic writer [Daniel Petersen, 2012].
  • Danehy-Oakes argued that the Outsider's vision of the passion and Dionysus's dying-and-reanimated pattern together make Dionysus a prefiguring of Christ [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2014].

Against

  • Berman argued that the Dionysus and Christ models are conflated with others such as Hercules/Jesus and Alexander/Typhon, and that interpretations are personal mythologies [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Berman tentatively raised a Dionysus or Jesus reading of Zama's death but admitted uncertainty and said staffs may simply be important [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Mason argued that the Hierogrammates' era fixes the future-or-past ambiguity and that Dionysus matters as a Whorl god and son of Pas, not as a Christ precursor [Andrew Mason, 2012].
  • Berman argued that Silk and Dionysus, not Christ, figure in the Short Sun, and that Urth is pre-Christian, so the Christ reading does not apply [Lee Berman, 2014].

Where it stands: Still disputed: raised by Andrew Mason in 2010 and revived by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes in 2014, it drew repeated disputes from Lee Berman and never reached agreement, with the threads ending in impasse.
