Where Seawrack's name comes from

Seawrack's name is a pun (shipwreck), a mishearing (Scylla) or drawn from the Irish silkie or Syrinx.

16 threads · 25 posts · for 19 / against 9 · first raised 2000-09-05 by Robert Borski
Main voices: Lee Berman (9), António Pedro Marques (4), b sharp (3), James Wynn (3), Robert Borski (2), Scott Wowra (2)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the Whorl list in September 2000, when Robert Borski proposed that Seawrack's real name was an Irish seaweed term, Screadhbhuidhe, and built his case on Horn's remarks in On Blue's Waters [Robert Borski, 2000] [Robert Borski, 2000]. Peter Westlake praised the proposal without adding evidence [Peter Westlake, 2000]. The question was reopened on Urth in March 2009, when John Smith suggested Sycorax [John Smith, 2009], b sharp proposed Sirenia and Shipwreck [b sharp, 2009], and David Stockhoff suggested the Irish silkie [David Stockhoff, 2009]. Other candidates that year included an East Asian origin from James Wynn [James Wynn, 2009], an alien non-human word from Jordon Flato [Jordon Flato, 2009], and Syrinx from Scott Wowra [Scott Wowra, 2009]. Stuart Hamm argued that the author made the name an unpronounceable alien word [Stuart Hamm, 2009], and Wowra later proposed an Ancient Greek source [Scott Wowra, 2009].

The debate became most intense in August and September 2010. Lee Berman argued that Seawrack is a shipwreck pun, reasoning from the silent-c pronunciation of Scylla and the sirens' role in wrecking ships [Lee Berman, 2010] [Lee Berman, 2010]. António Pedro Marques argued both for a misheard form of Scylla [António Pedro Marques, 2010] [António Pedro Marques, 2010] and against it, calling Berman's reasoning about an older source illogical [António Pedro Marques, 2010]. Berman also proposed Sirenia and Syrinx as possible real names [Lee Berman, 2010]. James Wynn tested Syrinx against a sound-alike clue and rejected it while still calling it the strongest candidate [James Wynn, 2010]. Dave Tallman proposed the Polish syrenka, the mermaid on Warsaw's coat of arms [Dave Tallman, 2010], and Wynn agreed with it [James Wynn, 2010]. Thalassocrat argued Horn named her after the seawrack plant he found on the island [thalassocrat, 2010].

Later posts kept the question open. Marques disputed the Screadhbhuidhe derivation on phonetic and naming grounds [António Pedro Marques, 2010]. Berman recommended Borski's book The Long and Short of It for its treatment of the Irish seaweed claim, while noting that it focuses on other Wolfe works [Lee Berman, 2011]. In 2012 Berman disputed Mark Millman's view that the pirate girl becomes Seawrack, arguing instead that the Mother grew Seawrack and suggesting a Siren wordplay [Lee Berman, 2012].

For

  • Borski argued that the name derives from the Irish seaweed Screadhbhuidhe, tracing sea-wrack through algae taxonomy and the OED [Robert Borski, 2000].
  • Stockhoff suggested the Irish silkie, a shape-changing seal-or-woman, as a source for the name [David Stockhoff, 2009].
  • Wowra argued that the name is drawn from the nymph Syrinx, citing the matching sound and her seductive quality [Scott Wowra, 2009].
  • Tallman proposed the Polish syrenka, linking the figure's missing arm to Horn's renaming of her [Dave Tallman, 2010].
  • Thalassocrat argued Horn named her after the seawrack he pulled from the ground, given her green hair and frailty [thalassocrat, 2010].

Against

  • Berman argued Seawrack is a shipwreck pun, since Horn would know the name Scylla and the sirens caused wrecks [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Marques argued Scylla's silent-c pronunciation sounds closer to shipwreck, and that Horn would have no need to convert Scylla into Seawrack [António Pedro Marques, 2010].
  • Marques argued the Screadhbhuidhe derivation fails on pronunciation and meaning, citing the lenited bhuidhe and the Vironese naming conventions [António Pedro Marques, 2010].
  • Hamm held that the author made clear the name is an unpronounceable word from a totally alien language [Stuart Hamm, 2009].
  • Flato argued the name is an unfamiliar non-human word, since she passes from the mother's world into humanity [Jordon Flato, 2009].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the list never reached consensus, and the candidates (Irish seaweed, silkie, syrenka, Syrinx, Scylla, shipwreck pun, alien word) remained in debate across the years.
