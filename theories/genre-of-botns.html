Science fiction or fantasy

The Book of the New Sun is science fiction disguised as fantasy (or the reverse), and what that means for reading it.

12 threads · 14 posts · for 5 / against 9 · first raised 1999-11-01 by Michael Straight
Main voices: Lee Berman (2), Daniel Petersen (2), Michael Straight (1), Jeremy Crampton (1), Jesper Svedberg (1), Lane Haygood (1)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the list by Michael Straight on 1999-11-01, who argued that The Book of the New Sun looks like science fiction but has too many wonders to be explained scientifically, and that the genre misdirection makes those wonders stronger [Michael Straight, 1999]. Jeremy Crampton answered the next day with the opposite claim, that the book is fantasy, not science fiction, because the science-versus-religion distinction is unstable within the text [Jeremy Crampton, 1999].

The debate resurfaced in 2008 and 2010 with different framings. David Stockhoff argued that Wolfe retells Christian myth as science fiction [David Stockhoff, 2008], while Lane Haygood placed the book in the Dying Earth subgenre as science fantasy, since magic is real and physical laws can be broken [Lane Haygood, 2008]. Ryan Dunn proposed a fantasy mystery in which Severian is steered by many characters [Ryan Dunn, 2010].

The sharpest exchanges came in December 2011, when Gerry Quinn, Lee Berman, Daniel Petersen and António Pedro Marques argued over whether the book is sword and sorcery and whether it has a science fiction feel. Quinn argued it is not really sword and sorcery [Gerry Quinn, 2011], Berman defended a broad fairy-tale and sword-and-sorcery reading and gnostic content [Lee Berman, 2011] [Lee Berman, 2011], Marques insisted on a real sword-and-sorcery element [António Pedro Marques, 2011], and Petersen argued for a science-fictional far-future world [Daniel Petersen, 2011] [Daniel Petersen, 2011]. Michael Thayer's 2013 post, which argued the book is far-future science fiction disguised as fantasy, was the last substantive contribution on the list [Michael Thayer, 2013].

For

  • The wonders in the book, such as the fountain, the claw and the mirrors, resist scientific explanation, which Straight took as a reason to read the genre misdirection as deliberate [Michael Straight, 1999].
  • Wolfe's central theme is myth and its approach to the Divine, so the Christ question becomes moot and the book retells Christian myth as science fiction, according to Stockhoff [David Stockhoff, 2008].
  • The Dying Earth setting, with magic and physical laws that can be broken, places the book closer to science fantasy than to science fiction, according to Haygood [Lane Haygood, 2008].
  • Petersen argued the book has a genuine science-fiction feel at first and builds far-future worlds rather than alternate sword-and-sorcery realms [Daniel Petersen, 2011] [Daniel Petersen, 2011].
  • Thayer argued that the book's things that look mythic are often technological, such as the castle tower as rocket and the Cumaen as alien, supporting far-future science fiction disguised as fantasy [Michael Thayer, 2013].

Against

  • Crampton argued that Ash's claim that science and religion are one faith makes the book's science-versus-fantasy line unstable, so it reads as fantasy [Jeremy Crampton, 1999].
  • Quinn argued the book is not really sword and sorcery, since the blade matters little and few fights occur, though swords and sorcery are present [Gerry Quinn, 2011].
  • Berman argued the book draws broadly on fairy tales, myths and sword-and-sorcery motifs, and that its gnostic religious content lies beneath that surface [Lee Berman, 2011] [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Marques argued that the book has a real sword-and-sorcery element, not just a passing feel [António Pedro Marques, 2011].
  • Dunn argued that Severian is a puppet manipulated by many characters, a fantasy-mystery reading that does not fit a science-fiction frame [Ryan Dunn, 2010].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the science fiction and fantasy readings were argued repeatedly from 1999 to 2013 without consensus, and the Gollancz poll thread ended with no resolution on where the novel belongs.
