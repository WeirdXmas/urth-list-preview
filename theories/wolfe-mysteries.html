Wolfe's puzzles have no final answers

Wolfe's mysteries are meant to stay open, or some apparent puzzles are mistakes; the opposing view is that every puzzle has an answer in the text.

76 threads · 62 posts · for 32 / against 28 · first raised 1997-06-15 in the thread "Wolfe's Answers"
Main voices: Lee Berman (8), Marc Aramini (7), James Wynn (4), Michael Straight (2), Alex David Groce (2), Roy C. Lackey (2)

The debate

The theory was first aired on the list by Michael Straight in 1997, who disputed calling Wolfe's works puzzle stories and argued for subtle telling that careful readers can mostly catch [Michael Straight, 1997]. Alex David Groce in 1998 argued that Wolfe deliberately withholds clues in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, so its mysteries cannot be reliably solved [Alex David Groce, 1998], and in 1999 Mitchell A. Bailey argued that a court case would undercut the novel's intended lack of an objective answer about the abos [Mitchell A. Bailey, 1999].

In the early 2000s the debate centred on whether the text itself settles puzzles. Thomas Bitterman and Tami Whitehead argued that Wolfe's puzzles show meaning lies in the text [Thomas Bitterman, 2003][Tami Whitehead, 2003]. In 2006 b sharp cited a McCaffrey interview in which Wolfe said his puzzles should be solvable [b sharp, 2006], while Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued Wolfe withholds answers and uses true statements misleadingly [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2006]. Roy C. Lackey and Adam Stephanides argued that Severian's and Weer's narratives leave errors or gaps that cannot be resolved [Roy C. Lackey, 2008][Adam Stephanides, 2000].

From 2010 to 2011 the sides hardened. Lee Berman argued that the Marsch puzzle cannot be settled and that Wolfe's asymptotic mysteries have no final answers [Lee Berman, 2011][Lee Berman, 2011], and Jack Smith doubted that the mysteries in the longer works have definite answers [Jack Smith, 2010]. James Wynn and Berman's own later post argued instead that single intended answers exist [James Wynn, 2010][Lee Berman, 2011], while Marc Aramini and Gerry Quinn argued that the short stories and most puzzles have correct solutions and adequate clues [Marc Aramini, 2011][Gerry Quinn, 2011]. Danehy-Oakes added that Wolfe seeks clarity rather than insoluble puzzles [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011]. Later, in 2014, Berman argued that Wolfe intends unsolvable mysteries [Lee Berman, 2014], Aramini argued that Wolfe withholds definitive answers [Marc Aramini, 2014], and Richard Simon argued that the best reading explains the most events [Richard Simon, 2014]. Aramini's 2018 post argued the puzzles are objectively solvable [Marc Aramini, 2018].

Many threads, including the Barrington interview, Wolfe's brilliance and Shaving Clones threads, ended in impasse, with posters citing the same Wolfe interview remarks to support opposite views.

For

  • Alex David Groce argued that Wolfe withholds clues in The Fifth Head of Cerberus, so its mysteries cannot be reliably solved [Alex David Groce, 1998].
  • Lee Berman argued that the Marsch puzzle cannot be settled because the evidence is contradictory and any conclusion rests on the reader's prejudices [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Jack Smith argued that Wolfe uses puzzles and narrative holes to recreate real-life uncertainty and doubted that the longer works' mysteries have definite answers [Jack Smith, 2010].
  • Lee Berman argued that Wolfe builds asymptotic mysteries with no final answers in The Book of the New Sun [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Dave Lebling and Craig Brewer argued that some apparent puzzles are careless errors or unintentional products rather than designed mysteries [Dave Lebling, 1999][Craig Brewer, 2008].

Against

  • Marc Aramini argued that Wolfe's short stories each have an interpretive key and correct puzzle solutions [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • Gerry Quinn argued that Wolfe's puzzles usually have explicit solutions and adequate clues, to be solved like detective stories [Gerry Quinn, 2011].
  • James Wynn argued that universal truths exist and that Wolfe fixed single answers to many open questions [James Wynn, 2010].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that the text is wholly textual and that Wolfe seeks clarity rather than insoluble puzzles [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2011].
  • Marc Aramini argued in 2018 that Wolfe's puzzles are objectively solvable and that the Long Sun and Short Sun resolve the New Sun ambiguity [Marc Aramini, 2018].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the list never converged, most threads ended in impasse, and posters on both sides repeatedly cited the same Wolfe interview remarks to support opposing views.
