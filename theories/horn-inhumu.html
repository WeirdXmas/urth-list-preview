Horn is an inhumu

Horn (or the Silk-Horn narrator) is himself an inhumu, or has been replaced by one.

59 threads · 71 posts · for 34 / against 27 · first raised 1999-04-10 by Michael Straight
Main voices: Nicholas Gevers (7), Michael Straight (6), Alice Turner (6), Adam Stephanides (6), Dennis G. Berdanis (5), Sheila Herndon (3)

The debate

The idea first appears on the list in August 2000, when Nicholas Gevers reported that John Clute's SF Weekly review of In Green's Jungles claimed it was reasonably clear that Horn or Silk is himself an inhumu [Nicholas Gevers, 2000]. Clute's private replies, relayed by Gevers, said he heard echoes of The Fifth Head of Cerberus and would reread it, and that the third volume was near [Nicholas Gevers, 2000] [Nicholas Gevers, 2000]. Within a day, several list members responded: David Lomax and Sheila Herndon proposed the idea, while Robert Borski, Alice Turner, Fernando Gouvea and Jim Henley disputed it [David Lomax, 2000] [Sheila Herndon, 2000] [Robert Borski, 2000] [Jim Henley, 2000].

The August 2000 thread, and later ones, produced a range of variants. Michael Purdy proposed that willing self-giving lets an inhumu take over a person [Michael Purdy, 2000]. Lomax suggested Horn's soul bolsters Silk's inhumu body [David Lomax, 2000], and James Jordan read the same transfer as a Christlike self-giving that teaches Horn the secret [James Jordan, 2000]. In February 2001, Horn's eating and his unexplained trip to Green were cited for the inhumu reading, while Peter Westlake rejected it because Horn eats and drinks [BraveSaintCroix, 2001] [Peter Westlake, 2001].

In 2002 the idea returned in the Urth list. Andy Robertson proposed that an inhumu drained Horn in the green jungle, so that Horn came to believe he was human; Michael Straight opposed this, arguing that inhumi do not take on victims' memories and that a dead inhumu body would have been noticed [Andy Robertson, 2002] [Michael Straight, 2002] [Michael Straight, 2002]. Stephen Case, Alice Turner, Jerry Friedman and Inire then debated the matter in July 2002, with Turner calling it a red herring and Friedman noting the list consensus leaned against it [Stephen Case, 2002] [Alice Turner, 2002] [Jerry Friedman, 2002] [Inire's biggest fan, 2002]. Larry Miller raised it again in 2011 without a direct answer [Larry Miller, 2011].

For

  • Horn's rare eating, Hoof's report that he only pretended to eat, and Silk's reluctance to eat were cited as signs of an inhumu [BraveSaintCroix, 2001] [Andy Robertson, 2002] [Stephen Case, 2002].
  • Oreb's ability to recognize inhumi and his fondness for Horn were offered as evidence, along with Horn's loyal and self-serving narration [David Lomax, 2000] [Inire's biggest fan, 2002].
  • Horn's warnings about detecting inhumi, his repeated mention of not eating, and the lack of comment on his appearance were cited by Sheila Herndon [Sheila Herndon, 2000].
  • The bleeding wounds in Silk's apparent suicide scene were read by Alex Groce as possibly the work of an inhumu, and Herndon proposed that an inhumu fed on Silk and that Horn was transported into its body [Alex David Groce, 2000] [Sheila Herndon, 2000].
  • Tom Foster and Michael Straight noted the sinister closing line of On Blue's Waters, which Straight thought could be Wolfe teasing readers with the suspicion [Tom Foster, 2002] [Michael Straight, 2002].

Against

  • Horn writes, rides, uses weapons, eats and is liked by Oreb, which Gouvea and Borski argued inhumi do not do [Fernando Q. Gouvea, 2000] [Robert Borski, 2000].
  • Peter Westlake argued Horn is not an inhumu because he eats and drinks, and because he shows Hide his teeth [Peter Westlake, 2001] [Peter Westlake, 2000].
  • Michael Straight argued that inhumi do not take on victims' memories, and that the Neighbor-ring retrieval in the lander makes a dead inhumu body implausible [Michael Straight, 2002] [Michael Straight, 2002].
  • Alice Turner argued that Horn has become a tolerant human, and that his hostility to Sinew comes from his aging body rather than an inhumu identity [Alice Turner, 2000] [Alice Turner, 2002].
  • Alex Groce called the inhuma reading pointless, over-clever and unsupported by evidence against the strong textual evidence [Alex David Groce, 2000].

Where it stands: Raised in August 2000 through John Clute's review and never confirmed by him in the posts; it was disputed by most respondents and recurred in 2001, 2002 and 2011, so the question was left open without consensus.
