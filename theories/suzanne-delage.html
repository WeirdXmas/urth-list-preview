What happened with Suzanne Delage

The unspoken event in "Suzanne Delage" is supernatural (she is a vampire or a ghost) or a repressed memory.

22 threads · 36 posts · for 22 / against 14 · first raised 1997-07-12 by Michael Andre-Driussi
Main voices: Gerry Quinn (7), Gwern Branwen (7), Marc Aramini (6), David Stockhoff (5), Michael Andre-Driussi (2), adam louis stephanides (2)

The debate

The supernatural and memory readings of "Suzanne Delage" first surface in July 1997, when James Jordan asked where the fantasy element lies and suggested a ghost story [James Jordan, 1997], and Michael Andre-Driussi argued the story resembles Peace and proposed a Proustian source [Michael Andre-Driussi, 1997]. In May 1998 Dave Lebling proposed a vampire explanation [Dave Lebling, 1998], and adam louis stephanides offered a ghost-lover reading and argued the narrator had forgotten something [adam louis stephanides, 1998][adam louis stephanides, 1998]. Peter Westlake found the vampire reading fitting but under-implied and preferred Alice's theory [Peter Westlake, 1998].

In 2001 Dan'l Danehy-Oakes linked the memory theme to Chesterton's The Man Who Knew Too Much [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2001][Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2001]. In December 2010 a long run of posts followed: Gerry Quinn proposed an inverted romance horror reading [Gerry Quinn, 2010], David Stockhoff a Proustian memory anecdote with cloning [David Stockhoff, 2010], David Duffy argued the narrator saw Suzanne as a youth [David Duffy, 2010], and Jerry Friedman a hidden chess and supernatural reading [Jerry Friedman, 2010]. Gwern Branwen disputed the missed-chances reading [Gwern Branwen, 2010], and Quinn later conceded supernatural forces could work with it [Gerry Quinn, 2010].

From October 2011 Marc Aramini argued for repressed memory and an unreliable narrator [Marc Aramini, 2011][Marc Aramini, 2011], while Quinn rejected any memory significance and called the Proust link an ordinary unmet figure [Gerry Quinn, 2011][Gerry Quinn, 2011]. Aramini repeated the repressed-memory case in 2014 [Marc Aramini, 2014][Marc Aramini, 2014], and António Pedro Marques called it a necessary first step [António Pedro Marques, 2014]. In August 2023 Gwern Branwen and Eric Bourland argued the lost sweetheart was taken by a vampire modelled on Dracula, citing the Edges commentary [Gwern Branwen, 2023][Eric Bourland, 2023], and Gwern developed the Dracula inversion in several later posts [Gwern Branwen, 2023][Gwern Branwen, 2023][Gwern Branwen, 2023][Gwern Branwen, 2023].

For

  • The narrator's lost sweetheart and forgotten extraordinary event are signalled by the name, the flu details and the title's stress on memory [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • The narrator's own remark that his history holds an incredible but not supernatural thread supports a realistic repressed-memory reading [Marc Aramini, 2014].
  • The story's meditation that an extraordinary experience is forgotten implies the narrator knew Suzanne when young [David Duffy, 2010].
  • The Pie Club is a victim-lure and the narrator's defeat mirrors Jonathan Harker's in Dracula [Gwern Branwen, 2023].
  • Le Guin's introduction and cover blurbs in Edges show the narrator has lost memory, and vampiric mind-control explains the lost sweetheart [Gwern Branwen, 2023][Eric Bourland, 2023].

Against

  • The name Suzanne Delage carries no real significance and could as well be Mary Macdonald [Gerry Quinn, 2011].
  • The story is about an ordinary unmet woman drawn from Proust, and the forgotten experience needs no supernatural explanation [Gerry Quinn, 2011].
  • Kidd's introduction should not guide interpretation of the story [Gerry Quinn, 2010].
  • Westlake found the vampire reading under-implied and preferred not to make Suzanne a vampire [Peter Westlake, 1998].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the memory and supernatural readings have been argued since 1997 without consensus, with Aramini defending repressed memory, Gwern and Bourland advancing the vampire reading in 2023, and Quinn and the thread's Factory Farmer dispute rejecting it.
