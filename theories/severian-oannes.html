Severian becomes a fish god like Oannes

Severian ends as a god of the sea or lake for the people of Ushas, like the Babylonian Oannes.

6 threads · 7 posts · for 6 / against 1 · first raised 2006-05-19 by Malcolm McGugan
Main voices: Lee Berman (3), Malcolm McGugan (1), Marc Aramini (1), Andrew Mason (1), David Stockhoff (1)

The debate

The idea that Severian becomes a fish god like Oannes was first raised by Malcolm McGugan in May 2006 [Malcolm McGugan, 2006], who floated it alongside the claim that Baldanders might be Abaia himself and framed it as a lake god. The question stayed largely dormant until late 2010, when Lee Berman tied the fish imagery around Pia's lake (Oannes, Murene, Diuturna) to a family-based reading of Severian's partners [Lee Berman, 2010]. Marc Aramini then argued most directly for the theory in January 2011, saying that by the end of Urth Severian has become the Oannes of his people and that fish imagery recurs through the Long Sun and Short Sun books [Marc Aramini, 2011].

The debate broadened in 2011 and 2012. Lee Berman disputed Aramini's emphasis on tree themes by arguing that sea and fish-god motifs matter as much [Lee Berman, 2011]. Andrew Mason read the Oannes passage in The Sword of the Lictor as a clue that Severian is a form of the Outsider [Andrew Mason, 2012], and David Stockhoff supported the wider idea that Dagon, Oannes and Pan are mermen, citing quoted sources [David Stockhoff, 2012]. Berman's September 2012 post was the most explicit version, proposing Severian as an ocean-borne fish god and linking Baldanders to the megatherians and godlings through the idea that great size marks godhood [Lee Berman, 2012].

Across the threads, the theory was rarely the main subject. The Fish and Cave thread saw David Stockhoff argue the fish symbol is so distorted it is hard to recognise, while Gerry Quinn questioned whether the text places Abaia in the sea at all [Lee Berman, 2010]. The Abaia thread and the Seawrack thread mostly discussed Abaia and the Mother rather than Severian's apotheosis, and the Oannes thread fragmented into arguments about the Increate and gnosticism. No post on the list reports a consensus.

For

  • Severian's ending as the Oannes of his people is argued from the book's closing material and from fish imagery recurring across the Sun books [Marc Aramini, 2011].
  • Oannes is treated as a manifestation of the Increate, and Severian's later claim to be Oannes is cited as a clue that he is a form of the Outsider [Andrew Mason, 2012].
  • Fish-god and sea themes, from giant eels to kelpie hierodules and mermaids, are argued to matter as much as tree themes, with Dionysus's sea origins as support [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • Dagon, Oannes and Pan are argued to be mermen, using quoted material on Rashi, Kimhi, Milton and Berossus [David Stockhoff, 2012].
  • Severian as an ocean-borne fish god is grounded in the Neighbors, the flooded planet and Wolfe's use of great size as a mark of godhood [Lee Berman, 2012].

Against

  • Gerry Quinn questioned whether the text ever places Abaia in the sea, which undercuts the fish-and-water reading [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • David Stockhoff argued the fish symbol is distorted beyond easy recognition, citing Wolfe avoiding an obvious Sunday school lesson [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Lee Berman's sister-and-river reading was disputed by Marc Aramini, though Berman's own family theme went beyond it [Lee Berman, 2010].
  • Berman's dispute with Aramini over tree versus sea themes leaves the weight of the fish imagery contested [Lee Berman, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed and never settled: the theory was raised by a few posters across scattered threads, with supporters and critics citing text and comparative myth without reaching agreement.
