Agia is a robot

Agia is (or becomes) an artificial being, perhaps a paracoita made for Hethor, which explains her survival and strength.

10 threads · 83 posts · for 35 / against 43 · first raised 2003-04-21 by Brett M. Grace
Main voices: David Stockhoff (26), Ryan Dunn (22), Jeff Wilson (8), Jerry Friedman (6), Paul B (4), Josh Geller (4)

The debate

The theory that Agia is a robot was first raised on the Urth list by Brett M. Grace in April 2003 [Brett M. Grace, 2003], though his version was that she is Hethor's paracoita, and Matthew Malthouse answered that she was a substitute for that lost companion [Matthew Malthouse, 2003]. The robot reading took off in June 2010, when Jeff Wilson suggested a metal plate or thick skull [Jeff Wilson, 2010] and Ryan Dunn argued from the sound of her head striking the wall, which he compared to a mason's hammer [Ryan Dunn, 2010]. Jerry Friedman disputed the metallic-sound reading by citing the text's comparison to a hammer tap [Jerry Friedman, 2010], and Dan'l Danehy-Oakes and Paul B. pressed back on the robot idea [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2010] [Paul B, 2010].

The debate spread over many posts in June 2010. David Stockhoff became the main proponent, arguing that the metallic head, her strength and her relation to Agilus point to a robot or made being [David Stockhoff, 2010] [David Stockhoff, 2010], and later proposed holy or gnostic readings and a Heirodule identity [David Stockhoff, 2010] [David Stockhoff, 2010]. Opponents such as Roy C. Lackey argued she is human, citing chem strength ratios, Silk's remark about her weight, and her eating and drinking [Roy C. Lackey, 2010]. Jeff Wilson argued that the aureole around her head is backlighting [Jeff Wilson, 2010], and David Stockhoff agreed the halo is a natural effect [David Stockhoff, 2010]. Ryan Dunn also linked her to Hethor's lost genicon poppet and the lemonwood box [Ryan Dunn, 2010] [Ryan Dunn, 2010].

The thread later split between paracoita, poppet, and human readings. Ryan Dunn and David Stockhoff discussed whether Hethor's doll is Agia, with Ryan citing her irises and Stockhoff disputing it [Ryan Dunn, 2010] [David Stockhoff, 2010]. In 2011 Lee Berman and Ryan Dunn revived the poppet idea [Lee Berman, 2011] [Ryan Dunn, 2011], and in 2012 Ryan Dunn passed on questions for Gene Wolfe about whether Agia is a robot or poppet [Ryan Dunn, 2012]. The discussion never reached agreement, and many posters, including António Pedro Marques, questioned whether fixing her nature explains anything [António Pedro Marques, 2010].

For

  • Jeff Wilson first suggested a robot with a metal plate in her head, after Ryan Dunn quoted the head-strike scene [Jeff Wilson, 2010].
  • Ryan Dunn argued the sound of her head against the wall was tinny and not like a human skull [Ryan Dunn, 2010] [Ryan Dunn, 2010].
  • David Stockhoff argued the sound of her head on stone, like solid metal, is a plain clue that she is a robot [David Stockhoff, 2010] [David Stockhoff, 2010].
  • Lee Berman argued her metallic head sound and her Asian features and resemblance to Agilus point to a construct or clone related to Agia [Lee Berman, 2011].
  • David Stockhoff argued that the Kim Lee Soong figures and the ninja weapons in the Antechamber support an oriental robot reading [David Stockhoff, 2010].

Against

  • Jerry Friedman noted the text compares the head sound only to a mason's hammer, not metal, and that Severian saw blood when Agilus was beheaded [Jerry Friedman, 2010] [Jerry Friedman, 2010].
  • Jeff Wilson argued the head sound resembles a baseball beaning and that her modesty and hunger tell against a sexbot robot [Jeff Wilson, 2010] [Jeff Wilson, 2010].
  • David Stockhoff argued the halo is backlight rather than a sign of machinery, citing a backlit cat photo [David Stockhoff, 2010].
  • Roy C. Lackey argued she cannot be a chem because her strength, weight, eating and drinking fit a human, and Silk said she was too heavy to lift [Roy C. Lackey, 2010].
  • David Stockhoff argued that a fully functional android would not boast of strength and a nuclear cell as Agia does in the text [David Stockhoff, 2010].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the robot reading was debated in many threads from 2003 to 2012 and never reached consensus, with most posters rejecting it or treating it as one unresolved possibility among several.
