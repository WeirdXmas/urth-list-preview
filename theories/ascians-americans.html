The Ascians are North Americans

The Ascians, with their Correct Thought, are the far-future descendants of North Americans (or of another modern state).

13 threads · 15 posts · for 6 / against 5 · first raised 1998-07-20 by mary whalen
Main voices: Lee Berman (2), mary whalen (1), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (1), Jeffrey Lefstin (1), mournings glory (1), Pedro Pereira (1)

The debate

The theory that the Ascians are North Americans was first raised on the list by mary whalen in 1998, who argued from the book's compass directions, the sun's rising and setting, and Severian's northward journey that the Commonwealth lies in South America and the Ascians in North America [mary whalen, 1998]. The question resurfaced in 2002 when Dan'l Danehy-Oakes disputed that Ascians come from under the equator, placing them north of it and calling their name a folk etymology [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2002]. Jeffrey Lefstin's 2006 question whether Ascian evokes Asian kept the North American framing in view [Jeffrey Lefstin, 2006].

The decisive turning point cited across the list was a Wolfe interview quoted by mournings glory in 2006, saying the Ascians were meant as a Turkish equivalent and the setting is South America with Byzantium as the model [mournings glory, 2006]. Later posters repeated this interview: Matthew King shared a version in 2008 and noted Thrace is not Byzantium [Matthew King, 2008], Roy Lackey quoted a McCaffery interview in 2010 [Roy C. Lackey, 2010], and an unnamed poster quoted it again in 2023 [Unknown, 2023]. Pedro Pereira argued for North America in 2007, placing the northern wars in Central America [Pedro Pereira, 2007].

The theory was also contested. Andrew Mason in 2011 argued that Ascians are North American and that Asians are a separate Xanthoderm people, citing Wolfe's insistence [Andrew Mason, 2011]. Lee Berman proposed instead that the name blends American and Asian, linking the Ascians to the United States and to a warning about socialism [Lee Berman, 2011]. Son of Witz countered that North American Ascians could descend from Asians despite Dollo's Law [Son of Witz, 2011], and David Stockhoff linked the Ascians to Chinese forces in Korea [David Stockhoff, 2011]. Gwern Branwen argued for a Chinese mandarin model instead [Gwern Branwen, 2009].

For

  • Wolfe's interview says the setting is South America and the Ascians stand for North Americans, as quoted by mournings glory [mournings glory, 2006] and repeated by Matthew King [Matthew King, 2008].
  • The compass directions, sun's rising and setting, and Severian's northward journey place the Commonwealth in South America and the Ascians in North America [mary whalen, 1998].
  • Pedro Pereira placed Nessus in South America and the northern wars in Central America, with the Ascians in North America [Pedro Pereira, 2007].
  • Roy Lackey argued the sun directions do not prove a Southern Hemisphere setting and cited a McCaffery interview placing the Ascians as North Americans [Roy C. Lackey, 2010].
  • An unnamed 2023 poster accepted the South America setting and the North American Ascian reading, citing the interview [Unknown, 2023].

Against

  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes disputed the equatorial origin and argued the name is a folk etymology rather than a clue to continent [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2002].
  • Andrew Mason argued that Ascians are a North American people distinct from Asians, rejecting an Asian reading for the woman Severian describes [Andrew Mason, 2011].
  • Son of Witz argued that North American Ascians could descend from Asians, since climate and diet can change appearance [Son of Witz, 2011].
  • Gwern Branwen read the Ascians as modeled on a Chinese mandarin bureaucracy, not on North Americans [Gwern Branwen, 2009].
  • David Stockhoff argued the Ascians owe more to Wolfe's Korean War experience than to North American origins [David Stockhoff, 2011].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Wolfe interview is widely cited in favour of the North American reading, but several posters offered rival Asian, mandarin, and Korean War readings without a settled consensus.
