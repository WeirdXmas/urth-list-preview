Chris is flawed, not evil

Chris is a morally complex narrator, neither simply good nor evil.

7 threads · 9 posts · for 5 / against 5 · first raised 2007-12-01 by thalassocrat
Main voices: Daniel Petersen (2), Andrew Bollen (2), thalassocrat (1), Fernando Q. Gouvea (1), Matthew Groves (1), Dave Tallman (1)

The debate

The theory that Chris is morally complex rather than evil is most clearly stated by Fernando Q. Gouvea, who in December 2007 argued that Chris in Pirate Freedom is a flawed 18th-century man whose faults show once civilization's veneer falls away [Fernando Q. Gouvea, 2007]. The same month thalassocrat took the opposite line, calling Chris a monster who knowingly sins and whose confession is worthless [thalassocrat, 2007], and Matthew Groves replied that Chris is not simply evil, comparing him to Silk and Able and reading his confession as knowing better than his era's norms [Matthew Groves, 2007].

The debate continued through 2008 and 2009. Dave Tallman argued that Chris began as a sinner but was redeemed [Dave Tallman, 2008], while Jerry Friedman tied Chris's view on not fighting molestation to Matthew 5:39 and Catholic teaching on self-defence [Jerry Friedman, 2009]. Henry Eissler defended the moral acceptability of Chris's plan to break his vow [Henry Eissler, 2009]. In November 2012 Daniel Petersen argued that Chris is more complex than a mob boss and resembles Severian more than Ed EDson's reading allows, pointing to the confessor's spiritual sensitivity [Daniel Petersen, 2012][Daniel Petersen, 2012]. Andrew Bollen countered that Chris knows good but chooses evil, citing the killing of Michet and Chris's remark that he would do it again [Andrew Bollen, 2012].

Several threads widened the question without settling it, including the Pirate Freedom revisited discussion of Chris's parentage, the YA thread in which Jonathan Goodwin and Mo Holkar read Chris sympathetically while John Watkins and Jerry Friedman found his choices dubious, and the PF review thread on whether the confession is credible.

For

  • Chris is a flawed 18th-century man whose faults are exposed when civilization's veneer falls away [Fernando Q. Gouvea, 2007]
  • Chris is not simply evil, and his confession shows he knows better than his era's norms, as with Silk and Able [Matthew Groves, 2007]
  • Chris began as a sinner but was redeemed, and his return to his family is defensible [Dave Tallman, 2008]
  • Chris's stance on not fighting molestation matches Catholic doctrine on self-defence, making him less likely to be an evil character [Jerry Friedman, 2009]
  • Petersen argues Chris is morally complex, neither simply good nor evil, and points to the confessor's spiritual sensitivity [Daniel Petersen, 2012]

Against

  • Chris is a monster who knowingly sins, killed Michet, and refused to undo it, so his confession is worthless [thalassocrat, 2007]
  • Chris knows good but chooses evil, and his confessions lack real repentance [Andrew Bollen, 2012]
  • Chris's plan to break his celibacy vow is disturbing, not a morally acceptable choice [Henry Eissler, 2009]
  • Chris's remark that he would do it again, tied to Ignacio's choice, shows deliberate evil [Andrew Bollen, 2012]

Where it stands: Still disputed: the list never reached consensus, and the discussion ended with posters restating their positions, with Petersen saying only that he would reread the book [Daniel Petersen, 2012].
