The Book of the New Sun is a theodicy

The books argue for God's justice in a world of suffering (or explicitly refuse to).

10 threads · 8 posts · for 0 / against 6 · first raised 1997-07-01 in the thread "Theodicy"
Main voices: Ori Kowarsky (2), Daniel Petersen (2), Dan'l Danehy-Oakes (1), Dave Lebling (1), Lane Haygood (1), Marc Aramini (1)

The debate

The earliest post in this record is by Ori Kowarsky on 1999-11-20 [Ori Kowarsky, 1999], who disputed the idea that The Book of the New Sun can be read as redemption, arguing that the Briah project and the replacement of humanity by the Green Man resemble Ushas's destruction and that providence stays unproven. Days later he asked Alex David Groce for the passage where the strongest justification of God's ways is made, and proposed that readers check the text themselves [Ori Kowarsky, 1999]. The question of whether the book is a theodicy at all was then reframed by Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, who in 2002 argued that Severian's miracles may be an attempt to justify Christ's behaviour and that the book is theodicy rather than allegory [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2002].

In 2010 Dave Lebling argued that the book is effectively five volumes on theodicy and divine purpose, citing The Book of the Long Sun and noting that the hieroi and Tzadziel are not God [Dave Lebling, 2010]. The question then moved largely to Silk's Long Sun books: Daniel Petersen asked whether Silk offers a free-will theodicy that still relies on strong sovereignty [Daniel Petersen, 2011], Lane Haygood argued that all evil falls within God's plan [Lane Haygood, 2011], and Petersen replied that the view is one of loving purpose rather than unknown purposes [Daniel Petersen, 2011]. The 2018 post by Marc Aramini is the latest on the list; he argued that Wolfe's handling of evil in the Typhon scene is orthodox and that naturalist readers see manipulation where the text shows freedom under duress [Marc Aramini, 2018].

The thread summaries add context without giving post ids, so they are not cited here: the 'Messianic' and 'mankind' threads pressed Ori Kowarsky and Alex David Groce on textual evidence, and the 'Typhon's concessions' thread saw Aramini and Danehy-Oakes dispute the Solar Cycle and Jesus's absence. The cards for this theory vary, some saying the book is a theodicy, others that it is not, which makes the disputed claim harder to pin down.

For

  • Dave Lebling argued that the book is effectively five volumes on theodicy and divine purpose, citing Long Sun [Dave Lebling, 2010].
  • Dan'l Danehy-Oakes argued that Severian's miracles may be an attempt to justify Christ's behaviour, supported by the Webster definition of theodicy [Dan'l Danehy-Oakes, 2002].
  • Marc Aramini argued that Wolfe's handling of evil and death in the Typhon scene is orthodox in its theodicy [Marc Aramini, 2018].
  • Daniel Petersen asked whether Silk offers a free-will theodicy that still relies on strong divine sovereignty [Daniel Petersen, 2011].
  • Lane Haygood argued that God as first cause is responsible for evil, so all evil is part of God's plan [Lane Haygood, 2011].

Against

  • Ori Kowarsky argued that the New Sun project is destructive and unjustified as redemption, resembling Ushas's destruction [Ori Kowarsky, 1999].
  • Ori Kowarsky argued that the novel's rationalist explanations leave providence unproven as theodicy [Ori Kowarsky, 1999].
  • Ori Kowarsky asked Alex David Groce to identify the passage with the strongest justification of God's ways, and asked readers to check the text [Ori Kowarsky, 1999].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the question was raised in 1999, argued through the early 2000s and again around Silk's Long Sun in 2011, and in 2018 Aramini still asserted an orthodox reading, with no consensus recorded.
