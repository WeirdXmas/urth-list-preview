Where the name Pas comes from

Pas is named from Pan (and pas, "all"), or from the demon Pazuzu.

19 threads · 32 posts · for 23 / against 8 · first raised 1998-07-15 by mary whalen
Main voices: James Wynn (9), Lee Berman (8), António Pedro Marques (3), mary whalen (2), Jack Smith (2), Andrew Mason (2)

The debate

The theory was first raised on the list by mary whalen in 1998, who argued that Pas comes from the Greek masculine word for all rather than from Pan [mary whalen, 1998]. Interest then lay dormant until James Jordan (2003) and James Wynn (2003) argued for Pan-based readings of Pas's role and death, citing Ovid and Plutarch [James Jordan, 2003][James Wynn, 2003]. The question of the name's source became heavily argued on the list from 2009 onward, when James Wynn tied Pas to Pan and Greek links [James Wynn, 2009][James Wynn, 2010]. Lee Berman's proposal that Pas derives from the Babylonian demon Pazuzu, a wind god, entered in July 2010 and recurred through 2010 [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2010].

The November 2010 wave was the most intense. Jack Smith proposed Pas as a corruption of Pan, quoting Scylla's line that the name meant god of everything [Jack Smith, 2010]. Andrew Mason countered that Pas is the masculine form of 'all' with a different origin from Pan, citing the same line [Andrew Mason, 2010]. António Pedro Marques doubted the Pan etymology and suspected an obvious link had been missed [António Pedro Marques, 2010][António Pedro Marques, 2010]. James Wynn argued for Pan with Plutarch and Herodotus references, while also weighing Pazuzu as possibly an afterthought [James Wynn, 2010][James Wynn, 2010]. Gerry Quinn supported Pan as a changed form, using the Allah-to-Ah Lah example [Gerry Quinn, 2010].

Later posts moved the theory into wider frameworks: Nick Lee proposed Phanes and Erikapaios as the Outsider's identity, linking Pas to them [Nick Lee, 2011], and Lee Berman linked Pas to Pan and Dionysus while suggesting Wolfe inserts himself as Pas [Lee Berman, 2013]. Several threads, including 'Wolfe's Puzzles' and 'Wind god', ended without agreement on the name's origin.

For

  • Scylla's line in Lake of the Long Sun chapter 11 that Pas means god of everything is cited to support a Pan connection [Jack Smith, 2010][Jack Smith, 2010]
  • Pan's death recorded by Plutarch and his appearance in Herodotus are cited as parallels to Pas's death and return [James Wynn, 2003][James Wynn, 2010]
  • Pazuzu, a Babylonian wind god and cognate of Typhon-Set, is proposed as the source given the wind imagery in Return to the Whorl [Lee Berman, 2010][Lee Berman, 2010]
  • Gerry Quinn argues names shift over time, so Pas may be a modified form of Pan, citing Allah-to-Ah Lah in Wolfe's text [Gerry Quinn, 2010]
  • James Wynn argues Wolfe deliberately layers many myths onto the New Sun, with Pas as an example of a Greek name meaning All [James Wynn, 2011]

Against

  • Mary Whalen argues Pas comes from the masculine Greek word for all, not the neuter pan or god Pan [mary whalen, 1998]
  • Andrew Mason argues Pan the god has a different origin and declension from Pas, so Pas is the masculine 'all' [Andrew Mason, 2010]
  • António Pedro Marques finds the Pan equation too simple and suspects something obvious has been overlooked [António Pedro Marques, 2010][António Pedro Marques, 2010]
  • James Wynn's own reading treats the Pazuzu link as possibly an afterthought, so the Pan source is not clearly preferred [James Wynn, 2010]
  • Jack Smith's proposed Pan corruption was challenged by Mason's claim that the masculine 'all' explanation fits the text [Andrew Mason, 2010]

Where it stands: Still disputed: the list never settled among the Pan, Pazuzu and masculine-'all' explanations, and posters continued to offer competing readings through 2013.
