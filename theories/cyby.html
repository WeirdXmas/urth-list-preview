Cyby is Wolfe's cameo

Cyby, Ultan's assistant in the library, is Wolfe's self-portrait, sitting at the feet of Borges.

11 threads · 15 posts · for 13 / against 1 · first raised 1998-08-26 by Dan Parmenter
Main voices: Adam Thornton (4), Dan Parmenter (2), Jeff Wilson (2), Geoff Alex Cohen (1), Alex David Groce (1), Matthew Malthouse (1)

The debate

The earliest post on the list on this theory is Dan Parmenter's 1998 post [Dan Parmenter, 1998], which argued that Borges shapes Wolfe's work and cited the Cyby-Ultan resemblance as one example. The specific cameo claim came in 1999, when Geoff Alex Cohen proposed that Cyby in the Library chapter stands in for Wolfe himself, grounding this in Cyby's physical description and in the apprentice-to-Borges symbolism [Geoff Alex Cohen, 1999]. Parmenter then offered Norman Thomas DiGiovanni as a possible model for Cyby, admitting his description was vague and that Wolfe was bald by then [Dan Parmenter, 1999]. Alex David Groce read Cyby as an authorial self-portrait of a humble apprentice [Alex David Groce, 1999], and Matthew Malthouse countered that this self-casting might show hubris [Matthew Malthouse, 1999].

The argument was revived in 2009 by Dave Lebling, who proposed Cyby as Wolfe's stand-in, quoting the physical description and noting Wolfe was about forty when the book appeared in 1980 [Dave Lebling, 2009]. Jeff Wilson disputed this, saying photos of Wolfe from that period did not fit Cyby's flat, square-faced description [Jeff Wilson, 2009]. Robert Borski had earlier argued that the man Severian describes in Citadel is Ouen, not the archive figure, using the balding head and blue eyes [Robert Borski, 1999]. Later posts by Adam Thornton and Stuart Hamm treated Cyby mainly as a playful Gary-Stu figure rather than arguing the cameo itself [Adam Thornton, 2007] [Stuart Hamm, 2007].

The main new evidence came in 2013, when Jeff Wilson stated that Wolfe has said his own insertion into the book is Cyby [Jeff Wilson, 2013]. This is the only post on the list that cites Wolfe to support the reading, and the record does not show whether that remark was checked. Thornton kept repeating the self-insertion reading in 2011 [Adam Thornton, 2011]. Across the years, the photo-based and description-based arguments remained in conflict without any settling evidence [Jeff Wilson, 2009] [Dave Lebling, 2009].

For

  • Cyby's physical description is said to match Wolfe around 1980, and Cohen takes this as the basis for the cameo reading [Geoff Alex Cohen, 1999].
  • Dave Lebling cites the same description and Wolfe's age in 1980 to argue Cyby is Wolfe's stand-in in the novel [Dave Lebling, 2009].
  • Alex David Groce reads Cyby as an authorial self-portrait of Wolfe as a humble apprentice, citing a half-remembered list comment [Alex David Groce, 1999].
  • Matthew Malthouse proposes Wolfe casts himself as Cyby sitting at Borges's feet, grounding this in Cyby's continued learning and inheritance of the master's mantle [Matthew Malthouse, 1999].
  • Jeff Wilson notes that Wolfe has said his own insertion into the book is Cyby, offering this as support [Jeff Wilson, 2013].

Against

  • Jeff Wilson argues that photos of Wolfe from that period do not match Cyby's flat, square-faced description [Jeff Wilson, 2009].
  • Robert Borski reads the man Severian describes in Citadel as Ouen, Severian's father, rather than Cyby, using the balding head and blue eyes [Robert Borski, 1999].
  • Dan Parmenter conceded that the DiGiovanni description is vague and Wolfe was bald by then, which weakens the physical match [Dan Parmenter, 1999].
  • Matthew Malthouse's reading suggests the self-casting might show hubris rather than modesty, which contests Groce's humble-apprentice framing [Matthew Malthouse, 1999].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the cameo was argued repeatedly on physical-resemblance grounds and challenged on photographic evidence, and the only cited author remark (Wilson's 2013 post) remained unverified on the list, so the list never settled it.
