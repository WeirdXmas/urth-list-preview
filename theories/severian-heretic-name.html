Severian's name points to a heresy

Severian's name recalls the Severians, an early Christian sect, or another saint or heresy, and the allusion is meant.

25 threads · 26 posts · for 18 / against 7 · first raised 1998-01-29 by Patrick O'Leary
Main voices: James Wynn (5), Lee Berman (5), Alice Turner (4), Gerry Quinn (2), Patrick O'Leary (1), Robert Borski (1)

The debate

The earliest post on the list is Patrick O'Leary's 1998 reading of Severian as a healer and saint, built on letter symbolism [Patrick O'Leary, 1998]. The first post that ties Severian's name to a heresy is Alice Turner's October 1998 quotation of the Oxford English Dictionary entry, which lists the Severians, a second-century Encratite or Gnostic sect, as a source [Alice Turner, 1998]. The same month Turner argued that a heretic's name suits a book full of saints' and Roman names, and that Dr. Talos's play is Manichaean in tone [Alice Turner, 1998]. Alex David Groce then offered the bishop Severian, not the Encratites, as the likelier source, citing the Catholic Encyclopedia [Alex David Groce, 1998].

The heresy reading was carried mainly by James Wynn, who in 2002 argued that the name derives from the Severians, a Gnostic sect linked to Tatian and the Encratites, and cited Irenaeus and Eusebius [James Wynn, 2002]. Turner backed him and thanked Crush for the Eusebius and Irenaeus passages [Alice Turner, 2002]. In 2005 Wynn widened the case, calling Severian a composite of heretical Christ figures [James Wynn, 2005] and arguing that the Sun books draw on Gnostic ideas with Severian named after a Gnostic cult [James Wynn, 2005]. In a survey of possible sources he called the Gnostic link the strongest, while listing saints, a bishop and Severan emperors [James Wynn, 2005].

Jerry Friedman proposed in 2002 that the name instead comes from one of several Catholic saints named Severian, checking a saints index [Jerry Friedman, 2002]. Later posts reused the name question in other threads without new textual evidence, and the posts record no decisive turning point. Most later uses of the name appear in threads on family, twins and Christology rather than on the heresy itself.

For

  • Turner's OED entry gave the Encratite and Gnostic Severians as the sense of the name [Alice Turner, 1998].
  • Turner argued that a heretic's name suits a book with saints' and Roman names and a Manichaean-style play [Alice Turner, 1998].
  • Wynn tied the name to the Severians and the Encratites, quoting Irenaeus and Eusebius [James Wynn, 2002].
  • Wynn argued the Sun books are a Gnostic pamphlet and named Severian after a Gnostic cult [James Wynn, 2005].
  • Wynn called the Gnostic sect the strongest link among the candidate sources for the name [James Wynn, 2005].

Against

  • Groce proposed the bishop Severian, not the Encratite heretics, as the source, citing the Catholic Encyclopedia [Alex David Groce, 1998].
  • Friedman proposed that the name comes from one of several Catholic saints named Severian [Jerry Friedman, 2002].
  • Wynn's composite-heretic reading was offered as a response to a claim that Severian is not a Christ figure, so it was contested from the start [James Wynn, 2005].

Where it stands: Still disputed: the Gnostic and Encratite reading was argued repeatedly, mainly by Wynn, but the saint and bishop readings were never settled and the list reached no consensus.
